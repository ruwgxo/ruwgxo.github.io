Production Deployment Taxonomy: What Production Adds to the Logical Architecture

Section Overview

One Line Summary

A complete logical architecture and a production service are different artifacts: the Chapter 13 behavioural monitoring gateway specifies what runs where, and production adds the non-functional engineering -- placement, latency, failure handling, availability, observability, and cost -- that keeps it running under load, failure, and cost.

Why This Section Exists

Chapter 13 ended with the behavioural monitoring gateway: signature, heuristic, ML, and behavioural detection integrated as a four-layer stack, with detectors placed at inline, near-real-time, and batch tiers by the state-latency coupling. That is a complete logical design and an incomplete system. A logical architecture does not survive contact with production load, production failures, and production cost without engineering that the design does not contain. This section is the anchor for Chapter 14: it establishes that deployment is a distinct discipline from detection, names the deployment concerns, and maps each one to the specific way a correct detection system dies in production if that concern is left unaddressed. Every subsequent section of the chapter is then a deliberate response to one of these failure modes rather than an arbitrary operations topic.

Position In Chapter

Section 14.01 is the taxonomy anchor for all 11 subsequent sections of Chapter 14, mirroring the taxonomy anchors of 12.01 and 13.01. It is the map that makes the placement sections (14.02-14.03), the performance sections (14.04-14.05), the resilience sections (14.06-14.07), the operability sections (14.08-14.09), the cost section (14.10), and the capstone (14.11) each a response to a named concern rather than a standalone topic.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

A Design Is Not a System: The Two-Artifact Argument

Body

Chapter 13 ended with a complete architecture. The behavioural monitoring gateway names every detector, assigns each one to the inline, near-real-time, or batch tier by the state-latency coupling, and specifies what the request hot path reads. Read it as a blueprint and nothing is missing: you can see which detector fires where, what state each one needs, and how the four detection layers combine into a verdict. It is a finished design. It is also not a system. The gap between the two is not a matter of writing the design out in more detail. A logical architecture and a production service are different artifacts that answer different questions. The logical architecture answers what runs where: which detector, at which tier, reading which state. A production service answers a second set of questions that the design never touches. How much time may the inline detector add to a request before the API is too slow to ship? What happens to a request when a detector throws an exception? How does one failing detector avoid taking down the whole gateway? Can the running system be seen well enough to tune and trust it? Does it stay affordable at production volume? None of these are answered by knowing what runs where. This is the central claim of Chapter 14, and this section states it before any mechanism is built. The design is correct and the design is incomplete, and those two facts are not in tension because completeness as a design and completeness as a service are measured on different axes. The behavioural monitoring gateway is one hundred per cent complete as a design. Measured as a service it is zero per cent ready, because not one of the non-functional questions above has an answer yet. Chapter 14 is the work of moving the artifact along the second axis, from a design that is right in principle to a service that survives production in practice.

Part 2

Title

Deployment Is a Distinct Discipline from Detection

Body

The reason the second set of questions goes unanswered in most detection work is that they belong to a different discipline. Detection engineering asks whether a detector catches the attack: its precision, its recall, its coverage of the threat surface, its explainability. Deployment engineering asks whether the detector keeps running once it is placed in front of live traffic: its latency, its failure behaviour, its blast radius, its observability, its cost. These are orthogonal. A detector can be excellent at the first and fatal at the second. Consider the ways this plays out. A detector with outstanding recall that adds two hundred milliseconds to every request is disabled within a week, not because it detects poorly but because it makes the product slow. A gateway with a flawless four-layer detection stack and no failure policy admits every attack the moment one detector throws an unhandled exception, because the default behaviour of an unhandled error is a behaviour chosen by the bug rather than by the engineer. A system with strong detectors and no circuit breakers turns one slow detector into a total outage when it holds every worker thread. A system that detects perfectly but emits no metrics cannot tell its operators that its false-positive rate has quietly tripled. Each of these is a correct detection system dying in production, and in each case the cause is a deployment concern, not a detection defect. The consequence for how the chapter is organised is direct. Detection quality does not buy you out of the deployment concerns. You cannot detect your way past a latency budget or a missing failure policy. Deployment is the discipline that keeps a correct detector running, and it must be engineered deliberately and separately. That is why Chapter 14 exists as its own chapter closing Part 3 rather than as a footnote to the detection chapters that precede it.

Part 3

Title

The Six Deployment Concerns and the Failure Each Prevents

Body

The deployment concerns are not an open-ended list of operations topics. They are a small, closed set, and each one exists because it prevents a specific way the system fails in production. Naming them as failure preventers rather than as generic best practices is what makes the rest of the chapter disciplined. Placement is the concern of where detection sits in the request path. Get it wrong and the detector either cannot see the traffic it must inspect or pays for an avoidable network hop, or runs after the model call when it needed to block before inference. Latency is the concern of how much time detection may add. Get it wrong and the inline detector exceeds its budget and is removed for degrading the API. Failure handling is the concern of what happens when a detector errors. Leave it unspecified and the gateway either blocks all traffic or admits all traffic the instant a detector throws, because an unspecified failure mode is a failure mode chosen by the bug. Availability is the concern of keeping one detector failure contained. Ignore it and a single slow or failing detector cascades into a total gateway outage. Observability is the concern of whether the running system can be seen, tuned, and trusted. Skip it and the detection rate, false-positive rate, and latency are invisible, and an invisible system cannot be operated or improved. Cost is the concern of staying affordable at production volume. Neglect it and compute, inference, and storage grow unbounded until the system is switched off when the bill arrives. Six concerns, six distinct failure modes, and each subsequent section of Chapter 14 is a response to one of them.

Part 4

Title

The Chapter 13 Tiers Are the Bridge Between Detection and Deployment

Body

The deployment concerns do not land on a blank slate. Chapter 13 already partitioned the detectors into three tiers -- inline, near-real-time, and batch -- by the state-latency coupling, and that partition is the bridge between the detection work and the deployment work. The tiers were chosen for a detection reason: a detector that needs a large historical state cannot run on the hot path, so it was pushed to a slower tier. But that same partition is exactly the structure the deployment layer needs, because the deployment concerns fall on the tiers unevenly. The inline tier is where latency, failure handling, and availability dominate. It is on the request hot path, it must hold the sub-50ms P99 budget, and any error or slowness it suffers is felt by the user immediately. It is also, by design, stateless or small-state, which is what lets it scale horizontally and near-linearly. The near-real-time and batch tiers are where cost, throughput, and observability dominate. They run the expensive, large-state analytics that Chapter 13 deliberately kept off the hot path -- session scoring, drift monitoring, coordinated-network analysis, population baselining -- and those are precisely the detectors whose compute and storage cost must be controlled by sampling, caching, and batching. This is why the state-latency tiers are more than a detection convenience. They already encode the latency constraint the deployment layer must enforce and already sort the detectors by which deployment concern governs them. The chapter can therefore treat the tiers as given and ask, for each tier, which deployment concerns apply and how they are engineered. The detection design and the deployment engineering meet at the tier boundary.

Part 5

Title

Non-Functional Requirements as First-Class Constraints

Body

The deployment concerns are the operational face of a more general idea: non-functional requirements are first-class constraints, not afterthoughts. A functional requirement says the gateway must block jailbreak prompts. A non-functional requirement says it must do so within fifty milliseconds at the ninety-ninth percentile, remain available when a detector fails, stay observable to its operators, and cost less than a stated bound per million requests. The functional requirement describes what the system does. The non-functional requirements describe the envelope within which it must do it, and in production the envelope is as binding as the function. Treating non-functional requirements as first-class has a sharp consequence: an unspecified non-functional requirement is not absent, it is chosen by the incident. If you do not set a latency budget, the budget is set the day the API is declared too slow. If you do not choose fail-open or fail-closed, the choice is made by whichever code path the exception happens to take. If you do not set a cost bound, the bound is set by the finance review that switches the system off. The deployment concerns are the way this chapter forces each non-functional requirement to be chosen deliberately, in advance, by the engineer rather than by the outage. This reframing is what separates a design from a service in one sentence. A design satisfies its functional requirements. A service satisfies its functional requirements inside its non-functional envelope, under real load, real failure, and real cost. The production readiness checklist in this section is simply the enumeration of that envelope, and the rest of the chapter is the engineering that brings the gateway inside it.

Part 6

Title

The Chapter Roadmap: Placement to Performance to Resilience to Operability to Cost

Body

The concerns are addressed in a deliberate order, and the order is itself an argument. The chapter moves through five stages: placement, then performance, then resilience, then operability, then cost. Each stage assumes the one before it, so the sequence is not arbitrary. Placement comes first because you cannot engineer the latency of a detector until you have decided where it sits. Sections 14.02 and 14.03 choose the architecture pattern -- API gateway, sidecar, or inline -- and the integration points, pre-processing and post-processing, that place detection relative to the model call. Performance comes second because once placement is fixed, the latency budget and the horizontal scaling can be engineered against it: Sections 14.04 and 14.05 hold the inline tier within the sub-50ms P99 budget and scale the tiers under load. Resilience comes third because a system that performs under normal load must still survive failure: Sections 14.06 and 14.07 make the fail-open versus fail-closed decision and add circuit breakers with graceful degradation. Operability comes fourth because a system that performs and survives must still be seeable: Sections 14.08 and 14.09 build the observability stack and the production dashboard, without which the system cannot be tuned or trusted. Cost comes fifth because a system that is fast, resilient, and observable is still switched off if it is too expensive: Section 14.10 applies sampling, caching, and batching to keep the tiers affordable. And the capstone, Section 14.11, assembles all five stages into one running production gateway that meets its latency, availability, and cost targets together -- the artifact that completes Part 3 and that Part 4 builds on. This section is the map; the sections that follow are the territory.

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import List, Optional


@dataclass
class ReadinessItem:

    requirement: str
    design_has_it: bool
    section_that_delivers: str


CHECKLIST: List[ReadinessItem] = [

    ReadinessItem('Placement', 'Post-processing detection intercepts the model response', False, '14.03'),
    ReadinessItem('Latency', 'Inline tier holds sub-50ms P99 under load', False, '14.04'),
    ReadinessItem('Latency', 'Per-detector latency budget is allocated and measured', False, '14.04'),
    ReadinessItem('Throughput', 'Tiers scale horizontally with backpressure', False, '14.05'),
    ReadinessItem('Failure handling', 'Fail-open or fail-closed decided per detector', False, '14.06'),
    ReadinessItem('Availability', 'Circuit breakers cut off a failing detector', False, '14.07'),
    ReadinessItem('Availability', 'Graceful degradation sheds non-essential detectors under stress', False, '14.07'),
    ReadinessItem('Observability', 'Golden signals emitted per detector and tier', False, '14.08'),
    ReadinessItem('Observability', 'An operable dashboard with SLO-driven alerting exists', False, '14.09'),
    ReadinessItem('Cost', 'Expensive tiers use sampling, caching, or batching', False, '14.10'),
    ReadinessItem('Cost', 'Cost is attributed per detector and per tier', False, '14.10'),
]


def evaluate(checklist: List[ReadinessItem]) -> None:
    print('=' * 78)
    print('PRODUCTION READINESS CHECKLIST')
    print('Starting state: the Chapter 13 logical architecture (design only)')
    print('=' * 78)
    by_concern = {}
    for item in checklist:
        by_concern.setdefault(item.concern, []).append(item)
    for concern, items in by_concern.items():
        print()
        print(f'[{concern}]')
        for it in items:
            mark = 'PASS' if it.design_has_it else 'GAP '
            print(f'  [{mark}] {it.requirement}')
            if not it.design_has_it:
                print(f'         -> delivered in Section {it.section_that_delivers}')


def readiness_score(checklist: List[ReadinessItem]) -> float:
    met = sum(1 for i in checklist if i.design_has_it)
    total = len(checklist)
    return 100.0 * met / total if total else 0.0


def summarise(checklist: List[ReadinessItem]) -> None:
    score = readiness_score(checklist)
    gaps = sum(1 for i in checklist if not i.design_has_it)
    print()
    print('=' * 78)
    print('READINESS SUMMARY')
    print('=' * 78)
    print(f'Production readiness of the logical architecture: {score:.0f}%')
    print(f'Open gaps to close in Chapter 14: {gaps}')
    print('The logical architecture is 100% complete as a DESIGN and 0% ready')
    print('as a SERVICE -- these are different artifacts, and Chapter 14 closes the gap.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    evaluate(CHECKLIST)
    summarise(CHECKLIST)

        for concern in t.dominant_concerns:
            coverage.setdefault(concern, []).append(t.name)
    return coverage


def print_coverage(tiers: List[DetectionTier]) -> None:
    coverage = concern_coverage(tiers)
    print()
    print('=' * 78)
    print('WHERE EACH CONCERN DOMINATES')
    print('=' * 78)
    for concern in sorted(coverage):
        joined = ', '.join(coverage[concern])
        print(f'  {concern:<18}-> {joined}')
    print()
    print('Latency dominates the inline tier because it is on the hot path.')
    print('Cost dominates the near-real-time and batch tiers because they run the')
    print('expensive, large-state detection Chapter 13 deliberately kept off the hot path.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    print_tier_mapping(TIERS)
    print_coverage(TIERS)

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Treating the completed Chapter 13 logical architecture as production-ready and moving straight to deployment without engineering the non-functional concerns

Why It Happens

The logical architecture looks finished. Every detector is named, every tier is assigned, and the request flow is fully specified, so it reads as a complete system rather than a complete design. The distinction between the two artifacts is not visible in the diagram.

Consequence

The gateway ships without a latency budget, a failure policy, circuit breakers, observability, or cost control, and dies in production the first time it meets real load, a detector error, or a cost review.

Correct Approach

Treat the logical architecture as 100% complete as a design and 0% ready as a service, and run the production readiness checklist to enumerate the deployment concerns that must be engineered before the gateway is a service.

Mistake 2

Description

Assuming that a high-quality detection stack compensates for weak deployment engineering

Why It Happens

Detection quality is where the intellectual effort of the previous chapters went, so it feels like the hard part is done. Precision, recall, and coverage are concrete and measurable, while the deployment concerns feel like plumbing.

Consequence

An excellent detector that adds too much latency, has no failure policy, or has no observability is disabled, bypassed, or distrusted in production regardless of how well it detects, and the detection quality is wasted.

Correct Approach

Recognise that deployment is a distinct discipline orthogonal to detection, and that you cannot detect your way past a latency budget, a missing failure policy, or an absence of observability. Engineer the deployment concerns deliberately and separately.

Mistake 3

Description

Leaving non-functional requirements -- latency budget, failure mode, cost bound -- unspecified and letting production set them

Why It Happens

Non-functional requirements are easy to defer because the system appears to work without them in a test environment with light load, no failures, and no cost visibility. The requirement seems optional until production proves otherwise.

Consequence

The latency budget is set the day the API is declared too slow, the failure mode is set by whichever code path the exception takes, and the cost bound is set by the finance review that switches the system off -- each requirement chosen by the incident rather than the engineer.

Correct Approach

Treat every non-functional requirement as first-class and choose it deliberately in advance. Set the sub-50ms P99 budget, the per-detector fail-open or fail-closed policy, and the cost bound before deployment, not after the outage that would otherwise set them.

Mistake 4

Description

Applying deployment concerns uniformly across all detectors instead of by tier

Why It Happens

A single uniform policy is simpler to reason about and implement than a per-tier one, and the state-latency tiers of Chapter 13 are easy to treat as an internal detail of detection rather than as the structure the deployment layer should follow.

Consequence

The inline tier is burdened with cost optimisation it does not need while the batch tier is held to a latency budget it cannot meet, and the deployment engineering fights the tier structure instead of using it.

Correct Approach

Use the Chapter 13 tiers as the bridge: apply latency, failure handling, and availability engineering to the inline tier, and cost, throughput, and observability engineering to the near-real-time and batch tiers, because the state-latency partition already sorts detectors by dominant concern.

Mistake 5

Description

Reading Chapter 14 as a grab-bag of operations topics rather than a deliberate response to named failure modes

Why It Happens

Latency, scaling, circuit breakers, observability, and cost are each a large field with its own literature, so the sections can look like independent operations tutorials that happen to be collected in one chapter.

Consequence

Sections are studied or skipped in isolation, the connection between each mechanism and the specific production failure it prevents is lost, and engineering effort is spent on mechanisms that do not map to a real failure mode for the system at hand.

Correct Approach

Read every Chapter 14 section as a response to one of the six deployment concerns and the failure it prevents, following the placement-to-performance-to-resilience-to-operability-to-cost progression that this taxonomy section establishes.

Section Summary

Compact

A complete logical architecture and a production service are different artifacts. The Chapter 13 behavioural monitoring gateway is 100% complete as a design and 0% ready as a service, because the design answers what runs where while a service must also answer the non-functional questions of placement, latency, failure handling, availability, observability, and cost. These six deployment concerns are a closed set, each preventing a specific way a correct detection system dies in production: detection that runs too late to block, a detector that is too slow to keep, an unhandled error that fails open, a hung detector that cascades into an outage, a silent false-positive rise that goes unseen, and unbounded cost that switches the system off. Deployment is a distinct discipline from detection, and detection quality does not buy you out of it. The Chapter 13 state-latency tiers are the bridge: they already sort detectors by which deployment concern governs them, with latency, failure handling, and availability dominating the inline tier and cost, throughput, and observability dominating the near-real-time and batch tiers. Chapter 14 addresses the concerns in a deliberate order -- placement, performance, resilience, operability, cost -- and the capstone assembles all five stages into one running gateway that meets its latency, availability, and cost targets together.

Key Takeaways

  • A logical architecture and a production service are different artifacts measured on different axes: the Chapter 13 gateway is complete as a design and not yet ready as a service
  • The deployment concerns are a closed set of six -- placement, latency, failure handling, availability, observability, and cost -- and each prevents a specific way a correct detection system dies in production
  • Deployment is a distinct discipline from detection, and detection quality does not compensate for unaddressed deployment concerns
  • An unspecified non-functional requirement is not absent but chosen by the incident, so latency budgets, failure policies, and cost bounds must be set deliberately in advance
  • The Chapter 13 state-latency tiers are the bridge between detection and deployment and already sort detectors by which deployment concern dominates each tier
  • Chapter 14 addresses the concerns in the deliberate order placement, performance, resilience, operability, cost, and the capstone assembles them into one running production service
