Chapter Summary and the Path to Monitoring

Section Overview

One Line Summary

The chapter closes by drawing its parts into one picture: a detection flows from the Chapter 15 suite, through the shared detection-event schema and the enrichment that fills it, out through Sigma rules and the platform connectors to the SIEM -- and the section consolidates that architecture, a per-platform reference card, an integration checklist, the chapter's mistakes, and a knowledge assessment, then hands off to Chapter 17's monitoring and tuning.

Why This Section Exists

Chapter 16 built the last mile of the detector suite one piece at a time -- schema, Sigma generator, three connectors, webhook, enrichment. This section is where the pieces become a single mental model. Read in isolation, each section solves a local problem; read together, they are one pipeline with one contract at its centre, and seeing that whole is what lets an engineer reason about the integration rather than recall six separate builds. The summary states the architecture as a single flow, gives a reference card for choosing and configuring a platform, and provides a checklist that turns 'integrate the suite with our SIEM' into concrete, ordered steps. It also sets up the handoff that Part 4 has been building toward. Delivering detections to the SIEM is not the end of the story: it is the beginning of the feedback loop. Once detections flow in as actionable alerts, a team can see which detectors fire, which alerts turn out to be false positives, and where the suite is blind -- and that visibility is the raw material for tuning. Chapter 17 is that loop. This section's final job is to frame the transition: the integration Chapter 16 built is what makes the monitoring and tuning of Chapter 17 possible, because you cannot tune what you cannot see.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

The Whole Surface as One Flow

Body

Step back from the six builds and one pipeline appears. A request reaches the Chapter 15 detector suite, which produces a DetectorResult. A single function, build_event, turns that result and its request context into a DetectionEvent -- the platform-neutral, serialisable contract at the centre of everything. The enrichment pipeline fills that event's open slots with the Chapter 13 behavioural context, the derived IOCs, and the response actions, so the event is now an actionable alert while keeping the exact same shape. And then the event fans out: the Sigma rules express the detection logic portably for any platform to evaluate, and the connectors -- Splunk over HEC, Datadog over the Logs API and metrics, Elastic through Logstash, or the custom webhook for anything else -- each map that one event to their platform and deliver it. The shape of the flow is the lesson. There is one contract, built in one place, enriched in one place, and consumed by every delivery path. That is why five different platforms stay consistent: none of them sees a bespoke payload, all of them translate from the same schema, and a change to what a detection carries -- a new field, a revised severity rule, a new enrichment -- happens once and reaches all of them. The chapter's architecture is not five integrations; it is one detection event with five ways out.

Part 2

Title

Choosing and Configuring a Platform

Body

The reference card that follows turns the flow into a decision. If a team is on Splunk, the path is HEC delivery plus the add-on plus the Sigma rules as correlation searches. On Datadog, it is the Logs API for detail and the metrics channel for rates that sit beside the Chapter 14 observability signals. On Elastic, it is the Logstash transform, the explicit keyword mapping, daily indices under a lifecycle policy, and the Sigma rules as detection rules. On anything else, it is the signed, versioned webhook. And across all of them, enrichment runs before delivery, so whichever path a team takes, the alert arrives with its context and response actions attached. The common structure under the four connectors is worth naming because it is what makes the suite maintainable. Every connector is a schema-to-platform mapping behind a swappable transport, configured by data with its secret read from the environment, and tested end to end on a stub before it ever touches the real platform. That uniformity means adding a sixth platform later is a known exercise -- write the mapping, stub the transport, test, wire the secret -- not a fresh invention. The reference card captures the per-platform specifics; the shared shape is what lets them all be reasoned about the same way.

Part 3

Title

The Feedback Loop: From Delivery to Tuning

Body

Delivering detections to the SIEM completes Chapter 16, but its real significance is what it starts. Until the detections reach a place a team watches, the suite runs blind: it makes decisions no one can observe in aggregate, and there is no way to know whether a detector is too aggressive, too lax, or silent when it should be firing. Integration ends that blindness. With detections flowing in as enriched, actionable alerts -- tagged by detector, verdict, and severity, carrying entity and session context -- a team can finally see the suite's behaviour: which detectors fire and how often, which alerts get dismissed as false positives, where incidents slipped through with no alert at all. That visibility is precisely the raw material for tuning, and tuning is Chapter 17. The metrics channel feeding Datadog, the indexed detections in Elastic, the correlation searches in Splunk -- these are not just where alerts live; they are the measurement surface the next chapter reads to decide what to adjust. A detector whose flags are almost always dismissed needs a higher threshold; a gap where incidents recur with no detection needs a new rule; a severity that consistently over- or under-states urgency needs remapping. None of those decisions is possible without the integration this chapter built. Chapter 16 is the sense organ; Chapter 17 is the learning it enables.

Part 4

Title

What the Chapter Established

Body

Four commitments carried through every section. First, one detection-event schema: the entire surface consumes a single contract, built by one function, so a detection is expressed once and delivered everywhere consistently -- the single most important structural decision in the chapter. Second, portability before lock-in: Sigma gives detection logic that runs on any platform, generated from the detectors so it never drifts from them. Third, delivery plus enrichment equals actionability: a connector that ships a raw detection ships a fact, and only the enrichment that adds context, IOCs, and response actions makes it an alert a responder can act on. Fourth, automation over memory: generated-and-hashed rules and stubbed-and-tested connectors keep the SIEM in sync with the detectors by construction rather than by someone remembering to update it. Those commitments are why the chapter is a system and not a collection of exporters. Each connector was simple because the schema did the hard work of being the shared contract; enrichment composed with every path because it preserved that schema; deployment stayed current because generation made drift detectable. The knowledge assessment that follows checks these ideas -- the schema as contract, the push/pull and platform distinctions, the verdict-to-severity and verdict-to-Sigma mappings, the reason enrichment must preserve the schema, and the role integration plays in enabling monitoring. Mastery of the chapter is holding the whole flow at once: request to result to event to enriched alert to platform.

Part 5

Title

Transition to Chapter 17: Monitoring and Tuning

Body

Part 4 has moved from building detectors (Chapter 15) to delivering their detections to security operations (Chapter 16). Chapter 17 closes the loop by turning the delivered detections back on the detectors themselves. Monitoring is watching the suite through the SIEM it now feeds -- detection rates, false-positive rates, coverage gaps, latency trends -- and tuning is acting on what monitoring reveals: adjusting thresholds, adding and retiring rules, remapping severities, and measuring whether each change improved the suite. It is the maintenance discipline that keeps a detection system effective as the threats and the traffic it sees keep changing. The handoff is direct. Every measurement Chapter 17 relies on is a detection this chapter delivered: the aggregate rates come from the Datadog metrics channel, the searchable detection history from Elastic and Splunk, the enriched context that tells a true positive from a false one from the enrichment pipeline. Chapter 16 built the visibility; Chapter 17 uses it to close the feedback loop, so the suite does not merely run but improves. With detections now reaching security operations as actionable alerts, the next chapter turns to keeping the detectors that produce them sharp.

Assessment

Title

Chapter 16 Knowledge Assessment

Description

Twenty questions covering the integration surface: the detection-event schema as contract, the push/pull model, the Sigma generator and its mappings, the three platform connectors, the custom webhook, and the enrichment pipeline. Answers follow the questions.

Questions

  • Q1. Why is a detection that is computed but never delivered to the SIEM considered not actioned?
  • Q2. What single object does every part of the integration surface consume, and what function produces it?
  • Q3. What does the detection-event schema add to a Chapter 15 DetectorResult, and why?
  • Q4. Why is severity derived once in the schema rather than in each connector?
  • Q5. Distinguish a push integration from a pull integration, and classify each Chapter 16 delivery path.
  • Q6. What makes Sigma portable, and why generate the rules rather than hand-author them?
  • Q7. Name the three governing keys of a Sigma rule and what each controls.
  • Q8. How does a detector verdict map to a Sigma condition, and why does allow stay quiet?
  • Q9. How does a content hash over the rule set keep the SIEM in sync with the detectors?
  • Q10. In the Splunk connector, which fields are promoted to indexed fields, and what stays in the event body, and why?
  • Q11. Why must the ISO timestamp be converted before sending to Splunk HEC?
  • Q12. What does a Splunk add-on package, and why ship the integration as one?
  • Q13. Why does the Datadog connector use two channels, and what does each carry?
  • Q14. How do detection events aggregate into Datadog metric series, and why aggregate at the connector?
  • Q15. How do the Datadog detection metrics connect to the Chapter 14 observability work?
  • Q16. In the Elastic connector, why is an explicit keyword mapping necessary for the facet fields?
  • Q17. Why do detections write to daily indices under a lifecycle policy?
  • Q18. Why does the custom webhook require an HMAC signature that the dedicated connectors did not?
  • Q19. What does the enrichment pipeline add to a raw detection, and from where?
  • Q20. Why must enrichment write only into the schema's existing slots?

Answers

  • A1. Because operational value comes from a responder acting on it; inside the gateway it changes nothing -- a tree falling in an empty forest.
  • A2. The DetectionEvent, produced by the single build_event function from a DetectorResult and its request context.
  • A3. A timestamp, a derived severity, the request context (request id, session, surface, source), and iocs/labels slots -- everything a SIEM needs that the detector-facing result omits.
  • A4. So every platform sorts detections identically; deriving it per connector lets the same detection read critical on one platform and medium on another.
  • A5. Push: the gateway sends events and owns delivery (Splunk, Datadog, Elastic, custom webhook). Pull: the platform evaluates rules against events it holds (Sigma-based).
  • A6. Sigma is neutral logic each platform compiles to its own query language; generating from the detectors keeps the rules in sync rather than drifting as a hand-authored snapshot would.
  • A7. logsource (the event stream the rule runs on), detection (the field selection that matches), condition (how selections combine).
  • A8. The verdict becomes the selection value; flag and block alert (medium and high), and allow stays quiet because a match per allowed request would bury the real alerts.
  • A9. Regenerating after a detector change alters the hash; a differing hash signals the rule set changed and, with auto-redeploy, triggers deployment -- sync by construction.
  • A10. detector, verdict, and severity are promoted for fast filtering and alerting; the full detection stays in the body because an investigation needs the scores, evidence, session, and surface.
  • A11. HEC expects an epoch time; sending the ISO string mis-parses the event time and places detections at the wrong point on the Splunk timeline.
  • A12. The index, sourcetype, field extractions, and saved searches/dashboards -- so POSTed JSON becomes a structured, queryable data source rather than opaque text.
  • A13. A detection is both an event and a data point in a rate: the Logs API carries per-detection detail; the metrics interface carries aggregate counts by dimension.
  • A14. Events are counted by (detector, verdict, severity) into one series per combination; aggregating at the connector gives exact rates cheaply instead of counting raw logs.
  • A15. They are submitted under a namespace alongside the Chapter 14 golden-signal metrics, so operational and detection signals share dashboards and can be correlated.
  • A16. Without it Elasticsearch guesses string facets as analysed text, which cannot be aggregated cleanly, so Kibana breakdowns by detector or verdict return wrong or empty buckets.
  • A17. Daily indices make time-scoped queries efficient and let old data be dropped whole; the ILM policy bounds the footprint automatically so indices do not fill the cluster.
  • A18. A generic endpoint has no platform token machinery, so the contract must carry its own trust; the HMAC lets the receiver verify authenticity and integrity and reject forgeries.
  • A19. The Chapter 13 Section 13.11 entity/session/graph context and known-bad indicators (into labels and iocs) plus verdict-and-context-appropriate response actions.
  • A20. So the enriched event keeps the DetectionEvent shape and every connector delivers it unchanged; new top-level fields would break the schema mapping and stop enrichment composing with delivery.

Section Summary

Compact

Section 16.07 synthesises Chapter 16 into one flow and hands off to Chapter 17. The whole surface is a single pipeline: a request reaches the Chapter 15 detector suite, build_event turns the result into the platform-neutral DetectionEvent that is the chapter's central contract, the enrichment pipeline fills that event's open slots with the Chapter 13 behavioural context, IOCs, and response actions while preserving its shape, and the event then fans out -- as portable Sigma rules and through the Splunk, Datadog, Elastic, and custom-webhook connectors that each map the one event to their platform. The lesson of the flow is that there is one contract, built and enriched in one place and consumed by every delivery path, which is why five platforms stay consistent and why a change to a detection reaches all of them at once. The section provides a reference card for choosing and configuring each platform, a checklist that turns integration into ordered steps, and a consolidation of the chapter's mistakes against the principle each protects, plus a twenty-question assessment. Its final work is the handoff: delivering detections to the SIEM ends the suite's blindness and creates the measurement surface -- Datadog rates, indexed Elastic history, Splunk correlation searches, enriched context -- that Chapter 17's monitoring and tuning reads to adjust thresholds, add and retire rules, and remap severities. Chapter 16 built the sense organ; Chapter 17 is the learning it enables, because you cannot tune what you cannot see.
