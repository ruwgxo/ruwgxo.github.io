Section Overview

One Line Summary

The five-level detection maturity model gives the detection programme a measurable position, a vocabulary for improvement conversations, and a 90-day roadmap to the L3 minimum bar that every production AI deployment requires.

Why This Section Exists

Sections 11.02 through 11.07 built the DaC pipeline, the layered detection model, the ATT&CK coverage system, and the Sigma rule portfolio. Each section improved one dimension of the programme. But without a unified model that scores all dimensions together, there is no single answer to the question every CISO eventually asks: "How good is our detection programme, and what would it take to make it better?" The detection maturity model answers that question with a five-level scale measured across five dimensions. It makes the programme''s current state concrete enough to act on — not "we have some rules and a CI pipeline" but "we are at L2.3, with a clear roadmap to L3 in 90 days and an investment estimate for L4 by end of year." The model also establishes the minimum bar: L3 is the lowest acceptable maturity for a production AI system facing external adversaries. Anything below L3 in production is a documented, accepted risk. The 90-day roadmap is practical rather than theoretical. It targets L3 specifically because L3 is achievable by a team of two to three engineers without organisational change, budget, or new tooling beyond what Chapters 11-13 provide. L4 and L5 require additional investment — ML detection infrastructure, a dedicated detection engineering team, and a red team programme — and are scoped appropriately for the programme size they require.

Position In Chapter

Prose Content

Part 1 Maturity Is Programme Property

Heading

Maturity Is a Programme Property, Not a Rule Property

Body

The most important framing principle of the detection maturity model: maturity describes the detection programme as a whole, not individual rules within it. A programme with one perfectly written, fully tested, coverage-mapped, FPR-tracked Sigma rule is not an L4 programme. It is an L1 programme with one good rule. Maturity requires every dimension of the programme to reach the level, not just the best-performing rule or the most recently built component. This principle has practical consequences. It prevents organisations from declaring a high maturity level because they have built an impressive detection for a single attack family while leaving the rest of the programme ungoverned. It forces a systematic assessment across five dimensions simultaneously: Dimension 1 — Rule quality: Are rules tested, versioned, and metadata-complete? Dimension 2 — ATT&CK coverage: What fraction of relevant ATLAS techniques have at least partial detection coverage? Dimension 3 — FPR management: Is the false positive rate tracked per rule, and is there an active process to keep it within targets? Dimension 4 — Deployment pipeline: Do rules deploy through CI/CD with rollback capability, or are they deployed manually? Dimension 5 — Proactive hunting: Does the programme actively search for novel attack evidence not yet covered by deployed rules? A programme at L3 overall must be at L3 on all five dimensions. A programme that is L4 on rule quality, L4 on pipeline, L4 on coverage, but L1 on FPR management and L1 on hunting is an L1 programme that has done excellent work in two dimensions. The weakest dimension sets the floor.

Part 2 L1 Adhoc

Heading

L1 — Ad-Hoc Detection

Body

L1 is the starting state. It is where the seven-track gateway sits at the end of Part 2. It is also where most detection programmes in AI-deploying organisations sit today, because detection engineering for AI is a new discipline and most organisations have not yet invested in the governance infrastructure that L2 and beyond require. L1 characteristics per dimension: Rule quality: Rules exist — there are Sigma files and Python detectors — but they have no automated tests, no version history beyond the working directory, and no metadata standard enforced at review time. A rule that stops working is discovered when it stops producing alerts, not when a test fails. ATT&CK coverage: Coverage is unknown. No coverage heat map has been generated. The team knows roughly which attack families the rules target but cannot quantify what fraction of relevant ATLAS techniques have any detection, or which have confirmed evasion variants with no compensating control. FPR management: Alert volume is tracked in aggregate — the SIEM dashboard shows total alerts per day — but FPR is not tracked per rule. When a rule produces excessive false positives, the analyst team notices through alert fatigue before the detection engineering team notices through metrics. The fix is typically to raise the threshold directly in the SIEM without a commit. Deployment pipeline: Rules are deployed by copying files to the SIEM or editing rules directly in the SIEM console. There is no CI stage, no deployment audit trail, and no rollback mechanism faster than a SIEM console edit that takes 30–60 minutes and is not logged. Proactive hunting: Detection is entirely reactive. Rules are created after an incident or after a threat intelligence report surfaces a new attack. There is no systematic programme to search for novel attack evidence using analyst-driven hypothesis testing. The L1 gateway is not a failure. All detection programmes start at L1. The Part 2 work has produced seven functioning tracks that block real attacks. L1 is a starting point with five identified improvement paths, not a broken system.

Part 3 L2 Defined

Heading

L2 — Defined Detection

Body

L2 is reached when the programme has formalised its practices: rules are in version control, a CI/CD pipeline exists, coverage is mapped at least quarterly, and FPR data is collected even if not yet systematically acted upon. L2 is the direct output of Section 11.02 (DaC pipeline) combined with the Section 11.05 coverage measurement. After completing the gateway migration described in Section 11.02, the programme is at L2. L2 characteristics per dimension: Rule quality: All rules are in Git with commit history. All rules have the eight required metadata fields. Tests exist for the majority of rules — the migration from Section 11.02 included test authoring. The CI pipeline enforces lint and test before deployment. ATT&CK coverage: A coverage heat map has been generated at least once. The map identifies partial and full coverage per technique. A gap backlog exists, ordered by priority score from Section 11.05. The heat map is regenerated quarterly. FPR management: Per-rule FPR data is being collected — each alert is tagged with a rule ID and analyst dispositions (true positive / false positive) are recorded. No FPR targets have been set and no automated alerting fires when FPR crosses a threshold. The data collection is the precondition for L3 FPR management. Deployment pipeline: Rules deploy through the CI/CD pipeline from Section 11.02. The rollback manifest is populated. Alert flood detection is configured. Manual deployment to the SIEM is prohibited by team policy, though not physically enforced by the pipeline (the SIEM still allows direct edit by credentialed users). Proactive hunting: Not yet systematic. The gap backlog from Section 11.05 drives new rule development reactively against known technique gaps. Analyst-driven hypothesis testing happens occasionally but without a formal procedure or cadence. The gap between L2 and L3 is the gap between "we have the data" and "we act on the data." L2 collects FPR data but does not enforce FPR targets. L2 has a coverage map but does not automatically detect regression. L2 has a CI pipeline but does not physically prevent manual SIEM edits. L3 closes all three of these gaps.

Part 4 L3 Managed

Heading

L3 — Managed Detection

Body

L3 is the minimum acceptable maturity for a production AI system facing external adversaries. Below L3, the detection programme lacks the quality controls needed to maintain its effectiveness over time: rules degrade without being caught, false positives accumulate without being addressed, and coverage gaps widen as new techniques emerge without anyone noticing. L3 is achievable by a team of two to three engineers in 90 days using only the tooling built in Chapters 11 through 13 of this book. No additional budget, headcount, or specialised infrastructure is required beyond what a standard SIEM and a Git-based CI/CD system provide. The 90-day roadmap in this section''s deliverable operationalises the path. L3 characteristics per dimension: Rule quality: Every rule in production has automated tests that run in CI on every PR. Regression tests are added for every confirmed false positive. The test suite covers all seven gateway tracks. Rule quality is enforced by the pipeline, not by team discipline: a rule without tests does not merge. ATT&CK coverage: The coverage heat map is regenerated weekly in CI diff mode. Coverage regression triggers a CI failure with the rule ID and commit hash of the change that caused it. New ATLAS techniques are detected quarterly via the taxonomy expansion check. The gap backlog is actively worked in sprint planning. FPR management: Per-rule FPR targets are set and documented in each rule''s metadata. The false_positive_manager.py from Section 11.09 monitors per-rule FPR in real time and opens a detection engineering queue ticket when any rule exceeds its target. FPR is reviewed in the weekly detection engineering sync. The SOC queue is not the FPR feedback mechanism — the DaC queue is. Deployment pipeline: Manual SIEM edits are physically impossible for production rules, enforced by SIEM RBAC: only the CI service account has write access to production rules. All changes flow through the repository. Rollback completes in under five minutes from flood detection. Proactive hunting: Structured threat hunting runs at least once per quarter using the formal hypothesis procedure from Section 11.12. Hunt findings feed the detection backlog: each confirmed hunting finding that does not have a rule becomes a P1 gap item, bypassing normal priority scoring.

Part 5 L4 Measured

Heading

L4 — Measured Detection

Body

L4 extends L3 with ML-based detection, quantitative coverage targets, and a formal red team programme. It requires additional investment: at minimum one dedicated detection engineer (separate from the security operations role), ML infrastructure capable of training and serving detection classifiers, and a red team function — internal or contracted — that exercises the detection programme on a regular cadence. L4 characteristics per dimension: Rule quality: ML-based detectors (Chapter 12) are integrated alongside signature and heuristic rules. Each ML detector has a precision-recall curve from its validation set, a production FPR target calibrated from the ROC curve, and automated retraining triggers when the production FPR drifts above target by more than 20% relative. Rule quality includes both Sigma rules and ML model artefacts. ATT&CK coverage: Quantitative coverage targets are set per ATLAS tactic category: for example, "all Adversarial ML techniques with likelihood ≥ 3 must reach score 2 within two quarters." Coverage is reported to leadership monthly as a metric alongside other security KPIs. Techniques at score 0 with likelihood ≥ 4 trigger an escalation to the security architecture team for compensating control assessment. FPR management: Real-time FPR dashboards are available to both the detection engineering team and the SOC. Analyst disposition data feeds back into ML detector retraining pipelines automatically. The feedback loop from analyst disposition to rule improvement runs on a weekly cadence. Deployment pipeline: The pipeline includes a canary deployment stage: new rules deploy to a canary traffic slice (5–10% of production requests) before full deployment. Alert rate on canary is compared to baseline before the full rollout is approved. This catches calibration issues before they affect the full production alert queue. Proactive hunting: Monthly threat hunting exercises, driven by red team findings and external threat intelligence. Each hunt produces at minimum one new detection hypothesis. Red team exercises run quarterly; every successful red team finding that was not detected becomes a P0 detection gap regardless of its priority score.

Part 6 L5 Optimised

Heading

L5 — Optimised Detection

Body

L5 is continuous improvement institutionalised. The programme is never complete; new attack techniques always create new gaps; the goal is not to close all gaps but to ensure that the rate of gap closure consistently exceeds the rate of gap creation. L5 requires organisational commitment beyond what a single team can provide: executive sponsorship, a dedicated detection engineering function separate from security operations, and integration of detection programme metrics into the organisation''s risk reporting. L5 characteristics per dimension: Rule quality: Automated gap detection — the programme generates a coverage report after every threat intelligence intake and automatically proposes detection hypotheses for uncovered techniques based on ATLAS technique descriptions and the existing rule portfolio. Rule authoring uses templates and ML-assisted completion; human review focuses on validation rather than authoring. ATT&CK coverage: Coverage targets are reviewed and revised quarterly based on the evolving threat landscape. Coverage improvement rate is a reported metric: the programme measures how many technique scores improved in the last 90 days and benchmarks against a target rate. FPR management: Analyst disposition data continuously retrains FPR models. The programme maintains a system-level FPR budget: the total false positive volume across all rules is capped at a level that keeps SOC analyst workload within capacity. When a new rule is proposed, its expected FPR contribution is assessed against the remaining FPR budget before the rule is approved for production. Deployment pipeline: Zero-touch deployment for routine threshold calibrations within a defined safe range. Human review required only for logic changes, new rules, and threshold changes outside the safe range. The pipeline generates a natural-language change summary for every deployment that is readable by non-engineers on the security team. Proactive hunting: Continuous hunting — the programme operates a persistent hunting function that runs automated hypotheses against production log data in near-real-time. Red team exercises run monthly. External threat intelligence is ingested automatically and mapped to ATLAS techniques within 24 hours of publication.

Part 7 Gateway Score

Heading

Scoring the Seven-Track Gateway: Current Maturity Baseline

Body

Applying the five-dimension scoring model to the seven-track gateway at the end of Section 11.07 — after completing all DaC pipeline work, coverage measurement, and Sigma rule portfolio work from Sections 11.02 through 11.07: Dimension 1 — Rule quality: L2. All seven tracks are in version control (Section 11.02 migration). All rules have the eight required metadata fields. The test suite exists and runs in CI. Not yet L3 because regression tests from confirmed false positives are not yet systematically added — that requires the FPR tracking tooling from Section 11.09. Dimension 2 — ATT&CK coverage: L3. The heat map is generated from deployed rules (Section 11.05). Coverage diff runs in CI (Section 11.05 attck_coverage_heatmap.py). Taxonomy expansion check runs quarterly. The gap backlog is actively worked. L3 is reached here because all three L3 coverage requirements are in place. Dimension 3 — FPR management: L1. No per-rule FPR targets. No automated FPR alerting. Disposition data is not being collected systematically. This dimension will move to L3 after Section 11.09. Dimension 4 — Deployment pipeline: L2. Rules deploy through CI/CD (Section 11.02). Rollback manifest is populated. Alert flood detection is configured. Not yet L3 because SIEM RBAC preventing manual edits requires infrastructure configuration outside the pipeline code itself — flagged as a parallel infrastructure task. Dimension 5 — Proactive hunting: L1. No formal hunting procedure exists yet. Section 11.12 builds the hunting playbook. Composite score: the floor across all five dimensions is L1 (FPR management and hunting). Overall programme maturity: L1 with three dimensions at L2 and one at L3. This is an accurate and honest baseline, not a discouraging one: five sections of Chapter 11 remain, each advancing at least one dimension. The end-of-chapter score in Section 11.14 will show the full progression. Minimum viable path to L3: advance FPR management to L3 (Section 11.09), confirm pipeline to L3 (Section 11.02 RBAC item), and establish the hunting programme at L3 (Section 11.12). All other dimensions are already at L3 or above.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
'''detection_maturity_assessor.py
L1-L5 detection programme maturity assessment across five dimensions.
Scores the seven-track gateway and produces a gap report with remediation map.
Chapter 11, Section 11.08 — AI Security Mastery
Author: Raghav Dinesh | github: ruwgxo

Usage:
  python3 detection_maturity_assessor.py
  python3 detection_maturity_assessor.py --state state.json
  python3 detection_maturity_assessor.py --target 3
'''

from __future__ import annotations
import argparse
import json
import pathlib
import sys
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from enum import IntEnum

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Maturity level enum
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

class MaturityLevel(IntEnum):
    L1 = 1
    L2 = 2
    L3 = 3
    L4 = 4
    L5 = 5

LEVEL_NAMES = {
    MaturityLevel.L1: 'L1 — Ad-Hoc',
    MaturityLevel.L2: 'L2 — Defined',
    MaturityLevel.L3: 'L3 — Managed',
    MaturityLevel.L4: 'L4 — Measured',
    MaturityLevel.L5: 'L5 — Optimised',
}

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Programme state model
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

@dataclass
class ProgrammeState:
    '''Observable properties of the detection programme. All fields default to
    the post-Part-2 / pre-Chapter-11 state (L1 baseline).'''

    # Rule quality dimension
    rules_in_version_control: bool = False
    rules_have_metadata: bool = False
    rules_have_tests: bool = False
    tests_run_in_ci: bool = False
    regression_tests_from_fp: bool = False      # confirmed FPs → regression fixtures
    ml_detectors_with_pr_curves: bool = False   # L4: ML detectors have PR curves

    # ATT&CK coverage dimension
    heatmap_generated: bool = False
    heatmap_in_ci_diff_mode: bool = False
    taxonomy_expansion_check: bool = False      # quarterly new technique check
    quantitative_coverage_targets: bool = False  # L4: per-tactic targets set
    coverage_improvement_rate_tracked: bool = False  # L5

    # FPR management dimension
    per_rule_fpr_data_collected: bool = False
    per_rule_fpr_targets_set: bool = False
    fpr_automated_alerting: bool = False        # DaC queue opens on breach
    realtime_fpr_dashboard: bool = False        # L4
    analyst_disposition_feeds_retraining: bool = False  # L4

    # Deployment pipeline dimension
    rules_deploy_via_cicd: bool = False
    rollback_manifest_populated: bool = False
    manual_siem_edit_blocked_by_rbac: bool = False  # L3 physical enforcement
    canary_deployment_stage: bool = False       # L4
    zero_touch_safe_range_calibration: bool = False  # L5

    # Proactive hunting dimension
    formal_hunt_procedure_exists: bool = False
    hunting_quarterly_cadence: bool = False
    hunt_findings_feed_backlog: bool = False
    red_team_quarterly: bool = False            # L4
    automated_continuous_hunting: bool = False  # L5

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Scoring functions — one per dimension
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

@dataclass
class DimensionScore:
    dimension: str
    level: MaturityLevel
    evidence: list[str]
    gaps: list[str]
    remediation: list[str]

def score_rule_quality(s: ProgrammeState) -> DimensionScore:
    evidence: list[str] = []
    gaps: list[str] = []
    remediation: list[str] = []

    if s.rules_in_version_control:
        evidence.append('Rules in version control (Git)')
    else:
        gaps.append('Rules not in version control')
        remediation.append('Section 11.02: DaC migration — git init and commit all seven tracks')

    if s.rules_have_metadata:
        evidence.append('Eight required metadata fields enforced')
    else:
        gaps.append('Rule metadata fields not enforced')
        remediation.append('Section 11.02: rule_metadata_validator.py in CI lint stage')

    if s.rules_have_tests:
        evidence.append('Positive and negative tests for rules')
    else:
        gaps.append('No automated rule tests')
        remediation.append('Section 11.02: test_tracks.py with known-bad and known-good fixtures')

    if s.tests_run_in_ci:
        evidence.append('Tests run in CI on every PR')
    else:
        gaps.append('Tests not integrated in CI')
        remediation.append('Section 11.02: stage_test() in detection_as_code_pipeline.py')

    if s.regression_tests_from_fp:
        evidence.append('Confirmed FPs added as regression test cases')
    else:
        gaps.append('No regression tests from confirmed false positives')
        remediation.append('Section 11.09: false_positive_manager.py feeds regression fixture generator')

    if s.ml_detectors_with_pr_curves:
        evidence.append('ML detectors have precision-recall curves documented')

    # Level determination
    if not s.rules_in_version_control:
        level = MaturityLevel.L1
    elif not s.rules_have_tests or not s.tests_run_in_ci:
        level = MaturityLevel.L2
    elif not s.regression_tests_from_fp:
        level = MaturityLevel.L2   # tests exist but not complete until regression tests added
    elif s.ml_detectors_with_pr_curves:
        level = MaturityLevel.L4
    else:
        level = MaturityLevel.L3

    return DimensionScore('Rule Quality', level, evidence, gaps, remediation)

def score_attck_coverage(s: ProgrammeState) -> DimensionScore:
    evidence: list[str] = []
    gaps: list[str] = []
    remediation: list[str] = []

    if s.heatmap_generated:
        evidence.append('ATT&CK/ATLAS heat map generated')
    else:
        gaps.append('No coverage heat map')
        remediation.append('Section 11.05: attck_coverage_heatmap.py --save baseline.json')

    if s.heatmap_in_ci_diff_mode:
        evidence.append('Heat map runs in CI diff mode; regression triggers CI failure')
    else:
        gaps.append('Heat map not in CI; coverage regression undetected automatically')
        remediation.append('Section 11.05: add attck_coverage_heatmap.py --diff to CI pipeline')

    if s.taxonomy_expansion_check:
        evidence.append('Quarterly new ATLAS technique check configured')
    else:
        gaps.append('No taxonomy expansion check; new ATLAS techniques invisible')
        remediation.append('Section 11.05: attck_coverage_heatmap.py --check-new-techniques in quarterly review')

    if s.quantitative_coverage_targets:
        evidence.append('Quantitative per-tactic coverage targets set (L4)')
    if s.coverage_improvement_rate_tracked:
        evidence.append('Coverage improvement rate tracked as programme metric (L5)')

    if not s.heatmap_generated:
        level = MaturityLevel.L1
    elif not s.heatmap_in_ci_diff_mode or not s.taxonomy_expansion_check:
        level = MaturityLevel.L2
    elif s.coverage_improvement_rate_tracked:
        level = MaturityLevel.L5

Common Mistakes

Id

CM11.08.1

Mistake

Assigning a maturity level based on the best-performing dimension rather than the weakest

Why It Happens

A detection programme with an excellent CI/CD pipeline and a strong ATT&CK heat map is genuinely impressive in those dimensions. It is tempting to report overall programme maturity at the level of the best dimensions rather than the composite.

Why It Matters

A programme that is L4 on pipeline and coverage but L1 on FPR management is an L1 programme that will generate alert fatigue and lose analyst trust within months of production deployment. Reporting L4 maturity to leadership based on two dimensions conceals the L1 FPR gap and delays the investment needed to close it.

Correct Approach

The composite maturity level is the floor across all five dimensions. Report the floor, not the ceiling. Present per-dimension scores alongside the composite to show which dimensions are strong and which need investment. The per-dimension breakdown is more actionable for leadership than a single number.

Id

CM11.08.2

Mistake

Treating L3 as aspirational rather than as the minimum bar for production

Why It Happens

L3 sounds advanced. In a field where most programmes are at L1, reaching L3 feels like an ambitious achievement rather than a baseline requirement.

Why It Matters

An AI system facing external adversaries deployed with an L1 or L2 detection programme is a production system with no effective mechanism to detect when its detection is failing. Rule regressions go unnoticed; FPR accumulates until analysts stop responding; coverage gaps widen without anyone noticing. These are not theoretical risks — they are the consistent failure pattern of under-matured detection programmes.

Correct Approach

Treat L3 as the minimum bar for production, not as an achievement. If L3 cannot be reached before a production launch, the launch date should be deferred or a documented risk acceptance should accompany the deployment, naming exactly which dimensions are below L3 and what the acceptance criteria are for each gap.

Id

CM11.08.3

Mistake

Conflating the maturity score with security effectiveness

Why It Happens

A high maturity score feels like confirmation that the programme is effective. An L4 programme sounds safer than an L1 programme, and by most measures it is. But maturity is a process quality measure, not an outcome guarantee.

Why It Matters

An L4 programme with excellent process can have significant ATT&CK coverage gaps for recently published techniques. An L3 programme may have lower process quality but happen to have dense coverage of the specific techniques most relevant to its deployment profile. Maturity is a necessary but not sufficient condition for effective detection.

Correct Approach

Present maturity score and coverage score together. Maturity tells you how well the programme is run. Coverage tells you what the programme actually detects. Both are required for a complete picture. A high maturity score with amber-only coverage is still a programme with significant gaps.

Id

CM11.08.4

Mistake

Scoring maturity once and treating it as permanent documentation

Why It Happens

Running the maturity assessment takes time. Once completed, the result goes into a report and is not revisited until an audit or an incident prompts a review.

Why It Matters

Maturity regresses. A team that turns over loses institutional knowledge. A pipeline that gains exceptions over time loses its enforcement function. An FPR management system that is not actively maintained produces stale targets. A score that was L3 eighteen months ago may be L2 today.

Correct Approach

Run detection_maturity_assessor.py quarterly alongside the ATT&CK coverage review. Append the result to decisions.md with the date. A maturity regression between two consecutive assessments is treated with the same urgency as a coverage regression: it indicates programme health degradation that requires remediation in the next sprint.

Id

CM11.08.5

Mistake

Building L4 capabilities (ML detection) before consolidating L3 (FPR management)

Why It Happens

ML-based detection is technically interesting and feels like forward progress. Adding Chapter 12 ML detectors before completing the FPR management work from Section 11.09 is a common ordering error.

Why It Matters

ML detectors have higher and more variable FPR than well-calibrated signature rules. Deploying ML detectors into a programme without per-rule FPR targets and automated FPR monitoring is precisely the scenario that produces the alert fatigue death spiral described in Section 11.09: high ML FPR, analysts dismiss alerts, true positives missed. The ML investment produces negative defensive value.

Correct Approach

Follow the 90-day roadmap ordering: L3 FPR management (Section 11.09) before L4 ML detector integration (Chapter 12). The FPR tracking infrastructure built in Section 11.09 is what makes it safe to deploy ML detectors: every new ML detector gets a FPR target and automated monitoring from day one.

Section Summary

What You Learned

The five-level detection maturity model provides a measurable, communicable, and improvable programme assessment across five dimensions: rule quality, ATT&CK coverage, FPR management, deployment pipeline, and proactive hunting. Maturity is the floor across all dimensions, not the ceiling. The seven-track gateway is currently at L1 composite (post-Part-2 baseline), with three dimensions at L2 and one at L3 after Sections 11.02–11.07. L3 is the minimum bar for production AI systems and is achievable in 90 days using the tooling from Chapters 11–13. L4 requires additional ML infrastructure and a red team programme. The 90-day roadmap sequences the remaining Chapter 11 sections — FPR management (11.09), hunting (11.12), and pipeline RBAC — as the path from current state to L3.
