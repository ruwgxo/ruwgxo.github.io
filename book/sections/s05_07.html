Why ATLAS Exists: The Gap ATT&CK Could Not Fill

Content

mitre_att_and_ck_background: | MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge) was first published in 2013 as a structured knowledge base of adversary behaviour observed in real intrusions against enterprise networks. It catalogues how adversaries: gain initial access, execute code, establish persistence, escalate privilege, evade defences, move laterally, collect data, and exfiltrate it. ATT&CK became the de facto standard vocabulary for detection engineering, red teaming, and threat intelligence sharing across the security industry. By 2020, ATT&CK was referenced in vendor documentation, government advisories, and detection rule sets worldwide. the_gap_att_and_ck_leaves: | ATT&CK was built on observed behaviour against traditional IT systems: operating systems, network protocols, enterprise software. As ML systems began appearing in production deployments — in content moderation, fraud detection, autonomous vehicles, healthcare diagnostics — a new class of attack emerged that ATT&CK had no vocabulary for: gap_01_adversarial_examples: | Adversarial examples (Szegedy et al., 2013) exploit the decision boundaries of neural networks using crafted inputs that are indistinguishable to humans but cause the model to misclassify. This is not privilege escalation, not code execution, not lateral movement — it is manipulation of a learned function. ATT&CK has no technique for it. gap_02_training_data_poisoning: | An adversary who can influence a model's training data — by injecting examples, manipulating labels, or contaminating a data source that feeds a training pipeline — can alter the model's behaviour before it is ever deployed. This attack occurs months before the model reaches production. ATT&CK covers supply chain compromise of software, but not the systematic manipulation of statistical learning at training time. gap_03_model_extraction: | Reconstructing a model's functionality by querying its API has no ATT&CK analogue. The "exfiltration" of a neural network is not file transfer — it is the statistical reconstruction of learned weights through inference queries. The target is not data; it is a function approximator. gap_04_inference_evasion: | Bypassing a deployed ML-based security system (malware classifier, fraud detector, deepfake detector) through adversarially crafted inputs is an attack technique with no ATT&CK entry. The closest analogue is "defence evasion" — but the mechanism is exploitation of gradient-based vulnerabilities, not OS-level or network-level technique. atlas_origin: | MITRE ATLAS (Adversarial Threat Landscape for Artificial-Intelligence Systems) was developed by MITRE's AI security team in collaboration with industry partners and first published in 2021. It was explicitly designed to fill the ATT&CK gap for ML systems, using the same structural framework (tactics, techniques, sub-techniques) and the same philosophy (empirically grounded in real incidents, not theoretical). design_principles: att_and_ck_compatible: | ATLAS uses the same tactic-technique-sub-technique hierarchy as ATT&CK, enabling practitioners who know ATT&CK to immediately read ATLAS. Where an adversary campaign spans both traditional IT and ML systems (the common case), practitioners can track the full kill chain across both frameworks without switching vocabulary. empirically_grounded: | Every ATLAS technique is backed by at least one case study documenting real adversary behaviour — either a confirmed real-world incident or a peer-reviewed research demonstration. Theoretical attacks without empirical grounding are not included. living_framework: | ATLAS is versioned and continuously updated as new ML attack techniques are documented. The community can contribute case studies through a submission process, making ATLAS a living threat intelligence resource rather than a static catalogue. atlas_in_one_sentence: | ATLAS is to ML security what ATT&CK is to network and endpoint security: a structured, empirically grounded, community-maintained catalogue of adversary tactics and techniques, enabling detection engineers, red teamers, and threat intelligence analysts to speak a common language about ML-specific threats.

ATLAS Structure: Tactics, Techniques, Sub-Techniques, and Case Studies

Content

four_level_hierarchy: level_1_tactics: definition: | A tactic is the adversary's goal — what they are trying to achieve. Tactics are high-level and stable: they change rarely because adversary goals (reconnaissance, access, impact) are fundamental regardless of the specific technology being attacked. count_in_atlas: "12 tactics in ATLAS v2 (2024)" example: "ML Reconnaissance — the adversary is trying to learn about the target ML system" att_and_ck_parallel: "Initial Reconnaissance in ATT&CK" level_2_techniques: definition: | A technique answers: how does the adversary achieve their tactical goal? Techniques are more specific than tactics and evolve as new attack methods are discovered. Each technique has a unique AML.TXXXX identifier. count_in_atlas: "~75 techniques across 12 tactics (ATLAS v2)" example: "AML.T0040 — ML Model Inference API Access (technique for ML Reconnaissance)" att_and_ck_parallel: "Techniques in ATT&CK (e.g. T1566 Phishing)" level_3_sub_techniques: definition: | Sub-techniques describe specific implementations of a technique — variations in how the technique is executed. Not all techniques have sub-techniques. example: | AML.T0043.000 — Craft Adversarial Data: Perform Black-box Optimization AML.T0043.001 — Craft Adversarial Data: Perform White-box Optimization AML.T0043.002 — Craft Adversarial Data: Insert Backdoor Triggers att_and_ck_parallel: "Sub-techniques in ATT&CK (e.g. T1566.001 Spearphishing Attachment)" level_4_case_studies: definition: | Case studies are documented real-world incidents or peer-reviewed research demonstrations mapped to ATLAS tactics and techniques. They provide the empirical grounding for every technique entry and give practitioners concrete examples of what each technique looks like in practice. format: | Each case study documents: target system, adversary type, techniques used (as AML.TXXXX references), outcome, detection opportunities, and references. examples: - "Botnet detection system evasion via adversarial example crafting" - "GPT-2 training data extraction via prefix probing (Carlini et al.)" - "Face recognition system bypass via physical adversarial patches" - "Autonomous vehicle lane detection manipulation via road sticker attacks" reading_an_atlas_technique_entry: | Every ATLAS technique entry contains: id: "AML.TXXXX or AML.TXXXX.YYY" name: "Short descriptive name" tactic: "Which of the 12 tactics this technique serves" description: "What the adversary does and why" procedure: "Specific known implementations (linked to case studies)" mitigations: "Recommended countermeasures" detection: "Detection approach and data sources" references: "Academic papers, incident reports, CVEs" atlas_technique_id_format: | AML.TXXXX — technique (4-digit number, e.g. AML.T0040) AML.TXXXX.YYY — sub-technique (3-digit suffix, e.g. AML.T0043.001) AML.MXXXX — mitigation (M prefix) AML.CSXXXX — case study (CS prefix) The "AML" namespace distinguishes ATLAS identifiers from ATT&CK identifiers (which use plain T, M, G, S prefixes), enabling unambiguous cross-reference.

comparison_framework: | ATLAS and ATT&CK are designed to be complementary, not competing. def print_reference_card(tactics: List[AtlasTactic]) -> None:
    print("=" * 70)
    print("MITRE ATLAS — 12 TACTIC REFERENCE CARD")
    print("=" * 70)
    for i, t in enumerate(tactics, 1):
        layers = ", ".join(f"Layer {l}" for l in t.attack_surface_layers)
        print(f"\n{i:2d}. [{t.id}] {t.name}")
        print(f"    Goal:         {t.adversary_goal}")
        print(f"    Surface:      {layers}")
        print(f"    ATT&CK:       {t.att_and_ck_parallel}")
        print(f"    Primary Risk: {t.primary_risk}")
        print(f"    Techniques ({len(t.representative_techniques)}):")
        for tech in t.representative_techniques:
            print(f"      {tech.id:15s} {tech.name}")


def export_json(tactics: List[AtlasTactic], path: str = "atlas_tactics.json") -> None:
    data = [
        {
            "id": t.id,
            "name": t.name,
            "adversary_goal": t.adversary_goal,
            "attack_surface_layers": t.attack_surface_layers,
            "att_and_ck_parallel": t.att_and_ck_parallel,
            "primary_risk": t.primary_risk,
            "techniques": [
                {
                    "id": tech.id,
                    "name": tech.name,
                    "detection_signal": tech.detection_signal,
                    "data_source": tech.data_source,
                    "sigma_tag": tech.sigma_tag,
                }
                for tech in t.representative_techniques
            ],
        }
        for t in tactics
    ]
    with open(path, "w") as f:
        json.dump(data, f, indent=2)
    print(f"\nExported to {path}")


def export_sigma_tags(tactics: List[AtlasTactic],
                       path: str = "atlas_sigma_tags.txt") -> None:
    tags = sorted(set(
        tech.sigma_tag
        for t in tactics
        for tech in t.representative_techniques
    ))
    with open(path, "w") as f:
        for tag in tags:
            f.write(tag + "\n")
    print(f"Sigma tag list ({len(tags)} tags) exported to {path}")

def score_coverage(matrix: List[DetectionOpportunity],
                    covered_technique_ids: List[str]) -> Dict:
    for entry in matrix:
        entry.covered = entry.technique_id in covered_technique_ids

    total = len(matrix)
    covered = sum(1 for e in matrix if e.covered)
    by_difficulty = {"low": 0, "medium": 0, "high": 0}
    gaps_by_difficulty = {"low": [], "medium": [], "high": []}
    for e in matrix:
        if not e.covered:
            gaps_by_difficulty[e.difficulty].append(e.technique_id)
        else:
            by_difficulty[e.difficulty] += 1

    return {
        "total_techniques": total,
        "covered": covered,
        "coverage_pct": round(covered / total * 100, 1),
        "covered_by_difficulty": by_difficulty,
        "gaps_by_difficulty": gaps_by_difficulty,
    }


def print_coverage_report(report: Dict) -> None:
    print("\nATLAS DETECTION COVERAGE REPORT")
    print("=" * 50)
    print(f"Techniques in matrix: {report['total_techniques']}")
    print(f"Covered:              {report['covered']}")
    print(f"Coverage:             {report['coverage_pct']}%")
    print(f"\nGaps by detection difficulty:")
    for diff in ["low", "medium", "high"]:
        gaps = report["gaps_by_difficulty"][diff]
        if gaps:
            print(f"  {diff.upper():6s}: {', '.join(gaps)}")
        else:
            print(f"  {diff.upper():6s}: None — fully covered")
    print("\nRecommendation: Address low-difficulty gaps first (high ROI detection).")

ATLAS enables quantitative detection programme coverage measurement: covered techniques / total techniques = coverage percentage. This metric is defensible to stakeholders in a way that OWASP risk counts are not, because ATLAS techniques are specific observable behaviours, not risk categories.

Takeaway 08

Red team exercises structured around ATLAS tactics produce the most operationally realistic ML security assessments.
