Chapter 17 · Section 3 · ~6 pages

Performance Monitoring: Latency, Throughput, and Errors

Section Overview

One Line Summary

The second measurement family: the performance metrics that quantify how well the suite runs -- latency tracked with percentiles, throughput measured against provisioned capacity, and error rate per detector, all over rolling time windows -- with continuous tracking of the sub-50ms P99 budget and regression detection that fires in a warning band before the budget is breached, built on the Chapter 14 golden signals rather than replacing them.

Why This Section Exists

Detection metrics measure whether the suite catches attacks; performance metrics measure whether it can be run at all in production. A suite that detects perfectly but breaches its latency budget is not deployable, and a suite that met its budget at deployment can regress silently as detectors are tuned and traffic shifts. This section builds the monitoring that watches the running suite over time, and its emphasis is deliberately on over time: Chapter 14 instrumented the suite so it could be observed at a moment; Section 17.03 watches the trend, because the failure mode that matters here is not a sudden spike but a slow creep toward the budget that a point-in-time check misses until it is too late. The section makes three commitments. First, it tracks the metrics that matter as distributions, not averages: latency is monitored by percentile, because the P99 -- the slow tail one request in a hundred experiences -- is the number the budget is written against, and an average latency can look healthy while the P99 quietly breaches. Second, it detects regressions against the budget with a warning band: a P99 that has climbed past 80% of the budget is flagged before it crosses 100%, so the regression is caught while there is still headroom to act. Third, it builds on Chapter 14 rather than duplicating it -- consuming the same execution samples the golden-signal instrumentation already emits -- and it connects performance back to detection, because the two are coupled: a detector tuned for higher recall may cost latency, so performance monitoring is an input to the tuning decisions of the improvement half, not a separate concern.

Position In Chapter

Section 17.03 completes the measurement stage begun in 17.02, building the performance half to sit beside the detection half. Together they are the 'measure' step of the improvement loop. It builds directly on the Chapter 14 Section 14.08 observability stack, reusing its execution samples, and it feeds the tuning and scaling decisions that the retraining (17.05) and A/B testing (17.06) sections make, where a detection gain must be weighed against its performance cost.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

Performance as the Other Half of Measurement

Body

The improvement loop's measurement stage has two halves because a detector suite can fail in two independent ways. Section 17.02 measured the first: a suite can detect badly, missing attacks or drowning analysts in false alarms. This section measures the second: a suite can run badly, taking too long, falling over under load, or erroring out, regardless of how well it detects. The two failures are independent -- a suite can be accurate and slow, or fast and blind -- which is exactly why both must be watched, and why a team that monitors only one is exposed on the other. Performance monitoring is the discipline of knowing, continuously, whether the suite can actually be run in production at the quality it was built to. The governing number is the latency budget: the sub-50ms P99 the suite was designed against in Part 4. That budget is a promise to the gateway that detection will not add unacceptable delay to a request, and it is a promise that erodes. Detectors get tuned and grow heavier; traffic shifts toward inputs that take longer to evaluate; a model swap for better recall costs milliseconds. None of these announce themselves, and any of them can walk the P99 toward the budget over days. The purpose of this section is to make that erosion visible while it is still erosion and not yet a breach -- to watch the running suite over time so that a regression is a caught event with headroom to respond, not a page in the middle of the night when the budget is already blown.

Part 2

Title

Latency by Percentile: Why the Tail Is the Budget

Body

The single most important decision in latency monitoring is to measure it as a distribution, not an average, because the average is the number that lies. Imagine a detector that runs in 8 milliseconds for ninety-nine requests out of a hundred and 400 milliseconds for the hundredth. Its average latency is about 12 milliseconds -- comfortably under a 50ms budget, a green dashboard, nothing to see. But one request in a hundred is taking 400 milliseconds, eight times the budget, and for a suite handling millions of requests that is tens of thousands of users experiencing an unacceptable delay. The average hid the failure by drowning the slow tail in the fast majority. The budget is written against the P99 -- the latency the 99th-percentile request experiences -- precisely because the tail is where the pain is, and the tail is invisible to the mean. So the monitor tracks percentiles: the P50 to know the typical experience, and the P99 to know the tail the budget governs. Computing a percentile requires the distribution, so the monitor keeps a rolling window of recent latency samples per detector and reads the percentiles off it. The window is bounded -- a fixed number of recent samples -- so memory is constant and the measurement always reflects the recent past rather than the whole history, which matters because a regression that started an hour ago should show up now, not be diluted by a day of healthy samples. Percentiles over a bounded recent window are what make latency monitoring both truthful about the tail and responsive to change.

Part 3

Title

Throughput, Saturation, and Errors

Body

Latency is one golden signal; the section tracks the others that bear on whether the suite can be run. Throughput is the rate of requests the suite is actually handling, measured over the window's time span, and on its own it is just a number. It becomes meaningful against provisioned capacity: saturation is observed throughput as a fraction of the capacity the suite was provisioned for, and a saturation approaching or exceeding 1.0 means the suite is at or over its ceiling, where latency degrades and requests queue. Saturation is the early signal that the suite needs scaling before load turns into latency, and it connects performance monitoring to the scaling decisions that sit alongside tuning -- a detector at 90% saturation is one traffic spike away from breaching its budget for reasons that have nothing to do with the detector itself. Error rate is the third signal and a distinct one: the fraction of executions that failed outright, per detector. It is distinct from latency because a detector can be fast and wrong -- erroring quickly is still failing -- and distinct from detection quality because an error is not a verdict at all, it is the absence of one. A rising error rate on a single detector points at that detector's health -- a model-serving fault, a resource exhaustion, a bad deploy -- rather than at the suite's load or its detection tuning, so watching it per detector localises the problem. Together, latency percentiles, saturation, and per-detector error rate are the operational picture: how slow, how loaded, how broken. Each is read off the same rolling window, so one snapshot answers all three.

Part 4

Title

Regression Detection with a Warning Band

Body

Monitoring that only tells you the budget has been breached is monitoring that tells you too late. The point of watching the P99 over time is to catch it climbing toward the budget while there is still room to act, and that requires a threshold below the budget itself. The section's regression detection uses a warning band: a P99 under 80% of the budget is healthy; a P99 between 80% and 100% of the budget is a warning -- the suite is still within budget but has lost most of its headroom and is trending the wrong way; a P99 over the budget is a breach. The warning status is the one that earns the monitoring its keep, because it fires while the suite is still meeting its promise, giving a tuner time to find and reverse the regression before any user experiences a breach. This three-state view -- ok, warning, breach -- turns latency from a pass/fail check into a trend signal. A detector that has drifted from 20ms to 42ms against a 50ms budget is still technically passing, but it has moved from 40% to 84% of budget and is now in the warning band; the monitor surfaces that as a regression to investigate, not a green light. The headroom it reports -- how many milliseconds remain before breach -- quantifies how urgent the response is. Catching the regression here, in the warning band, is the difference between a tuning task scheduled for this week and an incident tonight. It is the performance analogue of the drift detection Chapter 13 built for detection quality: a signal that something has moved, raised early enough to act on deliberately rather than react to.

Part 5

Title

Building on Chapter 14, and the Performance-Detection Coupling

Body

This monitoring is not built from scratch. Chapter 14 Section 14.08 already instrumented the suite with the golden signals and stood up the metrics pipeline, the logging, and the tracing that emit an execution sample for every detector run -- how long it took, whether it errored, when it happened. Section 17.03 consumes exactly those samples: the performance monitor is a layer that reads the Chapter 14 telemetry into per-detector rolling windows and computes the trend and regression view on top. Reusing the Chapter 14 pipeline rather than instrumenting afresh is not just economy; it guarantees the performance monitoring and the operational observability agree, because they are reading the same underlying data. The monitor adds the time-series and budget-regression semantics that the improvement loop needs; it does not re-measure what Chapter 14 already measures. The final point is why performance monitoring belongs in a chapter about tuning at all: performance and detection quality are coupled, so the two measurement families feed one decision. A detector tuned for higher recall -- a lower threshold, a heavier model, an extra layer -- frequently costs latency, pushing the P99 toward the budget. So a proposed tuning change cannot be judged on its detection metrics alone; its performance cost has to be weighed in the same decision. This is why the improvement loop measures both families before it tunes: the retraining of 17.05 and the A/B testing of 17.06 both read performance alongside detection, promoting a candidate only if it improves detection without breaching the latency budget. Performance monitoring is not a parallel track that operations watches while security tunes; it is one of the two axes every tuning decision is made on. With both measurement families built, the loop turns to its improvement half, beginning with the dataset curation that supplies the labels.

Deliverables

Deliverable 1

Title

The Performance Monitor

Filename

performance_monitor.py

Description

Tracks per-detector performance over rolling windows and detects latency regressions against the budget. DetectorWindow keeps a bounded deque of samples and computes P50, P99, error rate, throughput, and saturation; LatencyBudget defines the sub-50ms P99 target and an 80% warning band; check_regression classifies a window as ok, warning, or breach with the remaining headroom; PerformanceMonitor registers detectors, ingests samples, and reports a snapshot and the regressions across the suite.

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention, standard-library collections (deque), dataclasses, time, and typing only. Percentiles are nearest-rank over the window -- exact and fast at window volumes, no numpy. The window is bounded by count so memory is constant and the measurement tracks the recent past. In the suite the samples are the Chapter 14 Section 14.08 execution telemetry; here sample_event-style samples drive the __main__ block, which shows a healthy detector (ok, wide headroom) beside a regressing one caught in the warning band before it breaches.

Code

# performance_monitor.py
# Section 17.03: Performance Monitoring -- Latency, Throughput, Errors
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 17 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention
#
# Detection metrics say how well the suite detects; these say how well it runs.
# A suite that met the sub-50ms P99 budget at deployment can regress as
# detectors are tuned and traffic shifts, and the regression must be caught
# before it breaches the budget rather than after. This module tracks latency
# (with percentiles), throughput against capacity, and error rate per detector
# over rolling windows, and it flags a regression against the budget -- building
# on the Chapter 14 Section 14.08 golden signals rather than replacing them.

import time
from collections import deque
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Deque, Dict, List, Optional, Tuple


# --- One observed detector execution ----------------------------------------

@dataclass
class PerfSample:
    detector: str
    latency_ms: float
    timestamp: float
    error: bool = False


def _percentile(values: List[float], pct: float) -> float:
    '''Nearest-rank percentile over a copy of the values. Standard library only;
    for the volumes a window holds this is exact and fast enough.'''
    if not values:
        return 0.0
    ordered = sorted(values)
    if pct <= 0:
        return ordered[0]
    if pct >= 100:
        return ordered[-1]
    k = max(0, min(len(ordered) - 1, int(round((pct / 100.0) * len(ordered) + 0.5)) - 1))
    return ordered[k]


# --- A rolling window of samples for one detector ---------------------------
# Bounded by count so memory is fixed; the window is what makes this monitoring
# over time rather than a single aggregate. Percentiles, throughput, and error
# rate are all read off the current window.

@dataclass
class DetectorWindow:
    detector: str
    capacity_rps: float                      # provisioned throughput ceiling
    maxlen: int = 1000
    _samples: Deque[PerfSample] = field(default_factory=lambda: deque(maxlen=1000))

    def __post_init__(self) -> None:
        # deque maxlen must be set at construction; rebuild if a custom maxlen was passed
        if self._samples.maxlen != self.maxlen:
            self._samples = deque(self._samples, maxlen=self.maxlen)

    def record(self, sample: PerfSample) -> None:
        self._samples.append(sample)

    @property
    def count(self) -> int:
        return len(self._samples)

    def latencies(self) -> List[float]:
        return [s.latency_ms for s in self._samples]

    def p50(self) -> float:
        return _percentile(self.latencies(), 50)

    def p99(self) -> float:
        return _percentile(self.latencies(), 99)

    def error_rate(self) -> float:
        if not self._samples:
            return 0.0
        return sum(1 for s in self._samples if s.error) / len(self._samples)

    def throughput_rps(self) -> float:
        '''Observed requests per second across the window's time span.'''
        if len(self._samples) < 2:
            return 0.0
        span = self._samples[-1].timestamp - self._samples[0].timestamp
        return _safe_div(len(self._samples), span)

    def saturation(self) -> float:
        '''Observed throughput as a fraction of provisioned capacity. Above 1.0
        the detector is over capacity -- a Chapter 14 golden-signal concern.'''
        return _safe_div(self.throughput_rps(), self.capacity_rps)


def _safe_div(num: float, den: float) -> float:
    return num / den if den else 0.0


# --- The latency budget and regression detection ----------------------------
# The budget is the sub-50ms P99 target the suite was built to. A regression is
# not just a breach -- it is drift toward the breach that should be caught in the
# warning band before the budget is actually violated.

@dataclass
class LatencyBudget:
    p99_budget_ms: float = 50.0
    warning_fraction: float = 0.8            # warn once P99 crosses 80% of budget

    @property
    def warning_ms(self) -> float:
        return self.p99_budget_ms * self.warning_fraction


@dataclass
class RegressionReport:
    detector: str
    p99_ms: float
    status: str                              # 'ok' | 'warning' | 'breach'
    budget_ms: float
    headroom_ms: float


def check_regression(window: DetectorWindow, budget: LatencyBudget) -> RegressionReport:
    p99 = window.p99()
    if p99 > budget.p99_budget_ms:
        status = 'breach'
    elif p99 >= budget.warning_ms:
        status = 'warning'
    else:
        status = 'ok'
    return RegressionReport(window.detector, round(p99, 3), status,
                            budget.p99_budget_ms, round(budget.p99_budget_ms - p99, 3))


class PerformanceMonitor:
    '''Tracks per-detector performance windows over time and reports regressions
    against the latency budget. Built to sit on top of the Chapter 14 metrics
    pipeline: it consumes the same execution samples the golden-signal
    instrumentation already emits.'''

    def __init__(self, budget: Optional[LatencyBudget] = None) -> None:
        self._budget = budget if budget is not None else LatencyBudget()
        self._windows: Dict[str, DetectorWindow] = {}

    def register(self, detector: str, capacity_rps: float, maxlen: int = 1000) -> None:
        self._windows[detector] = DetectorWindow(detector, capacity_rps, maxlen)

    def record(self, sample: PerfSample) -> None:
        if sample.detector not in self._windows:
            self.register(sample.detector, capacity_rps=1000.0)
        self._windows[sample.detector].record(sample)

    def snapshot(self) -> Dict[str, Dict[str, float]]:
        out: Dict[str, Dict[str, float]] = {}
        for name, w in self._windows.items():
            out[name] = {'p50': round(w.p50(), 3), 'p99': round(w.p99(), 3),
                         'error_rate': round(w.error_rate(), 4),
                         'throughput_rps': round(w.throughput_rps(), 2),
                         'saturation': round(w.saturation(), 3)}
        return out

    def regressions(self) -> List[RegressionReport]:
        return [check_regression(w, self._budget) for w in self._windows.values()]


if __name__ == '__main__':
    mon = PerformanceMonitor(LatencyBudget(p99_budget_ms=50.0))
    mon.register('prompt_injection', capacity_rps=2000.0, maxlen=500)
    mon.register('pii_scanner', capacity_rps=1000.0, maxlen=500)

    t = time.time()
    # prompt_injection: healthy, ~8ms with a thin tail
    for i in range(400):
        lat = 6.0 + (i % 10) * 0.4 + (35.0 if i % 200 == 199 else 0.0)
        mon.record(PerfSample('prompt_injection', lat, t + i * 0.001, error=(i % 300 == 0)))
    # pii_scanner: regressing -- NER model got heavier, P99 creeping toward budget
    for i in range(400):
        lat = 30.0 + (i % 20) * 0.9
        mon.record(PerfSample('pii_scanner', lat, t + i * 0.002, error=False))

    snap = mon.snapshot()
    for name, s in snap.items():
        print(f"{name:18} p50={s['p50']:.1f} p99={s['p99']:.1f} "
              f"err={s['error_rate']:.4f} sat={s['saturation']:.2f}")

    print('\n--- regression check against 50ms P99 budget ---')
    reports = {r.detector: r for r in mon.regressions()}
    for name, r in reports.items():
        print(f'{name:18} P99={r.p99_ms:.1f}ms  [{r.status}]  headroom={r.headroom_ms:.1f}ms')

    assert reports['prompt_injection'].status == 'ok'
    assert reports['pii_scanner'].status in ('warning', 'breach')
    # the healthy detector keeps real headroom; the regressing one has little or none
    assert reports['prompt_injection'].headroom_ms > 0
    assert reports['pii_scanner'].headroom_ms < reports['prompt_injection'].headroom_ms
    print('\nperformance monitoring + regression detection: ok')

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Monitoring latency by average instead of by percentile

Why It Happens

The average is a single, familiar number and it is what dashboards show by default.

Consequence

The average drowns the slow tail in the fast majority, so a P99 that breaches the budget looks healthy on average and tens of thousands of users hit an unacceptable delay unseen.

Correct Approach

Monitor latency by percentile, watch the P99 the budget is written against, and treat the average as at best a secondary view of the typical case.

Mistake 2

Description

Checking performance at a point in time rather than over a window

Why It Happens

A point-in-time aggregate is easy to compute and answers 'is it healthy right now'.

Consequence

A slow regression -- the P99 creeping toward the budget over days -- is invisible to a point check until it breaches, so the failure that matters most is the one that is missed.

Correct Approach

Track the metrics over a bounded rolling window so the recent trend is visible, and detect regressions against the budget while the P99 is still climbing.

Mistake 3

Description

Only alerting once the latency budget is already breached

Why It Happens

The budget is the promise, so breaching it feels like the natural and only place to raise an alarm.

Consequence

By the time the alert fires the promise is already broken and users are experiencing the delay; there was no warning while there was still headroom to act.

Correct Approach

Use a warning band below the budget -- flag a regression once the P99 crosses 80% -- so the climb is caught while the suite still meets its budget and can be corrected before a breach.

Mistake 4

Description

Judging a detection tuning change on its detection metrics alone

Why It Happens

The change was made to improve recall or precision, so those are the numbers that get checked.

Consequence

A recall gain that quietly cost latency pushes the P99 toward or past the budget, trading a detection improvement for a performance regression that no one weighed.

Correct Approach

Judge every tuning change on both families -- promote it only if it improves detection without breaching the latency budget -- because performance and detection are coupled.

Compact

Section 17.03 completes the loop's measurement stage with the performance family: the metrics that say how well the suite runs, as opposed to how well it detects. The governing number is the sub-50ms P99 latency budget, and the section's central discipline is measuring latency as a distribution rather than an average, because an average can look healthy while the P99 -- the tail one request in a hundred experiences -- breaches the budget; the budget is written against the tail because the tail is where the pain is. The monitor keeps a bounded rolling window of samples per detector and reads latency percentiles, throughput, saturation against provisioned capacity, and error rate off it, so one snapshot gives the full operational picture: how slow, how loaded, how broken. Regression detection uses a warning band -- ok under 80% of budget, warning between 80% and 100%, breach above -- so a P99 climbing toward the budget is caught while the suite still meets its promise and there is headroom to act, turning latency from a pass/fail check into an early trend signal, the performance analogue of Chapter 13's drift detection. The monitoring builds on the Chapter 14 golden-signal stack, consuming the same execution samples rather than re-instrumenting, which guarantees it agrees with the operational observability.
