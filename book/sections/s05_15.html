def generate_threat_model(cfg: DeploymentConfig) -> Dict:
    cfg_flags = {k: v for k, v in cfg.__dict__.items() if isinstance(v, bool) and v}
    applicable = []
    for t in THREAT_LIBRARY:
        if not t.conditions or any(c in cfg_flags for c in t.conditions):
            applicable.append(t)

    by_category: Dict[str, List[STRIDEThreat]] = {}
    for t in applicable:
        by_category.setdefault(t.category, []).append(t)

    impact_rank = {"low": 1, "medium": 2, "high": 3, "critical": 4}
    risk_register = sorted(applicable,
        key=lambda t: -({"low":1,"medium":2,"high":3}[t.likelihood] *
                         impact_rank[t.impact]))

    return {
        "deployment": cfg.name,
        "config": cfg.__dict__,
        "threat_count": len(applicable),
        "by_category": {k: [t.__dict__ for t in v] for k, v in by_category.items()},
        "risk_register": [t.__dict__ for t in risk_register],
        "owasp_coverage": sorted(set(o for t in applicable for o in t.owasp)),
        "atlas_coverage": sorted(set(a for t in applicable for a in t.atlas)),
    }

def run_quiz(interactive: bool = True) -> None:
    score = 0
    results = []
    print(f"\n{'='*60}")
    print(f"CHAPTER 5 KNOWLEDGE ASSESSMENT — 20 Questions")

    print(f"{'='*60}\n")

    for q in QUIZ:
        print(f"Q{q.q_id:2d} [{q.block} — {q.level}]")
        print(f"   {q.question}")
        for i, opt in enumerate(q.options):
            print(f"   {i+1}. {opt}")

        if interactive:
            try:
                ans = int(input("   Your answer (1-4): ").strip()) - 1
            except (ValueError, KeyboardInterrupt):
                ans = -1
        else:
            ans = q.correct   # auto-answer for testing

        correct = ans == q.correct
        if correct:
            score += 1
            print(f"   CORRECT")
        else:
            print(f"   INCORRECT — Answer: {q.correct+1}. {q.options[q.correct]}")
        print(f"   Explanation: {q.explanation[:100]}")
        print(f"   Reference: {q.section_ref}\n")
        results.append({"q_id": q.q_id, "correct": correct, "block": q.block})

    pct = score / len(QUIZ) * 100
    print(f"{'='*60}")
    print(f"FINAL SCORE: {score}/{len(QUIZ)} ({pct:.1f}%)")
    if pct >= 80:
        print(f"PASS — Ready to proceed to Chapter 6: Prompt Injection")

    by_block: Dict[str, List] = {}
    for r in results:
        by_block.setdefault(r["block"], []).append(r["correct"])
    print(f"\nPer-block breakdown:")

        b_pct = sum(corrects) / len(corrects) * 100

Key Takeaways

Takeaway 01

AI security is a superset of traditional security, not a replacement. Every traditional security concern (authentication, access control, availability) still applies. What is new is the eight-layer attack surface that extends from training data through model weights to production serving — and the five fundamental ways AI systems fail differently from software.

Takeaway 02

The four frameworks — OWASP LLM Top 10, MITRE ATLAS, NIST AI RMF, and the regulatory stack — cover overlapping terrain from four perspectives. They are not alternatives: OWASP gives the risk checklist, ATLAS gives the detection logic, NIST AI RMF gives the governance process, and regulation gives the legal floor. A mature AI security programme satisfies all four simultaneously and produces a single set of artefacts that evidence each.

Takeaway 03

Detection engineering is the connective tissue between threat knowledge and production protection. Understanding OWASP LLM01 is necessary but insufficient — what matters is the deployed injection classifier, the configured threshold, the tuned FP rate, and the alert playbook. Chapter 5 built the knowledge and the tools. Part 3 (Chapters 11-17) deploys them at production scale.

Takeaway 04

The risk prioritisation matrix and detection investment roadmap eliminate analysis paralysis. Faced with dozens of threats, practitioners who implement Sprint 1 controls (SafeTensors check, DoS rate limit, injection classifier, plugin allowlist) in two weeks have addressed the highest-ROI threats before most organisations have finished their threat modelling workshop.

Takeaway 05

Compliance is not security; security is not compliance. The EU AI Act Article 15 compliance documentation does not protect the model. The GDPR Article 25 privacy by design statement does not prevent membership inference. The detection programme protects the model. The compliance documentation is the audit evidence that the detection programme exists and is operational — both are required.

Takeaway 06

The enterprise threat model (STRIDE applied to RAG deployment) is the deliverable that connects all of Chapter 5. Every section's technical output — from the ATLAS Sigma rules to the bias audit to the ML SBOM — maps to a specific STRIDE category, OWASP risk, or NIST AI RMF function in that threat model. The threat model is not a document that gets filed — it is the living record of the security programme's scope and coverage.

Takeaway 07

Prompt injection is LLM01 because it is the most prevalent LLM attack primitive and the entry point for most production LLM exploits. Chapter 6 takes 18 sections to cover it in full depth. Everything in Chapter 5 — the attack surface, the ATLAS techniques, the detection logic — is the prerequisite for understanding why prompt injection is so difficult to fully defend against and how to approach it.

Takeaway 08

The security mindset Chapter 5 instils: (1) treat training data as untrusted code, (2) evaluate everything through the eight attack surface layers, (3) measure everything the MEASURE function requires, (4) translate every technical finding into management language, and (5) treat bias auditing as penetration testing. These five habits distinguish AI security engineers who build lasting programmes from those who address individual vulnerabilities in isolation.
