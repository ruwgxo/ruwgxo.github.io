Chapter 13 · Section 4 · ~7 pages

Time-Series Anomaly Detection: Temporal Deviation from the Behavioural Envelope

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Behavioural metrics are time series with seasonal structure; Section 13.04 builds the seasonal decomposition, forecasting, change-point, and multi-resolution detectors that flag temporal deviation from an entity envelope, catching both the sudden burst and the slow ramp a static threshold misses.

Why This Section Exists

Section 13.03 established per-entity baselines: a centre and a spread for each behavioural metric. A baseline answers is this single observation far from typical. It does not answer the temporal questions that the most important behavioural attacks raise: is this metric trending upward over days, did its behaviour change at a specific moment, is this spike just the usual Monday morning peak. Those questions require treating the metric as a time series, and that is what Section 13.04 builds. The motivating fact is that behavioural metrics are not stationary. Request rate rises during business hours and falls at night. Refusal rate has a weekly rhythm. A static threshold or a single baseline applied to a seasonal metric produces a detector that fires every morning when traffic legitimately rises and stays silent during a seasonal peak inside which an attack is hiding. The seasonality must be modelled and removed before deviation can be measured, or the detector spends its alert budget on the daily cycle and misses the attack. The section also confronts the two opposite temporal signatures of behavioural abuse. The burst attack is a sudden spike: high amplitude, short duration. The exfiltration campaign is a slow ramp: low amplitude per step, long duration, invisible to any detector tuned for spikes. A single detector tuned for one signature is blind to the other, so the section builds multi-resolution analysis that sees both, and change-point detection that catches the distinct signal of account compromise -- not an outlier point but a shift in the whole behaviour.

Position In Chapter

Section 13.04 is the core temporal detector of Chapter 13, built directly on the 13.03 baselines. It sits between baseline establishment and the drift detection of 13.05, which applies similar distributional comparison to the detectors and the model rather than to user behaviour. It feeds the rate-based detection of 13.07, which uses the temporal rate signal, and the entity profiler of 13.10, which folds temporal anomaly signals into the persistent profile. It is the section where the temporal family produces its primary detection output: a per-metric temporal anomaly score that respects seasonality and catches both spike and ramp.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

Behavioural Metrics Are Seasonal Time Series

Body

A behavioural metric observed over time is a time series, and behavioural time series have structure that a single baseline does not capture. Request rate is the clearest example. It is not a flat random series around a constant mean; it rises during the working day and falls overnight, it is higher on weekdays than weekends, and it may follow a monthly billing or release cycle. These are seasonal components: regular, predictable patterns at fixed periods. A static threshold or a single 13.03 baseline applied to such a series fails in two symmetric ways. Set the threshold high enough that the legitimate morning peak does not trip it, and an attack that occurs during the overnight trough -- when the legitimate rate is low and the threshold is far above it -- sails underneath. Set it low enough to catch the overnight attack, and it fires every single morning when the legitimate rate climbs into the alert band. The detector either misses attacks in the troughs or floods on the peaks, and there is no single static level that avoids both, because the metric's normal is itself a moving curve. The resolution is to model the seasonal curve and measure deviation from it rather than from a constant. The expected request rate at nine on a Tuesday morning is high; an observation that matches the seasonal expectation is normal even though it is high in absolute terms, and an observation that is high relative to the overnight expectation is anomalous even though it is low in absolute terms. Once the seasonal expectation is the reference, the same deviation logic from 13.03 applies -- but now against a time-varying expectation rather than a fixed centre. This is what makes time-series detection necessary as a layer above the static baseline: the baseline gives a centre, the time-series layer makes that centre move with the season.

Part 2

Title

Seasonal Decomposition: Isolating the Residual

Body

The standard way to separate the seasonal structure from the genuine anomalies is decomposition. A time series is decomposed into three additive components: the trend, the slow long-term movement; the seasonal, the repeating pattern at fixed periods; and the residual, what remains after trend and seasonal are removed. The residual is where anomalies live. After the predictable trend and the repeating seasonal cycle are subtracted, an ordinary observation leaves a small residual near zero, and an anomalous observation leaves a large residual. Decomposition turns a seasonal-anomaly problem into a stationary-anomaly problem. The raw series is non-stationary and seasonal, hard to threshold. The residual series, with trend and seasonality removed, is approximately stationary: it hovers near zero with a roughly constant spread, and a simple robust threshold on the residual -- a modified z-score of the residual using the 13.03 robust statistics -- detects anomalies that the raw series obscured. The morning peak, fully explained by the seasonal component, leaves a small residual and does not fire. An overnight attack, unexplained by trend or season, leaves a large residual and does fire, even though its absolute value is below the morning peak. Two practical points govern decomposition for behavioural security. First, the seasonal period must be discovered, not assumed: most behavioural metrics have a daily and a weekly period, and the decomposition must model both, but some metrics have an idiosyncratic period that an autocorrelation analysis reveals. Second, the decomposition itself must be robust to contamination, for the same reason the baseline was: if a sustained attack is present while the seasonal component is being estimated, a non-robust decomposition folds the attack into the seasonal pattern and learns to expect it. A robust decomposition resists this, keeping the attack in the residual where the detector can see it.

Part 3

Title

Forecasting-Based Detection: The Prediction Interval as a Moving Threshold

Body

An equivalent and often more operationally convenient framing is forecasting. Rather than decomposing the whole series, a forecasting model predicts the next value from the history, together with a prediction interval -- a band within which the next value is expected to fall with some confidence. An observation inside the interval is normal; an observation outside it is anomalous, and the distance outside the interval is the anomaly score. The prediction interval is a moving, self-adjusting threshold, which is exactly what a seasonal metric needs. At nine on a Tuesday the forecast is high and the interval sits high, so a high observation is normal. At three overnight the forecast is low and the interval sits low, so the same absolute value that was normal in the morning is now far outside the interval and fires. The forecasting model carries the seasonality, so the threshold inherits it for free. A simple seasonal forecaster -- one that predicts the value at a given time as the recent level adjusted by the seasonal factor for that time of day and day of week -- is sufficient for most behavioural metrics and is cheap to compute and update. The width of the prediction interval is the tuning knob, and it trades precision against recall in the familiar way. A wide interval fires only on extreme deviations: high precision, low recall, it catches only the loud attacks. A narrow interval fires on modest deviations: high recall, lower precision, it catches subtle attacks but also ordinary variation. The interval width is set per metric from the residual spread, using the robust spread from 13.03 so that the interval is not itself inflated by contamination, and tuned against the precision and recall targets for that metric's alert tier.

Part 4

Title

Streaming Detection: Scoring Each Point Without Recomputing History

Body

In production, the temporal detector sees one new observation at a time and must score it immediately, without recomputing the decomposition or refitting the forecaster over the entire history on every point. This is the streaming or online requirement, and it shapes the algorithm choice. A batch detector that recomputes the full decomposition on every new point is correct but does not scale: the cost grows with the history length, and the per-point latency drifts upward as the series lengthens. The streaming detector instead maintains a compact running state -- the current level, the current seasonal factors, the running residual spread -- and updates that state incrementally as each observation arrives. Exponential smoothing is the workhorse here: the level and the seasonal factors are updated by a small step toward each new observation, so the state reflects recent history without storing it, and the per-point cost is constant regardless of how long the series has run. The streaming detector scores each point against the current forecast and interval, then updates its state with the observation -- subject to the same contamination discipline as the baseline: a flagged observation updates the anomaly score but is held out of the state update, so a sustained attack does not smooth itself into the level and seasonal factors. This is the time-series analogue of the 13.03 contamination guard, and it is what keeps the slow ramp from being absorbed into the forecaster the way it would otherwise be absorbed into the baseline.

Part 5

Title

Change-Point Detection: When the Behaviour Changed

Body

Outlier detection and change-point detection answer different questions, and the difference matters for one specific attack. Outlier detection asks which observation is anomalous: a single point far from expectation. Change-point detection asks when did the distribution of the metric change: not one outlier point, but a sustained shift from one regime to another. The account-compromise attack is a change-point, not an outlier. When an account is compromised, the attacker's behaviour is often not a single dramatic spike. It is a sustained change in the entity's normal: the request rate steps to a new level, the endpoint mix shifts, the time-of-day pattern changes, and stays changed. Each individual observation in the new regime may sit within the interval that the forecaster, adapting, has come to expect -- so outlier detection, especially a streaming detector that smooths toward the new level, stops firing after the transition. Change-point detection catches the transition itself: it monitors for the moment the metric's distribution shifts and flags that moment, regardless of whether individual post-transition points look anomalous. The practical method monitors a statistic that is sensitive to a level or distribution shift -- a cumulative-sum of deviations, or a comparison of the recent window's distribution against the preceding window's -- and flags when that statistic crosses a threshold, indicating the two windows are drawn from different distributions. The change-point signal is high-value precisely because it survives the forecaster's adaptation: even as the streaming detector learns to accept the new regime, the change-point detector has recorded that a regime change happened, which is the account-compromise alert. The two detectors are complementary and both belong in the temporal family.

Part 6

Title

Multi-Resolution Analysis: Catching the Spike and the Slow Ramp

Body

The two highest-value temporal attacks have opposite signatures, and a detector tuned for one is blind to the other. The burst attack is high amplitude and short duration: a sudden spike of requests, a flood, a rapid enumeration. It is obvious at a fine time resolution -- per-minute or per-second -- where the spike towers over the surrounding values, and it is invisible at a coarse resolution where it is averaged into a single slightly-elevated hour. The slow-ramp attack is the mirror image: low amplitude per step and long duration, a request rate that creeps up by a few percent a day over weeks. It is invisible at a fine resolution, where each step is lost in ordinary variation, and obvious at a coarse resolution, where the cumulative trend over weeks is unmistakable. No single time resolution catches both. A detector that aggregates per minute sees the burst and misses the ramp; a detector that aggregates per day sees the ramp and misses the burst. Multi- resolution analysis runs the temporal detection at several time scales in parallel -- per minute, per hour, per day -- and combines the signals. The burst fires the fine-resolution detector; the ramp fires the coarse-resolution detector; the entity is flagged if any resolution fires. This is the temporal analogue of the multi-track gateway: parallel detectors at different scales, each covering the attacks the others miss. The combination is not a simple union of alerts, because a single underlying event can fire several resolutions and should not be triple-counted, and because the resolutions differ in their false- positive characteristics. The fine resolution is noisier and produces more false positives per alert; the coarse resolution is steadier but slower. The combined signal weights the resolutions by their reliability and deduplicates alerts that trace to the same underlying interval, producing one temporal anomaly verdict per entity that reflects whichever resolution carries the real signal. The output is the temporal anomaly score that 13.07 and 13.10 consume, and it is, by construction, sensitive to both the spike and the ramp.

time_series_anomaly_detector.py

Description

Decomposes a behavioural metric time series into trend, seasonal, and residual components and flags anomalies in the residual using robust statistics. Code

# time_series_anomaly_detector.py
# Section 13.04: Seasonal Decomposition Residual Anomaly Detector

# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Dict, List


def _median(values: List[float]) -> float:
    s = sorted(values)
    n = len(s)
    if n == 0:
        return 0.0
    mid = n // 2
    return float(s[mid]) if n % 2 else (s[mid - 1] + s[mid]) / 2.0


def _mad(values: List[float], centre: float) -> float:
    if not values:
        return 0.0
    return _median([abs(v - centre) for v in values]) * 1.4826


@dataclass
class Decomposition:
    trend: float
    seasonal: List[float]   # one factor per slot in the period
    residual_centre: float
    residual_spread: float
    period: int


def decompose(series: List[float], period: int) -> Decomposition:
    n = len(series)
    # Trend: robust overall level (median)
    trend = _median(series)
    # Seasonal: median of detrended values at each slot of the period
    slots: Dict[int, List[float]] = {i: [] for i in range(period)}
    for i, v in enumerate(series):
        slots[i % period].append(v - trend)
    seasonal = [_median(slots[i]) if slots[i] else 0.0 for i in range(period)]
    # Residual: what remains after removing trend and seasonal
    residuals = [series[i] - trend - seasonal[i % period] for i in range(n)]
    rc = _median(residuals)
    rs = _mad(residuals, rc)
    return Decomposition(trend, seasonal, rc, rs, period)


def score_observation(value: float, slot: int, decomp: Decomposition) -> float:
    expected = decomp.trend + decomp.seasonal[slot % decomp.period]
    residual = value - expected
    if decomp.residual_spread <= 1e-9:
        return 0.0 if abs(residual) < 1e-9 else float('inf')
    return (residual - decomp.residual_centre) / decomp.residual_spread


if __name__ == '__main__':
    # 7-day hourly-ish series with a daily peak (period 24), low overnight
    import math
    period = 24
    series = []
    for day in range(7):
        for hour in range(24):
            base = 50 + 40 * math.sin((hour - 6) / 24 * 2 * math.pi)
            series.append(max(base, 5))
    decomp = decompose(series, period)

    # Normal morning peak (hour 9): high absolute value, should NOT fire
    morning = decomp.trend + decomp.seasonal[9] + 3
    z_morning = score_observation(morning, 9, decomp)
    # Overnight attack (hour 3): absolute value below morning peak, SHOULD fire
    night_attack = decomp.trend + decomp.seasonal[3] + 60
    z_night = score_observation(night_attack, 3, decomp)

    print(f'Morning peak value {morning:.0f} at hour 9: residual z = {z_morning:.2f} (normal)')
    print(f'Overnight value {night_attack:.0f} at hour 3: residual z = {z_night:.2f} (anomaly)')
    print('The overnight attack fires despite a lower absolute value than the')
    print('morning peak, because seasonal decomposition compares against the hour.')

Streaming Forecast Anomaly Scorer

forecast_anomaly_scorer.py

Description

Streaming anomaly detector using exponential smoothing of level and seasonal factors, scoring each new observation against a prediction interval and updating state incrementally. Holds flagged observations out of the state update as a contamination guard. Implements the forecasting and streaming detection from Section 13.04 Parts 3 and 4.

Implementation Notes

Code

# forecast_anomaly_scorer.py
# Section 13.04: Streaming Forecast Anomaly Scorer
# 20260621 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 13 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Optional, Tuple


@dataclass
class StreamingForecaster:
    period: int
    alpha: float = 0.1          # level smoothing
    gamma: float = 0.05         # seasonal smoothing
    interval_k: float = 3.0     # interval width in robust spreads
    level: Optional[float] = None
    seasonal: List[float] = field(default_factory=list)
    resid_abs_ewma: float = 1.0  # running scale of residuals
    beta: float = 0.05           # residual scale smoothing
    step: int = 0

    def _ensure_init(self, value: float) -> None:
        if self.level is None:
            self.level = value
        if not self.seasonal:
            self.seasonal = [0.0] * self.period

    def forecast(self, slot: int) -> float:
        base = self.level if self.level is not None else 0.0
        return base + self.seasonal[slot % self.period]

    def interval(self, slot: int) -> Tuple[float, float]:
        f = self.forecast(slot)
        half = self.interval_k * self.resid_abs_ewma
        return f - half, f + half

    def score_and_update(self, value: float, slot: int, flagged_hint: bool = False) -> Tuple[float, bool]:
        self._ensure_init(value)
        f = self.forecast(slot)
        residual = value - f
        scale = self.resid_abs_ewma if self.resid_abs_ewma > 1e-9 else 1.0
        z = residual / scale
        lo, hi = self.interval(slot)
        is_anomaly = value < lo or value > hi

        # Contamination guard: do not smooth a flagged/anomalous point into state
        if not (is_anomaly or flagged_hint):
            s = slot % self.period
            new_level = self.alpha * (value - self.seasonal[s]) + (1 - self.alpha) * self.level
            self.seasonal[s] = self.gamma * (value - new_level) + (1 - self.gamma) * self.seasonal[s]
            self.level = new_level
            self.resid_abs_ewma = self.beta * abs(residual) + (1 - self.beta) * self.resid_abs_ewma
        self.step += 1
        return round(z, 3), is_anomaly


if __name__ == '__main__':
    import math
    fc = StreamingForecaster(period=24, interval_k=3.0)
    # Warm up over 5 days of seasonal data
    for day in range(5):
        for hour in range(24):
            v = 50 + 40 * math.sin((hour - 6) / 24 * 2 * math.pi)
            fc.score_and_update(max(v, 5), hour)

    # Day 6: inject an overnight burst at hour 3
    fired = []
    for hour in range(24):
        v = 50 + 40 * math.sin((hour - 6) / 24 * 2 * math.pi)
        v = max(v, 5)
        if hour == 3:
            v += 70  # burst
        z, anom = fc.score_and_update(v, hour)
        if anom:
            fired.append((hour, z))
    print('Anomalies fired on day 6:', fired)
    print('The hour-3 burst fired; the legitimate seasonal peak did not,')
    print('and the burst was held out of the smoothed state by the guard.')

Detects a sustained shift in a behavioural metric distribution -- the account-compromise signal -- using a cumulative-sum statistic and a two-window distribution comparison. Implementation Notes

Code

# change_point_detector.py
# Section 13.04: Change-Point Detector for Regime Shift
# 20260621 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 13 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Optional


def _mean(values: List[float]) -> float:
    return sum(values) / len(values) if values else 0.0


def _std(values: List[float]) -> float:
    if len(values) < 2:
        return 1.0
    m = _mean(values)
    return (sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in values) / (len(values) - 1)) ** 0.5 or 1.0


@dataclass
class CusumChangePoint:
    threshold: float = 5.0
    drift: float = 0.5
    reference_mean: Optional[float] = None
    reference_std: Optional[float] = None
    cusum_high: float = 0.0
    cusum_low: float = 0.0

    def fit_reference(self, baseline: List[float]) -> None:
        self.reference_mean = _mean(baseline)
        self.reference_std = _std(baseline)

    def update(self, value: float) -> bool:
        # Standardise against the reference regime
        z = (value - self.reference_mean) / self.reference_std
        # Two-sided CUSUM with allowance (drift)
        self.cusum_high = max(0.0, self.cusum_high + z - self.drift)
        self.cusum_low = min(0.0, self.cusum_low + z + self.drift)
        changed = self.cusum_high > self.threshold or self.cusum_low < -self.threshold
        if changed:
            # reset after detecting, so it can detect the next regime
            self.cusum_high = 0.0
            self.cusum_low = 0.0
        return changed


def two_window_shift(recent: List[float], prior: List[float]) -> float:
    # Standardised difference of window means -- a simple regime-shift score
    mp, mr = _mean(prior), _mean(recent)
    sp = _std(prior)
    return abs(mr - mp) / sp


if __name__ == '__main__':
    # Reference regime: ~10 requests/hour
    baseline = [10, 9, 11, 10, 12, 9, 10, 11, 10, 9, 10, 11]
    cp = CusumChangePoint(threshold=5.0, drift=0.5)
    cp.fit_reference([float(v) for v in baseline])

    # Stream: normal, then a sustained step to ~25 (account takeover)
    stream = [10, 11, 9, 10, 12, 25, 24, 26, 25, 27, 24, 25]
    detected_at = None
    for i, v in enumerate(stream):
        if cp.update(float(v)) and detected_at is None:
            detected_at = i
    print(f'Reference mean ~{_mean([float(v) for v in baseline]):.1f}')
    print(f'Sustained shift to ~25 detected at stream index: {detected_at}')

    prior = [float(v) for v in stream[:5]]
    recent = [float(v) for v in stream[5:]]
    print(f'Two-window shift score: {two_window_shift(recent, prior):.2f}')
    print('Change-point flags the regime shift even though each post-shift')
    print('point would look normal once a forecaster adapts to the new level.')

Implementation Notes

Code

# multi_resolution_analyser.py
# Section 13.04: Multi-Resolution Temporal Analyser
# 20260621 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 13 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Dict, List, Tuple


def _median(values: List[float]) -> float:
    s = sorted(values)
    n = len(s)
    if n == 0:
        return 0.0
    mid = n // 2
    return float(s[mid]) if n % 2 else (s[mid - 1] + s[mid]) / 2.0


def _mad(values: List[float], c: float) -> float:
    if not values:
        return 1.0
    return (_median([abs(v - c) for v in values]) * 1.4826) or 1.0


def aggregate(series: List[float], factor: int) -> List[float]:
    # Aggregate fine-resolution points into coarser buckets by sum
    out = []
    for i in range(0, len(series), factor):
        out.append(sum(series[i:i + factor]))
    return out


@dataclass
class ResolutionVerdict:
    resolution: str
    fired: bool
    score: float
    bucket_index: int


def detect_at_resolution(series: List[float], name: str, k: float = 4.0) -> ResolutionVerdict:
    if len(series) < 4:
        return ResolutionVerdict(name, False, 0.0, -1)
    history = series[:-1]
    c = _median(history)
    s = _mad(history, c)
    latest = series[-1]
    score = (latest - c) / s
    return ResolutionVerdict(name, score > k, round(score, 2), len(series) - 1)


def multi_resolution_detect(fine_series: List[float]) -> Tuple[bool, List[ResolutionVerdict]]:
    verdicts = [
        detect_at_resolution(fine_series, 'fine (per-minute)'),
        detect_at_resolution(aggregate(fine_series, 6), 'medium (per-6min)'),
        detect_at_resolution(aggregate(fine_series, 60), 'coarse (per-hour)'),
    ]
    # Dedup: if multiple fire, attribute to the coarsest that fired (one event)
    any_fired = any(v.fired for v in verdicts)
    return any_fired, verdicts


if __name__ == '__main__':
    # Case 1: a short burst -- fine resolution should fire, coarse should not
    burst = [2.0] * 200 + [40.0] + [2.0] * 19
    fired_b, vb = multi_resolution_detect(burst)
    print('Burst case:')
    for v in vb:
        print(f'  {v.resolution:<20} fired={v.fired} score={v.score}')
    print(f'  any fired: {fired_b}\n')

    # Case 2: a slow ramp -- coarse resolution should fire, fine should not
    ramp = [2.0 + i * 0.02 for i in range(220)]
    fired_r, vr = multi_resolution_detect(ramp)
    print('Slow-ramp case:')
    for v in vr:
        print(f'  {v.resolution:<20} fired={v.fired} score={v.score}')
    print(f'  any fired: {fired_r}')
    print('\nThe burst fires the fine resolution; the ramp fires the coarse.')
    print('A single-resolution detector would miss one of the two.')

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Applying a static threshold or a single baseline to a seasonal metric without modelling the seasonality

Why It Happens

The 13.03 baseline gives a centre and a spread, and applying a fixed threshold around that centre is the obvious next step. For a metric that happens to be roughly flat it works, so the seasonality problem is easy to overlook until a clearly seasonal metric like request rate is monitored.

Consequence

A seasonal metric has a normal that moves through the day and week. A threshold set above the morning peak lets an overnight attack pass underneath it; a threshold set to catch the overnight attack fires every morning. The detector spends its entire alert budget on the legitimate daily cycle and hides attacks inside seasonal peaks. The static threshold cannot win against a moving normal.

Correct Approach

Model the seasonality and measure deviation from the time-varying seasonal expectation, via seasonal decomposition or a seasonal forecaster whose prediction interval moves with the season. The detector then compares each observation against what is normal for that hour and day, firing on the overnight anomaly and staying quiet during the legitimate peak. time_series_anomaly_detector.py and forecast_anomaly_scorer.py carry the seasonality so the threshold inherits it.

Mistake 2

Description

Estimating the seasonal pattern with non-robust statistics so a sustained attack is folded into the season

Why It Happens

Seasonal decomposition by simple averaging is the textbook method, and averaging the values at each time slot to estimate the seasonal factor is straightforward. Robustness feels like an unnecessary complication when the data looks clean.

Consequence

If a sustained attack is present while the seasonal component is estimated, a non-robust average folds the attack into the seasonal pattern: the detector learns to expect the elevated level at that time of day and stops flagging it. The attack becomes part of the model's notion of normal, exactly the contamination failure from 13.03 reappearing in the seasonal estimate. Subsequent attacks at the same time slot are now expected and pass.

Correct Approach

Estimate the seasonal and trend components with robust statistics, the median rather than the mean, so a minority of attack observations at a time slot does not move the seasonal factor for that slot. The attack then remains in the residual where the detector sees it. The decompose function in time_series_anomaly_detector.py uses medians for exactly this reason.

Mistake 3

Description

Recomputing the full decomposition or refitting the forecaster over the entire history on every new observation in production

Why It Happens

A batch decomposition is the natural way to develop and validate the detector offline, and reusing that batch code in production is the least-effort path. On a short history it is fast enough that the scaling problem is not visible during testing.

Consequence

The per-point cost of a full recomputation grows with the history length. As the series lengthens over weeks and months in production, the per-observation latency creeps upward until the temporal detector cannot keep up with the event rate, and it either falls behind or is dropped. The detector that worked in a one-week test becomes unviable at production timescales.

Correct Approach

Use a streaming detector that maintains a compact running state -- level, seasonal factors, residual scale -- and updates incrementally with constant per-point cost regardless of history length. Exponential smoothing gives this directly. forecast_anomaly_scorer.py maintains constant-cost state and never recomputes over history.

Mistake 4

Description

Relying only on outlier detection and missing account compromise because the forecaster adapts to the new regime

Why It Happens

Outlier detection is the headline temporal capability: it catches the spike, the obvious anomaly, the dramatic deviation. Once it is built and catching bursts, it feels like the temporal problem is solved, and change-point detection looks like a redundant second mechanism.

Consequence

Account compromise is often a sustained regime change, not a single spike. After the transition, each individual observation sits within the band that a streaming forecaster, smoothing toward the new level, comes to expect, so outlier detection stops firing within a short time of the takeover. The most consequential behavioural attack produces only a brief outlier signal at the transition and then goes quiet as the detector adapts to it.

Correct Approach

Run change-point detection alongside outlier detection. Change-point detection flags the moment the distribution shifts and records that a regime change happened, surviving the forecaster's adaptation to the new level. The change-point signal is the account-compromise alert that outlier detection cannot sustain. change_point_detector.py monitors for the regime shift specifically.

Mistake 5

Description

Detecting at a single time resolution, so the detector is blind to either the burst or the slow ramp

Why It Happens

One time resolution must be chosen to aggregate the metric -- per minute, per hour, per day -- and a single choice is simpler to build, tune, and reason about. Whichever resolution is chosen catches the attacks visible at that scale, which feels like adequate coverage.

Consequence

The burst and the slow ramp have opposite signatures. A fine resolution sees the burst and averages the slow ramp into invisibility; a coarse resolution sees the ramp and averages the burst into a single slightly-elevated bucket. Whichever single resolution is chosen, the detector is structurally blind to one of the two highest-value temporal attacks, and the blind spot is not apparent from testing against the attack the chosen resolution does catch.

Correct Approach

Run the temporal detection at several resolutions in parallel and combine the signals with deduplication, so the burst fires the fine resolution and the ramp fires the coarse resolution and the entity is flagged if any resolution fires. multi_resolution_analyser.py runs fine, medium, and coarse detection together for exactly this coverage.

Section Summary

Compact

Behavioural metrics are seasonal time series, and Section 13.04 builds the temporal detectors that measure deviation from an entity's moving envelope rather than a static threshold. A single threshold on a seasonal metric either fires every morning or hides overnight attacks, so seasonal decomposition separates trend and seasonal structure from the residual where anomalies live, and forecasting frames the same idea as a moving prediction interval that carries the seasonality. The detector must stream: a constant-cost running state updated incrementally, with flagged observations held out of the state update as the temporal contamination guard. Change-point detection answers a different question from outlier detection -- when did the distribution shift -- which is the account-compromise signal that survives the forecaster's adaptation to a new regime. Multi-resolution analysis runs detection at fine, medium, and coarse time scales in parallel so the burst fires the fine resolution and the slow ramp fires the coarse resolution, covering both of the two opposite high-value temporal signatures that any single resolution would miss. The output is a per-entity temporal anomaly score, seasonality- aware and sensitive to both spike and ramp, that the rate and profiling families consume.

Key Takeaways

  • Behavioural metrics are seasonal time series, and a static threshold either fires on every legitimate peak or hides attacks in the troughs -- the seasonality must be modelled and removed before deviation is measured
  • Seasonal decomposition isolates the residual, where anomalies live after trend and seasonal structure are removed, and a seasonal forecaster frames the same idea as a prediction interval that moves with the season
  • The seasonal estimate must use robust statistics or a sustained attack is folded into the seasonal pattern and the detector learns to expect it
  • Temporal detection must stream with constant per-point cost, holding flagged observations out of the state update as the temporal analogue of the baseline contamination guard
  • Change-point detection answers when the distribution shifted -- the account-compromise signal that survives the forecaster adapting to the new regime, which outlier detection cannot sustain
  • Multi-resolution analysis catches both the sudden burst (fine resolution) and the slow ramp (coarse resolution), the two opposite signatures that any single-resolution detector is blind to one of

Section 13.05: Drift Detection -- Data Drift and Concept Drift Beneath the Detectors

Summary

Section 13.05 turns the temporal lens from the user to the detectors and the model, catching the silent degradation that no user-behaviour detector reveals. It distinguishes data drift -- the input feature distribution shifting away from the training distribution -- from concept drift, where the relationship between features and labels changes so a once-accurate detector decays with no input-side signal. It builds population stability index monitoring on input features, Kolmogorov-Smirnov and chi-squared tests for distribution comparison, and prediction-distribution monitoring that detects drift through the classifier score distribution rather than the inputs. It connects drift alerts to the Chapter 12 Section 12.14 retraining trigger and distinguishes benign drift (seasonal, product change) from adversarial drift (attack-distribution shift). Without drift monitoring, the Chapter 12 detectors are decaying assets with no alarm on their decay; Section 13.05 is that alarm.
