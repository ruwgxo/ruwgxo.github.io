Section Overview

One Line Summary

Five chapters of attacker knowledge demand a formal detection engineering discipline — this section makes the shift explicit, inventories what Part 2 built, and frames Chapter 11 as the methodology layer that governs everything Chapters 12–14 will implement.

Why This Section Exists

Part 2 (Chapters 6–10) armed you with attacker knowledge: how prompt injection works, how jailbreaks bypass alignment, how data poisoning corrupts training, how model extraction reconstructs proprietary weights, how adversarial examples fool classifiers. Each chapter also produced detection artefacts — Sigma rules, Python detectors, a growing gateway — but it produced them reactively, one attack family at a time. The result is a detection posture that is broader than most organisations achieve but narrower than it needs to be, and fragile in predictable ways: rules without tests, coverage without measurement, thresholds without calibration, no maturity baseline. Chapter 11 steps back. It does not introduce a new attack family. Instead it establishes the detection engineering framework that governs all seven gateway tracks and every rule produced hereafter: how rules are authored, tested, versioned, and deployed; how coverage is measured against MITRE ATT&CK and OWASP LLM Top 10; how false positives are managed; and how a detection organisation grows from ad-hoc Sigma rules to a production-grade detection-as-code programme. This section is the pivot point.

Position In Chapter

Section 11.01 opens Chapter 11. It frames the shift from attacker knowledge (Part 2) to detection engineering discipline (Part 3). Every subsequent section builds on the vocabulary and mental model established here.

Prose Content

Part 1 Attack Knowledge Prerequisite

Heading

Why Attack Knowledge Comes First

Body

Detection is not pattern matching. It is adversarial prediction: given that a skilled attacker is trying to breach your system right now, what observable evidence will they leave, and how do you distinguish that evidence from the noise of legitimate traffic? That question cannot be answered without answering a prior question: what will the attacker do? Defenders who skip attacker knowledge and jump straight to rule writing end up doing one of two things. They copy rules from public threat intelligence without understanding why those rules work — and cannot adapt them when the attacker changes tactics. Or they write rules for the attacks they can imagine, which is invariably a subset of the attacks a skilled attacker has already rehearsed. Part 2 was not optional background reading. It was attacker knowledge acquisition. You studied prompt injection until you could write a working injection yourself. You studied model extraction until you understood the query budget required to reconstruct a decision boundary. That knowledge is now the foundation of every detection rule in Chapter 11 and beyond. The attack-detection symmetry is exact: you cannot write a reliable detector for an attack you do not understand at the mechanism level. The seven-track gateway assembled across Chapters 6–10 is the concrete proof of this principle. Track 1 (injection detection) exists because Section 6.02 dissected how direct injection works. Track 6 (the query monitor) exists because Chapter 9 mapped the query patterns that model extraction produces. Each track is attacker knowledge translated into a detection primitive. Chapter 11 formalises that translation.

Part 2 Detection Gap

Heading

The Detection Gap Part 2 Left Behind

Body

After five chapters of attack study and parallel detection work, the gateway covers seven tracks and blocks a range of real attacks. That is more detection depth than most production AI deployments achieve. But depth is not the same as rigour, and breadth is not the same as coverage. Several categories of detection debt accumulated during Part 2: Rules without tests. Every Sigma rule and Python detector in the gateway was written to match a specific attack example. None of them has an automated test that confirms it fires on a known-bad input and does not fire on a known-good input. A rule without a test is a hypothesis, not a detection artefact. Thresholds without calibration. Track 2 flags a prompt when its perplexity exceeds 150. Track 7 blocks when its ensemble score exceeds 0.55. Both numbers were chosen to work on the examples in the chapter. Neither was calibrated against a representative sample of production traffic. An uncalibrated threshold is as likely to produce alert fatigue as reliable detection. Coverage without measurement. The seven tracks cover a selection of MITRE ATLAS techniques relevant to the attacks studied. But which ATLAS techniques have no corresponding track? Which OWASP LLM Top 10 categories are undetected? Without a coverage map, the gaps are invisible. Rules without version history. The gateway files exist in a working directory. They do not have commit history, release tags, or a changelog. If a rule is changed to reduce false positives, there is no record of what changed, why, or what the previous version was. Detection without maturity baseline. Is the current gateway at maturity Level 1, Level 3, or Level 5? Without a defined maturity model, there is no answer to that question and therefore no roadmap for improvement. Chapter 11 addresses all five categories. Each section in the chapter targets one or more of these debts directly.

Part 3 Detection Engineering As Discipline

Heading

Detection Engineering as a Discipline

Body

Security engineering and detection engineering are adjacent disciplines that are frequently conflated and should not be. Security engineering designs and builds secure systems: network segmentation, access controls, encryption, secure coding practices. Its primary deliverables are system properties — confidentiality, integrity, availability — enforced through architecture and implementation. Detection engineering designs and builds detection systems: rules, models, workflows, and pipelines that surface evidence of attacks that bypassed preventive controls. Its primary deliverables are detection artefacts — rules with known precision and recall, tested against labelled data, deployed through a managed pipeline, with false positive rates tracked per rule. The question security engineering answers is "how do we prevent this attack?" The question detection engineering answers is "how do we know when prevention has failed?" In mature organisations, these are separate teams with separate skills. A detection engineer needs to be simultaneously: a threat analyst (understands attacker behaviour deeply enough to predict what evidence they leave), a software engineer (writes and tests code that runs in production), a data scientist (calibrates thresholds and interprets precision-recall tradeoffs), and an operations engineer (manages the deployment pipeline and observes rule health in production). The combination is rare. The discipline has its own vocabulary: detection-as-code, detection lifecycle, detection maturity, ATT&CK coverage, false positive rate, mean time to detect. Part of Chapter 11's job is to establish that vocabulary precisely, so that Chapters 12–14 can use it without re-definition.

Part 4 Transition To Dac

Heading

From Ad-Hoc Detection to Detection-as-Code

Body

Ad-hoc detection is how every detection programme starts. An attacker technique is identified. A rule is written. The rule is deployed. The cycle repeats for each new technique. No single step is wrong. The aggregate is fragile. Ad-hoc programmes share a failure pattern: rules are written individually, deployed informally, rarely tested after deployment, and never retired even when they stop being relevant. The result is a rule set that grows monotonically, with no systematic way to measure its effectiveness, no way to understand which rules are producing value and which are generating noise, and no way to adapt the programme to evolving attacker behaviour. Detection-as-code (DaC) applies software engineering discipline to detection rule authoring. Every rule is a code artefact. It lives in version control. It has a test file. It has a review process before deployment. It has a deployment pipeline. It has metrics tracked in production. When a rule's false positive rate crosses a threshold, an alert fires in the detection engineering queue — not in the SOC queue. The five principles of detection-as-code, which Section 11.02 develops in full: Principle 1 — Rules are code. A Sigma rule is a software artefact governed by the same version control, code review, and deployment practices as any other piece of production code. The detection-as-code principle is violated the moment a rule is edited directly in a production SIEM without a corresponding commit to the rule repository. Principle 2 — Every rule has a test. A detection rule without a test is a hypothesis. Tests confirm that the rule fires on known-bad inputs (true positive validation) and does not fire on known-good inputs (false positive validation). Tests run in CI on every commit. Principle 3 — Coverage is measured, not assumed. What techniques does the current rule set cover? What is the ATT&CK heat map? Coverage that is not measured is a guess. Principle 4 — False positive rates are tracked per rule. Not as an aggregate across the entire rule set, but per rule. A rule with a 40% false positive rate is consuming analyst time without proportional defensive value. It is a candidate for calibration or retirement. Principle 5 — The detection programme has a maturity level. Maturity determines the investment required, the quality of outputs expected, and the roadmap for improvement. The gateway built in Part 2 will be refactored against these five principles throughout Chapter 11. By Section 11.13, it will be a detection-as-code artefact rather than a collection of ad-hoc scripts.

Part 6 Seven Track Inventory

Heading

The Part 2 Detection Inventory

Body

Before introducing Chapter 11's framework, it is worth naming precisely what Part 2 produced. The inventory below is the starting point; Section 11.08 will score each item against the maturity model. Track 1 — Injection and Semantic Classifiers (Chapters 6 and 7): the injection classifier from Section 6.17 (injection_classifier.py, including its centroid-similarity gate) and the semantic jailbreak classifier from Section 7.14 (jailbreak_classifier.py), plus the unified LLM01 Sigma rule for AML.T0051 and AML.T0054. Maturity: L1 (exists, no tests, no version history). Tracks 2 to 5 — Jailbreak Detectors (Chapter 7): the perplexity detector (Section 7.9), the shot-count detector (Section 7.10), the trajectory anomaly scorer (Sections 7.5 and 7.7) and the coherence analyser (Section 7.8). Tracks 4 and 5 keep per-session state. Maturity: L1 (thresholds tuned on chapter examples). Track 6 — Query Monitor and Rate Limiter (Chapter 9): query_monitor.py, rate_limiter.py and cross_user_correlator.py from Section 9.11, with the Sigma rules from its sigma_rule_generator.py. The only track with per-user state across sessions. Maturity: L1 (composite threshold uncalibrated on production traffic). Track 7 — Adversarial Detection (Chapter 10): gateway_track7.py from Section 10.13 -- feature squeezing before inference, consistency checking and the adversarial classifier after it -- plus the AML.T0043 and AML.T0015 Sigma rules. Maturity: L1. Two Part 2 artefacts sit outside the seven tracks. The gateway ensemble (seven_track_gateway_ensemble.py, Section 10.13) orchestrates the tracks and combines their scores; it has no fallback testing and no failure-mode documentation. The Chapter 8 poisoning monitor (chapter_08_production_pipeline.py, Section 8.15) runs upstream on training data, not on requests. Both carry the same debt as the tracks. All seven tracks: no automated tests, no version history beyond working directory state, no ATT&CK coverage map, no calibrated thresholds, no FPR tracking. L1 is not a failure; it is a starting point. Chapter 11 builds the programme that moves it to L3 by the end of Part 3.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
'''detection_as_code_principles.py
Five detection-as-code principles evaluated against the seven-track gateway.
Produces a readiness report: which principles are satisfied, which are not.
Chapter 11, Section 11.01 — AI Security Mastery
Author: Raghav Dinesh | github: ruwgxo
'''

from __future__ import annotations
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Callable
import pathlib

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Data model
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

@dataclass
class PrincipleResult:
    principle_id: int
    name: str
    satisfied: bool
    evidence: str
    remediation: str

@dataclass
class GatewayState:
    '''Represents the observable state of the seven-track gateway post-Part-2.'''
    tracks: list[str] = field(default_factory=lambda: [
        'Track 1 — Injection and Semantic Classifiers',
        'Track 2 — Perplexity Detector',
        'Track 3 — Shot-Count Detector',
        'Track 4 — Trajectory Anomaly Scorer',
        'Track 5 — Coherence Analyser',
        'Track 6 — Query Monitor and Rate Limiter',
        'Track 7 — Adversarial Detection',
    ])
    has_git_repo: bool = False
    has_test_suite: bool = False
    has_ci_pipeline: bool = False
    has_attck_coverage_map: bool = False
    fpr_tracked_per_rule: bool = False
    maturity_level_defined: bool = False

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Principle evaluators
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def evaluate_rules_are_code(state: GatewayState) -> PrincipleResult:
    satisfied = state.has_git_repo
    return PrincipleResult(
        principle_id=1,
        name='Rules are code (version control)',
        satisfied=satisfied,
        evidence=(
            'Git repository with commits per rule change detected.'
            if satisfied else
            'No Git repository found for gateway rule files.'
        ),
        remediation=(
            'Already satisfied.'
            if satisfied else
            'Initialise a Git repo for the detection-as-code pipeline; '
            'commit all seven track files with descriptive messages. '
            'See Section 11.02 for the recommended repository structure.'
        ),
    )

def evaluate_every_rule_has_tests(state: GatewayState) -> PrincipleResult:
    satisfied = state.has_test_suite
    return PrincipleResult(
        principle_id=2,
        name='Every rule has a test',
        satisfied=satisfied,
        evidence=(
            'pytest test suite with known-bad and known-good fixtures detected.'
            if satisfied else
            'No automated test suite found for any of the seven tracks.'
        ),
        remediation=(
            'Already satisfied.'
            if satisfied else
            'Create tests/test_tracks.py with a fixture for each track. '
            'Each fixture must include at least one known-bad input (expect FIRE) '
            'and one known-good input (expect PASS). Section 11.02 provides templates.'
        ),
    )

def evaluate_coverage_is_measured(state: GatewayState) -> PrincipleResult:
    satisfied = state.has_attck_coverage_map
    return PrincipleResult(
        principle_id=3,
        name='Coverage is measured, not assumed',
        satisfied=satisfied,
        evidence=(
            'ATT&CK heat map present with per-technique coverage status.'
            if satisfied else
            'No ATT&CK or ATLAS coverage map found; gaps are unknown.'
        ),
        remediation=(
            'Already satisfied.'
            if satisfied else
            'Run attck_coverage_heatmap.py (Section 11.05) to generate '
            'the first coverage map from the seven-track Sigma rules. '
            'Schedule weekly coverage map refresh.'
        ),
    )

def evaluate_fpr_tracked_per_rule(state: GatewayState) -> PrincipleResult:
    satisfied = state.fpr_tracked_per_rule
    return PrincipleResult(
        principle_id=4,
        name='False positive rates tracked per rule',
        satisfied=satisfied,
        evidence=(
            'Per-rule FPR tracking detected in detection operations database.'
            if satisfied else
            'No per-rule FPR tracking; aggregate alert counts only.'
        ),
        remediation=(
            'Already satisfied.'
            if satisfied else
            'Instrument each track''s alert output with a rule_id field. '
            'Pipe alerts to false_positive_manager.py (Section 11.09) '
            'to begin per-rule FPR accumulation.'
        ),
    )

def evaluate_maturity_baseline_defined(state: GatewayState) -> PrincipleResult:
    satisfied = state.maturity_level_defined
    return PrincipleResult(
        principle_id=5,
        name='Detection programme has a maturity level',
        satisfied=satisfied,
        evidence=(
            'Maturity assessment on file with L1-L5 scores per dimension.'
            if satisfied else
            'No maturity assessment on file; level is unknown.'
        ),
        remediation=(
            'Already satisfied.'
            if satisfied else
            'Run detection_maturity_assessor.py (Section 11.08) '
            'to produce the baseline maturity score for the gateway.'
        ),
    )

EVALUATORS: list[Callable[[GatewayState], PrincipleResult]] = [
    evaluate_rules_are_code,
    evaluate_every_rule_has_tests,
    evaluate_coverage_is_measured,
    evaluate_fpr_tracked_per_rule,
    evaluate_maturity_baseline_defined,
]

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Report
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def run_readiness_report(state: GatewayState) -> None:
    results = [fn(state) for fn in EVALUATORS]
    passed = sum(1 for r in results if r.satisfied)
    print(f'\nDetection-as-Code Readiness Report')
    print(f'Gateway: seven-track (post-Part-2 state)')
    print(f'Principles satisfied: {passed}/5\n')
    print(f'{"#":<4} {"Principle":<45} {"Status":<12} Remediation')
    print('-' * 110)
    for r in results:
        status = 'SATISFIED' if r.satisfied else 'DEBT'
        remediation_short = r.remediation[:60] + '...' if len(r.remediation) > 60 else r.remediation
        print(f'{r.principle_id:<4} {r.name:<45} {status:<12} {remediation_short}')
    print()
    if passed < 5:
        print('Detection debt summary:')
        for r in results:
            if not r.satisfied:
                print(f'  P{r.principle_id}: {r.remediation}')

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Entry point
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

if __name__ == '__main__':
    # Post-Part-2 state: nothing satisfied, all five debts active
    gateway = GatewayState(
        has_git_repo=False,
        has_test_suite=False,
        has_ci_pipeline=False,
        has_attck_coverage_map=False,
        fpr_tracked_per_rule=False,
        maturity_level_defined=False,
    )
    run_readiness_report(gateway)

def debt_label(debt: str) -> str:
    labels = {
        'no_tests': 'No automated tests',
        'no_version_history': 'No version history',
        'no_fpr_tracking': 'No per-rule FPR tracking',
        'template_corpus_unversioned': 'Template corpus unversioned',
        'threshold_uncalibrated': 'Threshold uncalibrated',
        'no_fpr_data': 'No FPR data collected',
        'label_baseline_unversioned': 'Label baseline unversioned',
        'composite_threshold_uncalibrated': 'Composite threshold uncalibrated',
        'perplexity_threshold_uncalibrated': 'Perplexity threshold uncalibrated',
        'session_state_unversioned': 'Session state schema unversioned',
        'no_failure_mode_tests': 'No failure mode tests',
        'no_ci_integration': 'No CI/CD pipeline integration',
    }
    return labels.get(debt, debt)

def print_inventory() -> None:
    print('\nPart 2 Detection Artefact Inventory')
    print(f'Total artefacts: {len(INVENTORY)}')
    all_techniques: set[str] = set()
    for a in INVENTORY:
        all_techniques.update(a.atlas_techniques)
    print(f'ATLAS techniques referenced: {len(all_techniques)}')
    print(f'All artefacts initial maturity: L1\n')

    by_track: dict[str, list[DetectionArtefact]] = {}
    for a in INVENTORY:
        by_track.setdefault(a.track, []).append(a)

    for track, artefacts in by_track.items():
        print(f'  {track}')
        for a in artefacts:
            debts = '; '.join(debt_label(d) for d in a.primary_debts)
            print(f'    [{a.artefact_type}] {a.filename}')
            print(f'      ATLAS: {", ".join(a.atlas_techniques)}')
            print(f'      Debt:  {debts}')
            print(f'      Target maturity: {a.target_maturity}')
        print()

    print('Debt category summary:')
    all_debts: dict[str, int] = {}
    for a in INVENTORY:
        for d in a.primary_debts:
            all_debts[d] = all_debts.get(d, 0) + 1
    for debt, count in sorted(all_debts.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1]):
        print(f'  {debt_label(debt)}: {count} artefacts affected')

Common Mistakes

Id

CM11.01.1

Mistake

Treating detection engineering and security engineering as synonyms

Why It Happens

Both disciplines produce security outcomes and many practitioners move between them. The vocabulary overlaps enough that the distinction feels academic.

Why It Matters

They require different skills, different metrics, and different career paths. A security engineer optimising for prevention will under-invest in detection instrumentation. A detection engineer who conflates the two will not know when to escalate a coverage gap to the security architecture team.

Correct Approach

Security engineering: prevents attacks. Detection engineering: observes when prevention fails. Both are necessary; neither substitutes for the other.

Id

CM11.01.2

Mistake

Beginning a detection programme at Layer 3 (ML models) before establishing Layer 1 (signature rules)

Why It Happens

ML-based detection is technically interesting. Practitioners familiar with machine learning default to the most sophisticated tool available.

Why It Matters

ML models require labelled training data that signature rules generate. Building ML-based detection before signature rules means training on synthetic data that does not reflect actual production traffic patterns. The resulting models have poor precision in production.

Correct Approach

Follow the layered detection model in Section 11.03. Signature rules first, then heuristics, then ML. Each layer generates the labelled data the next layer trains on.

Id

CM11.01.3

Mistake

Declaring the seven-track gateway sufficient and skipping Chapter 11

Why It Happens

Seven tracks sounds comprehensive. The gateway blocks attacks. It feels complete.

Why It Matters

L1 maturity with no tests, no coverage map, and no FPR tracking will produce alert fatigue within weeks of production deployment. The gateway as built cannot be maintained at scale.

Correct Approach

Chapter 11 is not optional polish on a working gateway. It is the governance layer without which the gateway degrades rather than improves over time.

Id

CM11.01.4

Mistake

Treating all five detection debt categories as equally urgent

Why It Happens

A complete list of problems looks like a flat to-do list.

Why It Matters

The debts compound. Rules without tests cannot safely have their thresholds calibrated (calibration might break the rule without CI catching it). Coverage without measurement cannot be improved systematically. The order matters.

Correct Approach

Fix in order: (1) version control, (2) tests, (3) coverage measurement, (4) threshold calibration, (5) maturity baseline. Each step enables the next.

Id

CM11.01.5

Mistake

Importing ATLAS technique IDs from public sources without verifying they match the actual attack observed

Why It Happens

ATLAS technique lists in blog posts and threat intelligence feeds are a convenient shortcut.

Why It Matters

An incorrect ATLAS technique mapping produces a coverage map that shows false coverage. A heat map that claims AML.T0029 is covered when the rule actually fires on a different signal pattern gives a false sense of security to leadership and analysts.

Correct Approach

Map ATLAS techniques from mechanism, not from label. Read the technique description, confirm that your detection rule actually fires on the behaviour the technique describes, then add the mapping.

Section Summary

What You Learned

Detection engineering is a discipline distinct from security engineering. It requires attacker knowledge as its prerequisite — you cannot detect what you do not understand at the mechanism level. Part 2 produced seven gateway tracks and a collection of Sigma rules and Python detectors, all at L1 maturity with five identifiable categories of detection debt: no tests, uncalibrated thresholds, unmeasured coverage, no version history, and no maturity baseline. Chapter 11 is the methodology layer that addresses all five debts and establishes the detection-as-code framework that governs Part 3. Section 11.02 begins with the first debt: version control and automated testing.

Key Terms

Term

Detection engineering

Definition

The discipline of designing, building, testing, and operating detection systems — rules, models, workflows — that surface evidence of attacks that bypassed preventive controls.

Term

Detection-as-code (DaC)

Definition

The principle that detection rules are software artefacts governed by version control, code review, automated testing, and managed deployment pipelines.

Term

Detection debt

Definition

Accumulated shortfall in detection programme rigour — untested rules, uncalibrated thresholds, unmeasured coverage — that manifests as false positives and missed attacks.

Term

Attack-detection symmetry

Definition

The principle that every attack family has a corresponding detection primitive, and that the quality of the detection primitive is bounded by the depth of attacker knowledge.

Term

L1 maturity

Definition

The first level of the detection maturity model: a detection artefact exists but has no automated tests, no version history, and no performance metrics. All Part 2 gateway tracks start at L1.
