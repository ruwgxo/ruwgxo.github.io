Chapter 10 · Section 10 · ~6 pages

Real-World Incident: Adversarial Examples in Production

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Four documented adversarial example incidents across physical vision, facial recognition, content moderation, and malware detection share one root cause: classifiers deployed without adversarial robustness controls.

Why This Section Exists

Section 10.10 mirrors the role Section 9.9 played in Chapter 9: it grounds the attack catalogue in documented production reality before the detection and defence sections build the controls that would have prevented each incident. The attacks in Sections 10.3-10.9 are not theoretical capabilities waiting for production adversaries to discover -- they are documented incidents that have already occurred against commercially deployed systems across four distinct security domains. The shared root cause across all four is the same: no adversarial input detection, no adversarial training, and no consistency checking. Every control built in Sections 10.11-10.15 is directly motivated by a specific gap in one of these documented incidents.

Position In Chapter

Section 10.10 is the bridge between the attack catalogue (Sections 10.3-10.9) and the defence architecture (Sections 10.11-10.15). It establishes that the threat is real before the defences are built. The detection opportunity mapping (deliverable 3) provides the direct link: for each incident, which Track 7 primitive would have fired.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

From Academic Papers to Production Systems

Body

The adversarial ML research field moved rapidly from demonstrating attacks in controlled settings (Szegedy et al. 2013, Goodfellow et al. 2014) to demonstrating attacks against production-grade deployed systems. The transition matters because it shifts the threat from "this is possible in principle" to "this has already happened to systems like yours." Section 10.10 documents four incidents where adversarial example techniques from the academic literature were demonstrated against commercially deployed or commercially representative systems. The incidents span four security domains: physical vision systems (autonomous vehicle stop sign detection), biometric access control (facial recognition), content moderation (LLM and text safety classifiers), and classical malware detection (antivirus PE binary classifiers). The diversity of domains is itself the key lesson: adversarial ML is not a risk specific to large language models or to image classifiers -- it applies wherever a learned classifier operates on inputs that an adversary controls. Each incident is analysed using the same four-category root cause framework: missing adversarial training, missing input-side detection, missing prediction consistency checking, and missing complementary non-ML controls. Across all four incidents, the same categories are absent. The controls built in Sections 10.11-10.15 directly address each root cause. The detection opportunity mapping in deliverable 3 of this section shows precisely which Track 7 primitive would have detected each incident, at what stage in the inference pipeline, and with what probability.

Part 2

Title

Incident 1: Stop Sign Attack Against Autonomous Vehicle Detectors

Body

Eykholt, Evtimov, Fernandes, Li, Rahmati, Xiao, Prakash, Kohno, and Song (2018) demonstrated in "Robust Physical Perturbations in the Real World" that black-and-white stickers placed on stop signs caused YOLO v2 and Faster RCNN detectors to misclassify stop signs as 45 mph speed limit signs at distances up to 30 metres under varied lighting and viewing angles. The attack used the Expectation over Transformation (EOT) optimisation introduced in Section 10.8. The attack mechanism: a stop sign with stickers covering approximately 2% of its visible surface caused YOLO misclassification rates of 100% in poster-board conditions and 84.8% in drive-by tests at posted speed. The stickers were camouflaged as graffiti -- visually inconspicuous to human drivers. No API access was required: the attack operated entirely in the physical environment before any digital processing. Root cause analysis. The deployed detectors had no adversarial training on patch-augmented images: the training distribution contained no examples of stop signs with adversarially optimised sticker patterns. There was no input-side anomaly detection for the camera pipeline: no mechanism to flag regions of the captured image with texture statistics inconsistent with natural sign surfaces. There was no multi-sensor redundancy: the system relied on a single optical camera without supplemental LIDAR or radar confirmation. There was no temporal consistency requirement: a sign that was classified inconsistently across multiple frames was not flagged. Detection opportunity: the patch detector from Section 10.8 computes texture anomaly scores for localised image regions. The sticker pattern has spatial frequency content inconsistent with the natural sign surface -- the anomaly score would flag the sticker region before the object classifier produces its output. Temporal consistency checking (the same sign should classify consistently across three consecutive frames) would reduce misclassification rate to near zero at the cost of one additional frame of latency.

Part 3

Title

Incident 2: Adversarial Glasses Bypassing Facial Recognition

Body

Sharif, Bhagavatula, Bauer, and Reiter (2016) demonstrated in "Accessorize to a Crime: Real and Stealthy Attacks on State-of-the-Art Face Recognition" that printed adversarial patterns on eyeglass frames could cause commercial face recognition systems to misidentify the wearer as a different target identity with 100% targeted attack success in lab conditions, degrading to 88% in physical wear conditions with lighting variation. The attack mechanism: the printed pattern on the glasses frames was optimised against a face recognition system using gradient descent on the classification loss. Wearing the printed glasses caused the system to output a target identity (an impersonation attack) or to fail to recognise the wearer (a dodging attack) without raising any liveness or anomaly alerts. The physical deployment required no API access: the glasses were worn in the normal access control scenario. Komkov and Petiushko (2021) extended this to hat-based adversarial patches against person re-identification models, demonstrating that the attack surface extends to any wearable item in the camera''s field of view. Root cause analysis. The deployed system had no adversarial training on face images with adversarial patch accessories -- the training distribution contained real glasses frames and natural accessories, not EOT-optimised adversarial patterns. There was no spatial anomaly detection for the face region: no mechanism to flag glasses frames with unusual texture frequency content. There was no multi-modal verification: infrared or 3D depth cameras would be unaffected by optical adversarial patterns designed for RGB classifiers. Detection opportunity: the spatial anomaly detector from Section 10.8 applied to the glasses/accessories region of the face image would flag the adversarial pattern texture. A paired RGB + infrared capture where the face is classified consistently under both modalities would resist any attack optimised for a single spectral range.

Part 4

Title

Incident 3: Content Moderation Evasion via Adversarial Text

Body

Jain, Schwarzschild, Wen, Kagklis, Wang, Goldblum, and Goldstein (2023), and separately Zou, Wang, Kolter, and Fredrikson (2023), demonstrated universal adversarial suffixes that bypass LLM safety alignment and content moderation classifiers. Zou et al. found that fixed adversarial suffixes appended to harmful prompts caused aligned LLMs (GPT-4, Claude, Llama 2) to produce content that their safety training was designed to prevent. The suffixes were found via an automated optimisation process analogous to the universal trigger search in Section 10.9. The attack mechanism: an adversarial suffix of approximately 20 tokens, appended to a harmful user prompt, caused the model to begin its response with an affirmative pattern that bypassed the safety classifier''s detection threshold. The suffix was transferable across models: a suffix found against one model transferred to others at significant rates, indicating shared structural vulnerabilities in safety classifier training. Earlier work by Garg and Ramakrishnan (2020) demonstrated that BERT-based hate speech and sentiment classifiers could be evaded at 70-88% rates using TextFooler-style synonym substitution, with adversarial examples that were rated as natural by human evaluators at 79% agreement rates. Root cause analysis. The safety classifiers in deployed LLMs had no adversarial training against suffix-based attacks: the RLHF safety training optimised against human red-team prompts and natural harmful content, not against gradient-optimised adversarial suffixes. There was no semantic consistency checking: the same request paraphrased without the adversarial suffix would be classified as harmful, but the system did not run consistency checks before responding. There was no per-request adversarial suffix detection. Detection opportunity: the semantic consistency check from Section 10.12 extended to text (back-translation or paraphrase with BERT) would catch adversarial suffix attacks -- the harmful prompt without the suffix classifies differently from the harmful prompt with the suffix, and the check flags this inconsistency. Universal adversarial triggers that are removed or modified by paraphrase lose their adversarial property.

Part 5

Title

Incident 4: Adversarial Malware Evading Antivirus ML Classifiers

Body

Grosse, Papernot, Manoharan, Backes, and McDaniel (2017) demonstrated adversarial perturbations to Android malware feature vectors that caused an ML-based malware classifier to misclassify malicious applications as benign. The attack operated in feature space (modifying Android manifest features) under an L0 constraint: only features that could be added to the malware without removing its core malicious functionality were modified. Demetrio, Biggio, Lagorio, Roli, and Armando (2021) demonstrated the GAMMA attack against PE (Portable Executable) Windows binary classifiers: adversarial perturbations to PE sections (appending benign content to the overlay section, modifying import table entries) that caused MalConv and other gradient-based PE classifiers to produce benign classifications while preserving the binary''s full malicious execution behaviour. The attack operated under a functionality-preserving L0 constraint -- only byte regions that do not affect execution were modified. Root cause analysis. The deployed antivirus ML classifiers had no adversarial training on functionality-preserving adversarial PE binaries: the training distribution was clean malware and benign software samples, not adversarially perturbed malware. There was no feature consistency checking: the same binary analysed with a static feature squeezer (feature normalisation, section stripping) would likely still be classified as malicious, but no such check was applied. There was no ensemble classification: agreement across multiple independent classifiers with different feature representations would reduce the adversarial example success rate. Detection opportunity: the feature squeezing analogue for structured data (normalise PE section content, strip overlay content, hash-normalise import table) would reduce the adversarial perturbation surface. Consistency checking across multiple PE classifiers with different feature representations (byte n-gram, API call sequence, control flow) catches adversarial perturbations that fool one feature representation but not others.

Part 6

Title

Root Cause Synthesis: The Four Missing Controls

Body

Across all four incidents, the root cause analysis produces the same four missing control categories. This convergence is not coincidental -- it reflects the fundamental structure of the adversarial ML defence problem. Missing control 1 -- Adversarial training: no incident system had been trained on adversarial examples. All four systems were trained on natural data distributions without adversarially augmented examples. Adversarial training (Section 10.15) directly addresses this gap: the inner training loop generates adversarial examples and the model is trained to classify them correctly, producing models with empirically higher robustness to the attack families represented in training. Missing control 2 -- Input-side adversarial detection: none of the four systems had a pre-inference stage that detected adversarial perturbations before the classifier saw the input. Feature squeezing (Section 10.11), patch localisation (Section 10.8), and Unicode anomaly detection (Section 10.9) are the input-side primitives. They operate before model inference and reduce the adversarial attack success rate independently of model robustness. Missing control 3 -- Prediction consistency checking: none of the four systems verified that the classifier''s output was stable under small input variations (digital) or under paraphrase/back-translation (text). Consistency checking (Section 10.12) catches adversarial examples that are fragile -- those that exist near the decision boundary and lose their adversarial property under small input perturbation. Missing control 4 -- Non-ML complementary controls: three of the four incidents (all physical and malware) were exploited partly because the deployed system had no non-ML complementary control. Multi-sensor fusion (LIDAR + optical for autonomous vehicles), infrared imaging (facial recognition), and multi-classifier ensemble agreement (malware detection) would all have reduced incident success rates. The adversary optimised against a single classifier; a two-layer system with independent failure modes is significantly harder to attack simultaneously.

Part 7

Title

Operator Response Patterns and Production Lessons

Body

Operator responses to adversarial ML incidents have followed three patterns, with varying effectiveness. Model-only response: retrain the model on adversarial examples from the specific incident attack type. This is the most common first response and the least durable: the retrained model is more robust to the specific attack variant that was published, but an adversary who encounters the updated model will adapt to a different attack variant. The stop sign attack and adversarial glasses papers were followed by new adversarial examples that bypassed the improved models. Detection layer addition: add an input-side anomaly detector (texture analysis for physical patches, Unicode filtering for text attacks) as a pre-inference stage. This is more durable than model-only response because it operates independently of the model''s decision boundary. Even if the adversary crafts a new attack variant, the detection layer does not need to be retrained for the specific attack -- it detects the structural properties (spatial texture anomaly, non-ASCII characters) that all variants share. Architecture change: replace single-sensor or single-classifier design with multi-sensor or multi-classifier ensemble. This is the most durable response because it changes the attack surface fundamentally: an adversary who optimised against one component must now optimise against multiple independent components simultaneously. The automotive industry''s adoption of multi-sensor fusion (LIDAR + radar + camera) after the adversarial stop sign research is an example of this response. The production lesson: incident response should add detection layers and architecture controls, not only update models. Model updates address the specific attack; detection layers and architecture changes address the attack class. A pre-production adversarial robustness checklist (deliverable 5) that requires Track 7 detection coverage before deployment would have prevented all four incidents in this section.

Root cause analysis framework for adversarial incidents using four missing control categories; generates structured RCA documents for security reporting and architecture review

# incident_root_cause_analyser.py
# Section 10.10: Adversarial Incident Root Cause Analysis
# 20260524 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import List, Dict
from adversarial_incident_timeline import INCIDENT_TIMELINE, AdversarialIncident


CONTROL_CATEGORIES = [
    'adversarial_training',
    'input_side_detection',
    'consistency_checking',
    'complementary_nonml_control',
]

CONTROL_LABELS = {
    'adversarial_training': 'Adversarial training (Section 10.15)',
    'input_side_detection': 'Input-side adversarial detection (Section 10.11)',
    'consistency_checking': 'Prediction consistency checking (Section 10.12)',
    'complementary_nonml_control': 'Non-ML complementary controls (multi-sensor, ensemble)',
}


@dataclass
class RootCauseReport:
    incident_id: str
    title: str
    missing_controls: List[str]
    contributing_factors: List[str]
    severity: str
    remediation_priority: str
    recommended_controls: List[str]


def classify_root_causes(incident: AdversarialIncident) -> Dict[str, bool]:
    '''Classify which control categories are missing for this incident.'''
    missing = {cat: False for cat in CONTROL_CATEGORIES}
    for cause in incident.root_causes:
        cause_lower = cause.lower()
        if 'adversarial training' in cause_lower:
            missing['adversarial_training'] = True
        if 'detection' in cause_lower or 'anomaly' in cause_lower or 'squeezing' in cause_lower:
            missing['input_side_detection'] = True
        if 'consistency' in cause_lower or 'temporal' in cause_lower or 'ensemble' in cause_lower:
            missing['consistency_checking'] = True
        if 'sensor' in cause_lower or 'liveness' in cause_lower or 'dynamic' in cause_lower or 'complementary' in cause_lower:
            missing['complementary_nonml_control'] = True
    return missing


def generate_rca(incident: AdversarialIncident) -> RootCauseReport:
    missing = classify_root_causes(incident)
    n_missing = sum(missing.values())
    severity = 'CRITICAL' if n_missing >= 3 else ('HIGH' if n_missing == 2 else 'MEDIUM')
    remediation_priority = 'Immediate' if not incident.api_required else 'High'
    recommended = [CONTROL_LABELS[cat] for cat, absent in missing.items() if absent]

    return RootCauseReport(
        incident_id=incident.incident_id,
        title=incident.title,
        missing_controls=[
            CONTROL_LABELS[cat] for cat, absent in missing.items() if absent
        ],
        contributing_factors=incident.root_causes,
        severity=severity,
        remediation_priority=remediation_priority,
        recommended_controls=recommended,
    )


def print_rca(rca: RootCauseReport) -> None:
    print(f'\n{"=" * 60}')
    print(f'[{rca.incident_id}] {rca.title}')
    print(f'Severity: {rca.severity}  |  Priority: {rca.remediation_priority}')
    print(f'\nMissing controls ({len(rca.missing_controls)}):')
    for ctrl in rca.missing_controls:
        print(f'  - {ctrl}')
    print(f'\nContributing factors:')
    for factor in rca.contributing_factors:
        print(f'  - {factor}')
    print(f'\nRecommended controls:')
    for rec in rca.recommended_controls:
        print(f'  [ADD] {rec}')


def cross_incident_pattern(incidents: List[AdversarialIncident]) -> None:
    print('\nCross-incident pattern analysis:')
    all_missing = {cat: 0 for cat in CONTROL_CATEGORIES}
    for inc in incidents:
        missing = classify_root_causes(inc)
        for cat, absent in missing.items():
            if absent:
                all_missing[cat] += 1

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Treating adversarial ML incident papers as theoretical demonstrations rather than as operational threat signals requiring production response

Why It Happens

Adversarial ML papers are published in academic venues (CVPR, CCS, USENIX). Security engineers in operations-focused roles may not monitor academic venues, or may discount academic demonstrations as "lab conditions" not applicable to production. The stop sign paper was published in 2018; autonomous vehicle security teams that read it as theoretical were behind the threat curve for years.

Consequence

Production classifiers remain undefended against documented attack families. The attack is already known to the adversary community; the defender has the information needed to implement Track 7 controls but does not act on it. The gap between attack publication and production defence widens with each incident not acted upon.

Correct Approach

Establish a process to review adversarial ML papers (arXiv cs.CR and cs.LG, major security conferences, MITRE ATLAS case study additions) within 30 days of publication. For each paper: does the attack apply to any deployed classifier? If yes, trigger the pre-production checklist (deliverable 5) for that classifier. The adversarial incident register (ADV-007) formalises this process.

Mistake 2

Description

Scoping adversarial ML defence only to LLM and image classification deployments, missing classical security domains (malware detection, fraud scoring, anomaly detection)

Why It Happens

Adversarial ML discourse is dominated by LLM safety and image classifier examples. Security teams responsible for malware detection, fraud scoring, or network anomaly detection classify their systems as "traditional ML" rather than "AI security" and do not apply adversarial robustness controls.

Consequence

Malware classifiers (GAMMA incident, INC-004), fraud models, and intrusion detection systems are all gradient-based learners with the same structural vulnerability to adversarial perturbation as image classifiers. They are not protected by adversarial training, input-side detection, or consistency checking. An adversary who discovers that a fraud model is vulnerable to feature-space adversarial perturbation (modify transaction features within domain constraints) can evade it systematically.

Correct Approach

Apply the pre-production adversarial robustness checklist (deliverable 5) to any ML classifier where the adversary can control the input -- including malware samples, transaction features, and network telemetry. The checklist items are domain-agnostic: adversarial training, feature squeezing analogue, consistency checking analogue, and non-ML complementary control apply to all domains.

Mistake 3

Description

Responding to an adversarial ML incident with a model update only, without adding a detection layer, leaving the system vulnerable to the next attack variant

Why It Happens

Model updates are the familiar incident response mechanism. After an adversarial incident, retraining on adversarial examples is the natural first action. It is faster than architectural changes and measurably improves robustness against the specific attack that was demonstrated.

Consequence

The updated model is more robust to the specific attack variant published in the incident paper. A new attack variant (higher kappa, different norm, adaptive adversary with knowledge of the updated model) may achieve comparable evasion rates. Without a detection layer (Track 7), the updated model has no defence against unknown future variants. The pattern repeats: incident → model update → new variant → incident.

Correct Approach

Model updates address the specific attack; detection layers address the attack class. The correct incident response adds: (1) adversarial training on the specific attack, (2) Track 7 detection layer covering the attack family, and (3) architecture controls (multi-sensor, ensemble) that are attack-variant-independent. Only the combination of all three provides durable defence rather than temporary patch coverage.

Mistake 4

Description

Calibrating the detection layer only against the specific attack from the incident report, missing related attack families in the same taxonomy cell

Why It Happens

The detection layer is built and validated against the incident attack (e.g., EOT patch detection calibrated against the specific Eykholt et al. sticker pattern). The detector is tuned to that specific pattern and declared operational.

Consequence

A new physical patch design (different texture, different colour profile, different size) evades the detector calibrated against the original sticker pattern. The detection coverage is specific to the published attack, not to the attack class (all adversarial patches). The adversary simply designs a different patch that falls outside the calibrated detection region.

Correct Approach

Calibrate detection against the attack class, not the specific attack instance. For patch detection: calibrate against patches of varied size, texture, and colour using the EOT transformation sweep from Section 10.8. For text adversarial detection: calibrate against TextFooler, HotFlip, and universal triggers (not just the GCG suffix). The physical_robustness_benchmark.py (Section 10.8) and cw_confidence_sweep.py (Section 10.5) provide the right calibration frameworks.

Mistake 5

Description

Not maintaining a post-deployment adversarial red-team schedule, allowing detection coverage to drift as new attack variants emerge after initial Track 7 deployment

Why It Happens

Track 7 is deployed at launch, passing all pre-production checklist items. The detection layer is treated as a static control that does not require ongoing maintenance. New adversarial attack papers accumulate without triggering a re-evaluation of Track 7 coverage.

Consequence

Track 7 coverage drifts: the adversarial classifier (Section 10.12) trained on attack families at deployment time degrades on novel attack families. The squeezer thresholds calibrated at deployment epsilon become miscalibrated as the production threat changes. Detection rates that were 85% at launch drift to 60% after 18 months of new attack publications without re-evaluation.

Correct Approach

Schedule quarterly adversarial red-team exercises: run the full detection opportunity mapping (deliverable 3) against newly published attacks, retrain the adversarial classifier if new attack families are not covered, and re-run the pre-production checklist annually. The adversarial incident register (ADV-007) tracks this cadence. Adversarial robustness is not a one-time deployment property -- it is an ongoing security posture that requires continuous maintenance.

Section Summary

Compact

Four documented adversarial incidents span physical vision (stop sign attack, adversarial glasses), text safety classifiers (LLM adversarial suffix), and malware detection (GAMMA PE attack). All four share the same root cause: no adversarial training, no input-side adversarial detection, no consistency checking, and no non-ML complementary controls. Every control built in Sections 10.11-10.15 directly addresses a specific root cause in one of these incidents. The detection opportunity mapping shows which Track 7 primitive would have fired: patch localisation for physical attacks, semantic consistency for text attacks, structured feature squeezing for malware. The correct incident response adds all three control layers -- model update, detection layer, architecture control -- not model update alone. The pre-production checklist (eight items, six blocking) operationalises this across any ML classifier deployment.

  • Adversarial ML is a production threat across four domains: physical vision, biometric access, text safety, and malware detection -- not only LLM
  • All four incidents share four missing controls: adversarial training, input-side detection, consistency checking, non-ML complementary control
  • Model-only incident response is insufficient: it addresses the specific attack but not the attack class; detection layers provide durable coverage

Title

Section 10.11: Detection Primitives -- Input Preprocessing and Feature Squeezing

Summary

Section 10.11 builds the first of three Track 7 detection primitives: feature squeezing. The detection mechanism is prediction-change-under-transformation: compare the model''s output on the original input versus a squeezed version. A large prediction change signals adversarial perturbation -- natural images are robust to small input transformations; adversarial perturbations are precisely calibrated and destroyed by transformation. Three squeezers are implemented: Gaussian spatial smoothing (effective against L-infinity digital noise), bit-depth reduction (reduces precision of gradient-based perturbations), and JPEG compression (lossy compression as adversarial signal destruction). A squeezer ensemble with disagreement scoring combines all three. The text canonicaliser (synonym normalisation and paraphrase detection) implements the squeezer analogue for LLM adversarial inputs. Section 10.11 closes with the squeezer latency benchmark that establishes how many squeezers fit within Track 7''s pre-inference latency allocation of the 50ms P99 budget.
