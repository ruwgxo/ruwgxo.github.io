Drift Detection: Data Drift and Concept Drift Beneath the Detectors

Section Overview

One Line Summary

The Chapter 12 detectors decay silently as the data and attack distributions shift; Section 13.05 turns the temporal lens on the detectors and the model themselves, building the population stability index, distribution tests, and prediction-distribution monitoring that make that decay visible and feed the retraining trigger.

Why This Section Exists

Every detector in the previous four sections watches the user. Section 13.05 watches the detectors. This is a different kind of monitoring with a different target, and it exists because a trained detector is not a permanent asset. The Chapter 12 classifiers were trained on a snapshot of inputs and attacks, and the world moves: input distributions shift as the product and its users change, and attack distributions shift as adversaries invent new techniques. A detector trained on last quarter's data degrades against this quarter's reality, and it does so silently -- its accuracy falls with no error message, no exception, no alert. The detector keeps returning confident scores; they are just increasingly wrong. Drift detection is the alarm on that silent decay. It is the monitoring that turns a degrading detector from an invisible liability into a visible, actionable alert. Without it, the only way to discover that a detector has decayed is to notice that attacks are getting through, which is discovering the failure by suffering its consequence. With it, the distribution shift that causes the decay is detected before the accuracy loss is large, and the retraining loop from Chapter 12 Section 12.14 is triggered to refresh the detector. The section also draws the distinction that governs the response: data drift versus concept drift, and benign versus adversarial drift. Data drift is the inputs moving; concept drift is the meaning of the inputs moving, which is more dangerous because it degrades the detector with no input-side signal. Benign drift should adapt the model; adversarial drift should alert the security team. Conflating these either floods analysts with seasonal noise or misses the attack- distribution shift that signals a new campaign.

Position In Chapter

Section 13.05 completes the temporal family of Chapter 13 by applying temporal distributional comparison to the detectors and the model rather than to user behaviour. It builds on the distribution and baseline concepts of 13.03 and the temporal framing of 13.04, and it closes the loop back to Chapter 12: its drift alerts feed the Section 12.14 live dataset curation and retraining trigger. It is distinct from the user-facing temporal detector of 13.04 in its target -- the health of the detection system itself -- and it is the section that makes the whole Chapter 12 detection layer a maintained system rather than a decaying one.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

The Trained Detector Is a Decaying Asset

Body

A classifier is trained once on a dataset and then deployed to score live traffic indefinitely. The training assumes the live traffic resembles the training data: that the inputs come from the same distribution and that the relationship between inputs and labels is the same. Both assumptions decay over time, and the detector decays with them. The decay is silent, which is what makes it dangerous. A classifier that has drifted does not raise an exception or return an error. It returns a confident score for every input, exactly as it did on day one. The scores are simply increasingly miscalibrated: attacks that would have scored high on the training distribution now score low because they no longer resemble the training attacks, and the detector passes them with confidence. From the outside, the detection pipeline looks healthy -- it is processing traffic, returning scores, and blocking the attacks it still recognises. The attacks it no longer recognises pass invisibly. Without drift monitoring, there is exactly one way to discover that a detector has decayed: notice that attacks are getting through that should have been caught. That is discovering the failure by suffering its consequence, after the damage, with no warning. Drift detection replaces this with an early signal. It monitors the distributions the detector depends on -- the input features and the output scores -- and alarms when they shift away from the training reference, before the accuracy loss has grown large enough to be noticed through successful attacks. The drift alarm is the difference between maintaining the detector on a schedule driven by measured decay and discovering the decay through a breach.

Part 2

Title

Data Drift and Concept Drift: Two Different Failures

Body

Drift comes in two kinds that must be distinguished because they have different signatures and different consequences. Data drift is a change in the input distribution. The features the detector sees move away from the distribution it was trained on. Prompt lengths grow because users adopt a new interaction style; the vocabulary shifts because a new domain of usage appears; the mix of endpoints changes because the product launches a new feature. The relationship between inputs and the correct label is unchanged -- an injection is still an injection -- but the inputs now occupy a region of feature space the detector saw little of in training, so its scores there are unreliable. Data drift is detectable on the input side: the feature distributions themselves have moved, and comparing the live feature distribution to the training distribution reveals the shift. Concept drift is a change in the relationship between inputs and labels. The feature distribution may be unchanged, but what the features mean has shifted: a pattern that was benign in training is now part of an attack, or an attack technique now presents through features that used to indicate benign traffic. The classic security example is a new attack technique: the adversary finds a phrasing that the detector scores as benign because no such attack existed in training, so the mapping from that feature region to the label has changed even though the feature region itself is not new. Concept drift is the more dangerous of the two because it can degrade the detector with no input-side signal: the inputs look familiar, the input-distribution monitors see nothing, and yet the detector is wrong because the meaning of those inputs has changed. Concept drift is detectable only by watching the relationship -- the detector's accuracy on labelled data, or the shift in its score distribution that signals its decisions are changing -- not by watching the inputs alone.

Part 3

Title

Population Stability Index: Measuring Input Distribution Shift

Body

The workhorse metric for data drift is the population stability index. It measures how much a feature's distribution has shifted from a reference distribution to a live distribution, by binning both into the same buckets and summing a per-bucket term that grows as the live proportion in a bucket diverges from the reference proportion. The result is a single number per feature: near zero when the live distribution matches the reference, growing as they diverge. The index has conventional interpretation bands that make it operationally convenient. A small value indicates no material shift and needs no action. A moderate value indicates a shift worth monitoring -- the feature has moved enough to watch but not enough to act. A large value indicates a significant shift that warrants investigation and likely retraining. These bands turn a continuous metric into a three-state signal that routes directly to a response, and they are stable enough across domains to be a reasonable default before per-feature tuning. The population stability index is computed per feature against a reference window, and the reference window choice is a design decision with the same character as the baseline window of 13.03. The reference should represent the distribution the detector was trained on or last validated against, so that drift is measured relative to the detector's actual operating assumption, not relative to an arbitrary recent window. Computing the index across all of a detector's input features produces a per-feature drift profile that localises the shift: it shows not just that the inputs drifted but which features drifted, which is the first input to deciding whether the drift is benign or adversarial and which retraining data is needed.

Part 4

Title

Distribution Tests: KS and Chi-Squared

Body

The population stability index gives a single interpretable number, but a complementary and more statistically grounded approach is to test directly whether the live and reference distributions differ, using a hypothesis test. The test depends on the feature type. For continuous features -- prompt length, embedding-norm, request rate -- the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test compares the live and reference samples by their cumulative distributions, returning a statistic that measures the largest gap between the two cumulative curves and a p-value for the null hypothesis that the two samples come from the same distribution. A small p-value rejects that null: the distributions differ, the feature has drifted. The KS test is non-parametric, making no assumption about the shape of the distribution, which suits behavioural features whose distributions are not normal. For categorical features -- endpoint, request type, language -- the chi-squared test compares the observed live category counts against the expected counts under the reference distribution, returning a statistic and a p-value for the null that the category proportions are unchanged. A small p-value indicates the category mix has shifted: a new endpoint dominating, a language appearing that was rare in training. The tests and the population stability index are used together rather than as alternatives. The index gives a continuous, interpretable magnitude that routes to the three-state response; the hypothesis tests give a principled significance signal that guards against calling drift on a shift that is within sampling noise. A practical drift monitor reports both per feature: the index magnitude for routing and the test p-value for significance, flagging a feature as drifted when the index is in the action band and the test is significant, which avoids both the index's lack of a significance notion and the test's lack of an interpretable magnitude.

Part 5

Title

Prediction-Distribution Monitoring: Catching Concept Drift

Body

Input-side monitoring -- the population stability index and the distribution tests on input features -- detects data drift but is blind to concept drift, because concept drift can occur with no input-distribution shift. To catch the decay that input monitoring misses, the monitor must watch the detector's outputs, not just its inputs. Prediction-distribution monitoring tracks the distribution of the classifier's output scores over time and compares it against a reference score distribution from when the detector was known to be accurate. The reasoning is that a detector making consistent decisions on a stable population produces a stable score distribution: a characteristic proportion of high scores, low scores, and borderline scores. When that distribution shifts -- the proportion of high scores collapses, or the scores pile up at the boundary -- the detector's decisions are changing, which is a signal that something about the relationship it learned no longer holds. A collapse in the high-score proportion is a particularly important signal in security: it can mean the attack technique has shifted so the detector no longer recognises the attacks, scoring them low -- exactly the concept drift that produces silent failure. Prediction-distribution monitoring is powerful precisely because it requires no labels. The gold standard for detecting concept drift is to measure the detector's accuracy on freshly labelled live data, but labels are expensive and slow, and waiting for them means waiting while the detector is already decayed. The score distribution is available immediately on every prediction, with no labelling. It is a leading indicator: a shift in the score distribution precedes the confirmation of accuracy loss that labelled data would provide, giving the early warning that is the whole point of drift detection. It is not a substitute for labelled validation -- a score-distribution shift can have benign causes too -- but it is the immediate, label-free signal that triggers the more expensive labelled investigation.

Part 6

Title

Closing the Loop: Retraining Triggers and Benign-versus-Adversarial Routing

Body

A drift alarm is not the end of the process; it is the start of a response, and the response depends on what kind of drift it is. The drift monitor feeds two distinct downstream paths, and routing to the correct one is the final job of this section. Benign drift -- the input distribution moving because the product changed, a seasonal pattern, a new legitimate usage mode -- calls for adaptation. The relationship the detector learned is still valid; it simply needs to be refreshed on data that includes the new distribution. This routes to the Chapter 12 Section 12.14 retraining trigger: the drift alarm signals that the live dataset has moved enough that the detector should be retrained on a corpus that includes the new distribution, and the retrained detector is validated and promoted through the model registry. The trigger must have a cooldown and a confirmation step -- a retrain is expensive, and a false drift alarm that triggers an unnecessary retrain wastes resources and risks promoting a model trained on a transient shift -- so the trigger fires only on confirmed, sustained drift, not a single window's noise. Adversarial drift -- the attack distribution shifting because an adversary has introduced a new technique -- calls for a security response, not just a retrain. A collapse in the high-score proportion that coincides with attacks getting through is not a benign distribution shift to adapt to; it is a signal that a new campaign is underway, and it routes to the security team as an alert, in addition to feeding the retraining corpus with the newly discovered attack examples. Conflating the two is the failure to avoid in both directions: treating adversarial drift as benign means quietly retraining on an attack and normalising it, while treating benign drift as adversarial floods the security team with seasonal noise. The monitor distinguishes them by correlating the drift signal with the other behavioural detectors -- a drift that coincides with rising anomaly scores, blocked-traffic rates, or coordinated-network signals is adversarial; a drift that coincides with a product launch or a seasonal boundary is benign -- and routes each to adaptation or alert accordingly. This routing makes drift detection both the maintenance alarm that keeps the Chapter 12 detectors current and a detection signal in its own right for the campaign that announces itself as a shift in the attack distribution.

Population Stability Index and Distribution Drift Detector

Filename

drift_detector.py

Description

Computes the population stability index per feature against a reference window and runs distribution tests, reporting a per-feature drift profile with magnitude and interpretation band. Implements the PSI and input-side drift detection from Section 13.05 Parts 3 and 4.

Implementation Notes

Code

# drift_detector.py
# Section 13.05: Population Stability Index Drift Detector
# 20260621 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 13 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import math
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Dict, List, Tuple


def _bin_edges(reference: List[float], n_bins: int) -> List[float]:
    s = sorted(reference)
    if not s:
        return [0.0, 1.0]
    edges = [s[0]]
    for i in range(1, n_bins):
        idx = int(i * len(s) / n_bins)
        edges.append(s[min(idx, len(s) - 1)])
    edges.append(s[-1])
    # ensure strictly increasing edges
    uniq = [edges[0]]
    for e in edges[1:]:
        if e > uniq[-1]:
            uniq.append(e)
    if len(uniq) < 2:
        uniq = [s[0], s[0] + 1.0]
    return uniq


def _proportions(values: List[float], edges: List[float]) -> List[float]:
    counts = [0] * (len(edges) - 1)
    for v in values:
        placed = False
        for b in range(len(edges) - 1):
            if edges[b] <= v <= edges[b + 1]:
                counts[b] += 1
                placed = True
                break
        if not placed:
            counts[-1] += 1
    total = sum(counts) or 1
    # smoothing to avoid zero proportions
    return [(c + 0.5) / (total + 0.5 * len(counts)) for c in counts]


def population_stability_index(reference: List[float], live: List[float], n_bins: int = 10) -> float:
    edges = _bin_edges(reference, n_bins)
    ref_p = _proportions(reference, edges)
    live_p = _proportions(live, edges)
    psi = 0.0
    for r, l in zip(ref_p, live_p):
        psi += (l - r) * math.log(l / r)
    return psi


def interpret_psi(psi: float) -> str:
    if psi < 0.1:
        return 'no material shift'
    if psi < 0.25:
        return 'shift worth watching'
    return 'significant shift -- investigate/retrain'


@dataclass
class FeatureDrift:
    feature: str
    psi: float
    interpretation: str


def drift_profile(reference: Dict[str, List[float]], live: Dict[str, List[float]]) -> List[FeatureDrift]:
    out = []
    for feature in reference:
        if feature in live:
            psi = population_stability_index(reference[feature], live[feature])
            out.append(FeatureDrift(feature, round(psi, 4), interpret_psi(psi)))
    return out


if __name__ == '__main__':
    import random
    random.seed(7)
    reference = {
        'prompt_length': [random.gauss(100, 20) for _ in range(500)],
        'embedding_norm': [random.gauss(1.0, 0.1) for _ in range(500)],
    }
    # prompt_length drifts upward (new usage style); embedding_norm stable
    live = {
        'prompt_length': [random.gauss(160, 25) for _ in range(500)],
        'embedding_norm': [random.gauss(1.0, 0.1) for _ in range(500)],

    for fd in drift_profile(reference, live):
        print(f'{fd.feature:<16} PSI={fd.psi:<8} {fd.interpretation}')
    print('prompt_length shows significant data drift; embedding_norm does not.')

Two-sample distribution tests for drift: a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for continuous features and a chi-squared test for categorical features, each returning a statistic and a significance decision. Implements the distribution tests from Section 13.05 Part 4.

Code

# distribution_tests.py
# Section 13.05: KS and Chi-Squared Distribution Drift Tests
# 20260621 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 13 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import math
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Dict, List, Tuple


@dataclass
class TestResult:
    statistic: float
    significant: bool
    detail: str


def ks_two_sample(reference: List[float], live: List[float], alpha: float = 0.05) -> TestResult:
    # KS statistic: max gap between empirical CDFs
    ref = sorted(reference)
    liv = sorted(live)
    n, m = len(ref), len(liv)
    if n == 0 or m == 0:
        return TestResult(0.0, False, 'empty sample')
    all_vals = sorted(set(ref + liv))
    d = 0.0
    for v in all_vals:
        fr = sum(1 for x in ref if x <= v) / n
        fl = sum(1 for x in liv if x <= v) / m
        d = max(d, abs(fr - fl))
    # critical value approximation for two-sample KS
    c_alpha = 1.358  # for alpha = 0.05
    crit = c_alpha * math.sqrt((n + m) / (n * m))
    return TestResult(round(d, 4), d > crit, f'D={d:.4f}, crit={crit:.4f}')


def chi_squared(reference_counts: Dict[str, int], live_counts: Dict[str, int], alpha: float = 0.05) -> TestResult:
    categories = set(reference_counts) | set(live_counts)
    ref_total = sum(reference_counts.values()) or 1
    live_total = sum(live_counts.values()) or 1
    stat = 0.0
    for cat in categories:
        ref_p = reference_counts.get(cat, 0) / ref_total
        expected = ref_p * live_total
        observed = live_counts.get(cat, 0)
        if expected > 0:
            stat += (observed - expected) ** 2 / expected
    dof = max(len(categories) - 1, 1)
    # rough critical value: chi-squared 0.95 quantile approx via Wilson-Hilferty
    crit = dof * (1 - 2 / (9 * dof) + 1.645 * math.sqrt(2 / (9 * dof))) ** 3
    return TestResult(round(stat, 4), stat > crit, f'chi2={stat:.4f}, dof={dof}, crit={crit:.2f}')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    import random
    random.seed(11)
    ref_cont = [random.gauss(100, 20) for _ in range(300)]
    live_cont_drift = [random.gauss(140, 20) for _ in range(300)]
    live_cont_same = [random.gauss(100, 20) for _ in range(300)]

    print('KS continuous (drifted):', ks_two_sample(ref_cont, live_cont_drift).detail)
    print('  significant:', ks_two_sample(ref_cont, live_cont_drift).significant)
    print('KS continuous (stable): ', ks_two_sample(ref_cont, live_cont_same).detail)
    print('  significant:', ks_two_sample(ref_cont, live_cont_same).significant)

    ref_cat = {'/v1/chat': 700, '/v1/complete': 250, '/v1/embed': 50}
    live_cat_drift = {'/v1/chat': 300, '/v1/complete': 250, '/v1/embed': 450}
    r = chi_squared(ref_cat, live_cat_drift)
    print('\nChi-squared categorical (drifted):', r.detail, '| significant:', r.significant)
    print('Endpoint mix shifted toward /v1/embed -- categorical drift detected.')

# prediction_distribution_monitor.py
# Section 13.05: Prediction-Distribution Monitor for Concept Drift
# 20260621 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 13 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Dict, List


def score_histogram(scores: List[float], bins: int = 5) -> List[float]:
    counts = [0] * bins
    for s in scores:
        idx = min(int(s * bins), bins - 1)
        counts[idx] += 1
    total = sum(counts) or 1
    return [c / total for c in counts]


def high_score_proportion(scores: List[float], threshold: float = 0.7) -> float:
    if not scores:
        return 0.0
    return sum(1 for s in scores if s >= threshold) / len(scores)


@dataclass
class PredictionDriftResult:
    high_score_ref: float
    high_score_live: float
    collapse_ratio: float
    concept_drift_suspected: bool
    detail: str


def monitor_prediction_drift(
    reference_scores: List[float],
    live_scores: List[float],
    collapse_threshold: float = 0.5,
) -> PredictionDriftResult:
    ref_high = high_score_proportion(reference_scores)
    live_high = high_score_proportion(live_scores)
    # collapse ratio: how much the high-score proportion fell
    collapse = 1.0 - (live_high / ref_high) if ref_high > 0 else 0.0
    suspected = collapse > collapse_threshold
    detail = (
        f'high-score proportion {ref_high:.2f} -> {live_high:.2f} '
        f'(collapse {collapse:.0%})'
    )
    return PredictionDriftResult(
        round(ref_high, 3), round(live_high, 3), round(collapse, 3), suspected, detail
    )

    import random
    random.seed(3)
    # Reference: detector catching attacks -- a healthy tail of high scores
    reference_scores = [random.random() for _ in range(400)] + [0.8 + 0.2 * random.random() for _ in range(120)]
    # Live: a new attack technique scores low -- high-score tail collapses
    live_scores = [random.random() for _ in range(400)] + [0.2 + 0.2 * random.random() for _ in range(120)]

    print(result.detail)
    print('Concept drift suspected:', result.concept_drift_suspected)
    print('A collapse in the high-score proportion with no input-side shift')
    print('signals concept drift -- a new technique the detector no longer flags.')

# drift_retrain_trigger.py
# Section 13.05: Drift Retraining Trigger and Benign/Adversarial Routing
# 20260621 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 13 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Callable, List, Optional


@dataclass
class DriftSignal:
    feature_or_model: str
    magnitude: float
    sustained_windows: int          # how many consecutive windows drifted
    coincident_anomaly_rate: float  # other behavioural signals at the time
    coincident_product_change: bool


@dataclass
class DriftRouter:
    min_sustained_windows: int = 3
    cooldown_windows: int = 10
    retrain_action: Optional[Callable[[DriftSignal], None]] = None
    alert_action: Optional[Callable[[DriftSignal], None]] = None
    _windows_since_retrain: int = field(default=999)

    def _is_adversarial(self, sig: DriftSignal) -> bool:
        # Adversarial if it coincides with elevated behavioural signals
        # and is NOT explained by a product change
        return sig.coincident_anomaly_rate > 0.2 and not sig.coincident_product_change

    def process(self, sig: DriftSignal) -> str:
        self._windows_since_retrain += 1

        # Not yet a confirmed, sustained drift -> watch only
        if sig.sustained_windows < self.min_sustained_windows:
            return 'watch: drift not yet sustained'

        if self._is_adversarial(sig):
            if self.alert_action:
                self.alert_action(sig)
            # adversarial drift also feeds the corpus, but always alerts
            return 'ADVERSARIAL: security alert raised + attacks queued for corpus'

        # Benign sustained drift -> retrain, subject to cooldown
        if self._windows_since_retrain < self.cooldown_windows:
            return 'benign drift confirmed but within cooldown -- retrain suppressed'
        if self.retrain_action:
            self.retrain_action(sig)
        self._windows_since_retrain = 0
        return 'BENIGN: retraining triggered (12.14) and cooldown reset'


if __name__ == '__main__':
    retrains: List[str] = []
    alerts: List[str] = []
    router = DriftRouter(
        retrain_action=lambda s: retrains.append(s.feature_or_model),
        alert_action=lambda s: alerts.append(s.feature_or_model),
    )

    cases = [
        DriftSignal('prompt_length', 0.3, sustained_windows=1, coincident_anomaly_rate=0.05, coincident_product_change=False),
        DriftSignal('prompt_length', 0.3, sustained_windows=4, coincident_anomaly_rate=0.05, coincident_product_change=True),
        DriftSignal('injection_score_dist', 0.4, sustained_windows=4, coincident_anomaly_rate=0.45, coincident_product_change=False),
    ]
    for c in cases:
        print(f'{c.feature_or_model:<22} -> {router.process(c)}')

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Deploying a trained detector without any drift monitoring and assuming its accuracy persists

Why It Happens

A detector that validated well at training time produces confident scores indefinitely, with no error or warning to suggest its accuracy has changed. The pipeline looks healthy -- it processes traffic and blocks the attacks it still recognises -- so there is no visible prompt to add monitoring of the detector's own health.

Consequence

Input and attack distributions shift over time, and the detector decays silently against them. The only way to discover the decay without drift monitoring is to notice attacks getting through that should have been caught, which is discovering the failure by suffering its consequence after the damage is done. The detector becomes a decaying asset with no alarm on its decay, and the decay is found through a breach.

Correct Approach

Monitor the distributions the detector depends on -- input features via the population stability index and distribution tests, output scores via prediction-distribution monitoring -- so the shift that causes decay is detected before the accuracy loss is large. drift_detector.py and prediction_distribution_monitor.py provide the input-side and output-side alarms that turn silent decay into an early, actionable signal.

Mistake 2

Description

Monitoring only the input distribution and missing concept drift that has no input-side signal

Why It Happens

Input-distribution monitoring with the population stability index is the standard, well-documented drift technique, and it catches the visible data drift that most drift discussions focus on. Once it is in place and catching input shifts, drift detection feels complete.

Consequence

Concept drift -- a change in what the inputs mean -- can occur with no input-distribution shift. A new attack technique that presents through familiar-looking features degrades the detector while the input monitors see nothing, because the inputs are not new; only their relationship to the correct label has changed. The most dangerous drift, the one that produces silent security failure, is exactly the one input monitoring misses.

Correct Approach

Add prediction-distribution monitoring that watches the detector's output score distribution, which shifts when the detector's decisions change even if the inputs do not. A collapse in the high-score proportion is a leading, label-free signal of the concept drift that input monitoring cannot see. prediction_distribution_monitor.py provides this output-side complement.

Mistake 3

Description

Triggering a retrain on every drift alarm with no confirmation or cooldown

Why It Happens

Drift detection is built to trigger retraining, so wiring each drift alarm straight to a retrain feels like completing the loop. A single window showing drift looks like sufficient cause to refresh the detector.

Consequence

A retrain is expensive and consequential. A single window's drift is often transient noise -- a brief seasonal spike, a sampling fluctuation -- and retraining on it wastes resources and risks promoting a model fitted to a transient shift that has already reversed. Triggering on every alarm makes the retraining pipeline thrash and can degrade the detector by training it on noise.

Correct Approach

Trigger retraining only on confirmed, sustained drift across multiple consecutive windows, and apply a cooldown so the pipeline cannot retrain repeatedly in a short period. The confirmation filters transient noise and the cooldown bounds the cost. drift_retrain_trigger.py requires a minimum number of sustained windows and enforces a cooldown before a retrain fires.

Mistake 4

Description

Treating adversarial drift as benign and quietly retraining on an attack distribution

Why It Happens

The drift response pipeline routes drift to retraining by default, because most drift is benign distribution movement that retraining correctly handles. An adversarial shift looks like just another distribution change to a monitor that does not check what the drift coincides with.

Consequence

Adversarial drift is an attack technique shifting the attack distribution. Retraining on it without recognising it as adversarial folds the attack into the training corpus and normalises it: the detector learns to expect the attack pattern as part of the data, and the security team is never alerted that a new campaign is underway. The drift response actively launders the attack into accepted normal.

Correct Approach

Classify drift as benign or adversarial by correlating it with the other behavioural signals -- rising anomaly rates, block rates, coordinated-network signals indicate adversarial drift, while a product launch or seasonal boundary indicates benign drift -- and route adversarial drift to a security alert as well as the corpus, never silently retraining on it. drift_retrain_trigger.py checks coincident signals before routing.

Mistake 5

Description

Choosing an arbitrary recent window as the drift reference instead of the detector training or last-validated distribution

Why It Happens

Comparing the current window to the immediately preceding window is the simplest reference choice and requires no separate stored reference. It detects sudden changes between adjacent windows, which looks like drift detection.

Consequence

Drift must be measured relative to the distribution the detector was actually trained on or last validated against, because that is the distribution the detector assumes. Comparing against a recent window misses a slow drift that moves the distribution gradually -- each adjacent window resembles the last, so no alarm fires, while the cumulative shift away from the training distribution grows large. The reference-window error makes the monitor blind to exactly the gradual drift that retraining is meant to catch.

Correct Approach

Anchor the drift reference to the detector's training or last-validation distribution, so drift is measured against the detector's real operating assumption and a gradual cumulative shift is detected even when no single adjacent-window comparison would flag it. The reference is updated only when the detector is retrained or revalidated, not on a rolling recent basis.

Section Summary

Compact

A trained detector is a decaying asset: input and attack distributions shift over time, and the Chapter 12 classifiers degrade silently against them, returning confident but increasingly wrong scores with no error to signal the decay. Section 13.05 is the alarm on that decay, applying temporal distributional comparison to the detectors and the model rather than to user behaviour. Drift comes in two kinds: data drift, a shift in the input distribution detectable on the input side, and concept drift, a shift in the relationship between inputs and labels that is more dangerous because it can degrade the detector with no input-side signal. The population stability index and KS and chi-squared tests catch data drift on the inputs; prediction-distribution monitoring catches concept drift through a shift in the score distribution -- a collapse in the high-score proportion -- as a leading, label- free indicator. Drift alarms close the loop to the Chapter 12 Section 12.14 retraining trigger, gated by sustained-drift confirmation and a cooldown so a transient shift does not trigger an expensive retrain. Crucially, drift is routed by kind: benign drift adapts the model through retraining, while adversarial drift -- a shift coinciding with rising attack signals -- raises a security alert rather than being quietly normalised into the corpus.

Key Takeaways

  • A trained detector is a decaying asset: it degrades silently as input and attack distributions shift, and without drift monitoring the decay is discovered only by suffering an attack it should have caught
  • Data drift is a shift in the input distribution, detectable on the input side; concept drift is a shift in the input-label relationship, more dangerous because it can degrade the detector with no input-side signal
  • The population stability index and KS and chi-squared tests catch data drift on the inputs, with the index giving an interpretable magnitude and the tests giving a significance signal
  • Prediction-distribution monitoring catches concept drift through a shift in the score distribution -- notably a high-score-proportion collapse -- as a leading, label-free indicator that input monitoring cannot provide
  • Drift alarms feed the Chapter 12 Section 12.14 retraining trigger, gated by sustained-drift confirmation and a cooldown so a transient shift does not trigger an expensive or noise-fitted retrain
  • Route drift by kind: benign drift adapts the model through retraining, while adversarial drift coinciding with rising attack signals raises a security alert rather than being quietly normalised into the training corpus

Title

Section 13.06: Model Behavioural Monitoring -- Output Distribution Shift as Injection-Success Signal

Summary

Section 13.06 turns from monitoring the detectors to monitoring the model itself, drawing the distinction between detecting an injection attempt on the input -- the Chapter 12 classifiers' job -- and detecting injection success on the output. Successful injection manifests as a shift in the model's output distribution: its refusal rate drops, its persona breaks, its format conformance fails, or it emits content it was instructed never to produce. The section builds a per-endpoint output distribution baseline as the reference for shift detection, output-anomaly detection for responses that violate the constraints the model normally honours, canary and honeytoken monitoring that turns injection success into a high-confidence binary signal, and self-consistency monitoring that detects when the model contradicts its own earlier behaviour within a session. It is the complement to the input-side classifiers and to the Section 12.09 PII output scanning: where the input layer detects the attempt, model behavioural monitoring confirms the outcome.
