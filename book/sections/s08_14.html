Section 8.14: Federated Learning and Gradient Poisoning - Decentralised Training Attack Surface

Section Overview

Title

Federated Learning and Gradient Poisoning: Decentralised Training Attack Surface

One Line Summary

Federated learning distributes training across many participants without centralising raw data, and that distribution makes the gradient update itself the attack surface. Byzantine participants contribute arbitrarily malicious gradients; Sybil attackers control multiple participant identities to amplify influence; model replacement attacks engineer gradient updates that overwrite server-side aggregated model behaviour. The defensive response is Byzantine- robust aggregation (median, trimmed mean, Krum, Bulyan) plus participant reputation tracking plus gradient anomaly detection. Federated learning extends the Chapter 8 framework to a decentralised threat model with distinctive economic properties: the attack surface is the participation interface itself, and trusted participation cannot be assumed.

Why This Section Exists

Sections 8.7 through 8.13 covered training-time attacks and defences primarily within centralised training contexts: a single organisation runs the training pipeline on data they control or aggregate. The threat model assumed the training pipeline operator is trustworthy and the attack surface is the data feeding into the pipeline. Section 8.14 covers the decentralised case: federated learning, where many participants jointly train a model by contributing gradient updates without sharing raw data, and the attack surface is the gradient contribution interface. Federated learning is a growing deployment pattern across cross-silo collaboration (multiple hospitals training a shared diagnostic model), on-device training (mobile keyboards learning user patterns without server-side data centralisation), and privacy-preserving ML deployments where regulatory or competitive constraints prevent centralised data pooling. Each deployment context has distinct threat model properties, but the underlying attack surface (gradient contribution) is shared. The defensive architecture established in Section 8.14 applies across contexts. The section also addresses a structural property that is unique to federated learning: the participants are typically untrusted by design. Centralised training assumes the training operator is trustworthy and asks how to defend against poisoned data. Federated training assumes participants may be Byzantine -- contributing arbitrarily malicious gradients without any structural constraint -- and asks how to aggregate contributions while limiting Byzantine influence. The defensive response operates on aggregation rather than data validation, which is a structurally different defensive paradigm from Sections 8.7-8.13. The third function of this section is grounding the federated learning threat model for organisations adopting it. Federated learning is often framed as inherently more secure than centralised training because raw data does not leave participant devices. The framing captures one privacy property correctly and elides the gradient-poisoning attack surface that centralised training does not have. Section 8.14 makes the tradeoff explicit: federated learning provides data privacy benefits at the cost of new attack surface, with defensive responsibility shifting from data validation (centralised) to aggregation robustness (federated).

The pipeline accommodates both centralised and federated deployments through configurable defensive tracks.

Prose Content

Part 1 Federated Learning Threat Model

Title

The Federated Learning Threat Model: Untrusted Participants by Design

Body

Federated learning is a training architecture in which a model is trained across many participants without centralising the raw training data. Each participant holds local data, computes gradient updates from that data against a shared model, and contributes the updates to a server (or to other participants in peer-to-peer variants). The server aggregates the updates into a new shared model and distributes it back. The training proceeds in rounds, with each round consisting of participant computation, contribution submission, server aggregation, and model redistribution. The principal data privacy property is that no participant's raw data is shared with any other participant or with the server. The threat model that emerges from this architecture is structurally different from centralised training. In centralised training, the attack surface is the data fed into the pipeline; the training operator is assumed trustworthy. In federated training, the data stays at participants and the attack surface is the gradient contributions themselves. Participants are typically untrusted by design: federated deployments often involve participants from multiple organisations (cross-silo) or from anonymous user populations (on-device), and structural trust between participants is absent. The defensive assumption is that some fraction of participants may be Byzantine -- contributing arbitrarily malicious gradients without any structural constraint on what they submit. The Byzantine participant capability matters because it is structurally more powerful than the data poisoning capability in centralised training. A data poisoner contributes training samples that influence the model through gradient descent on those samples; the influence is bounded by the gradient information present in the sample plus the aggregation pipeline. A Byzantine gradient contributor submits the gradient directly and can craft it to maximise effect: arbitrarily large magnitudes, directions explicitly engineered to shift the model toward attacker goals, patterns designed to evade aggregation defences. The gradient is a much higher-bandwidth attack channel than data, and Byzantine contributors can exploit this. The empirical scale of the threat is not theoretical. Cross-silo federated deployments involving tens to hundreds of participating organisations commonly assume some fraction may be adversarial -- driven by competitive considerations, malicious insider threats within participating organisations, or external compromise. On-device federated deployments involving millions of mobile devices assume that some fraction are adversary-controlled at any time -- compromised devices, malicious app installations, or coordinated adversary campaigns. The fraction varies by deployment but is typically nontrivial (1-10% is a common operational assumption); aggregating contributions while bounding Byzantine influence is the defensive problem. The threat model also has temporal structure. Federated learning operates over many rounds, sometimes hundreds or thousands. Adversaries can attack persistently across rounds, refining their gradient contributions based on observed model evolution, or strategically timing their attacks to maximise effect at specific training stages. The defensive infrastructure must operate continuously across rounds rather than as a one-time check. Section 8.14 covers the principal attack patterns and the Byzantine- robust aggregation defences that constitute the core federated learning security architecture. The treatment is necessarily compact compared to a full federated learning textbook; the focus is on the attack surface and defensive primitives that integrate with the broader Chapter 8 framework. For deeper engagement, the foundational papers (Krum: Blanchard et al. 2017; trimmed mean and median: Yin et al. 2018; Bulyan: El Mhamdi et al. 2018; FoolsGold: Fung et al. 2018) provide additional mathematical depth. Section 8.14 establishes the operational framework that Section 8.15 production pipeline integrates.

Part 2 Gradient Poisoning Attack Patterns

Title

Gradient Poisoning Attack Patterns

Body

Gradient poisoning attacks fall into several structural categories, each with distinctive mechanics and defensive implications. The attack taxonomy informs aggregation defence selection: different aggregation methods have different coverage profiles against different attack patterns. The simplest gradient poisoning is direct Byzantine contribution. The adversary participates in federated learning as a single participant and submits gradient updates designed to shift the aggregated model toward attacker goals. The gradient may be a large-magnitude update in an attacker-chosen direction (overwhelming clean contributions), a backdoor-installation gradient (engineered to install trigger-target associations across rounds), or a model-degradation gradient (engineered to reduce model accuracy generally). Direct Byzantine contributions from a single participant are limited in effect by the aggregation: a single malicious contribution among many clean ones is typically averaged out at appropriate scales. Sybil attacks extend Byzantine contribution by having a single adversary control multiple participant identities. If the adversary creates many participant accounts (in on-device contexts) or compromises multiple participating devices (via malware or supply chain attack), each controlled identity contributes gradients that, when aggregated, sum to attacker-chosen direction. The adversary's effective influence scales with the number of controlled identities. For deployments where identity verification is weak (anonymous on-device contexts, public cross-silo without identity validation), Sybil attacks are operationally cheap and can scale to substantial fractions of contributions. Model replacement attacks engineer gradient contributions specifically to overwrite the aggregated model with attacker-chosen behaviour. The adversary computes the gradient that, when added to the current model under the deployed aggregation rule, produces a target model. For federated averaging (FedAvg), the gradient required to install attacker-chosen weights w* given current model w_t is approximately n * (w* - w_t), where n is the number of participants. With sufficient Sybil participants or sufficient single-participant gradient magnitude, the model replacement is achievable in a single round. Model replacement attacks are particularly dangerous because they bypass the defensive assumption that single-round contributions have bounded effect. Backdoor installation via federated gradient poisoning operates similarly to centralised backdoor attacks (Section 8.5) but with different attack economics. The attacker contributes gradients that encode trigger-target associations. The aggregated model gradually learns the associations across rounds. The attack budget required (in terms of fraction of Byzantine participants) depends on the aggregation rule: for FedAvg without robust aggregation, fractions as low as 1-2% can install backdoors over many rounds; for robust aggregation methods, the fraction must exceed specific thresholds (Krum requires more than one-third Byzantine to fail). Coordinated multi-round attacks combine the above. The adversary contributes gradients across many rounds with strategic timing, gradually refining backdoor installation while evading per-round anomaly detection. The contributions in any single round may not look anomalous; the aggregate effect across rounds is the attack. Detection that operates per-round may miss multi-round attacks; detection requires temporal analysis across rounds. Targeted attacks on specific participants extend the threat. In peer- to-peer federated variants, an adversary participating in the network can target other participants' contributions specifically: by strategically positioning their own contributions, they can amplify or cancel specific other participants' gradients. The attack is more sophisticated and requires more participation control, but it can produce specific outcomes beyond aggregate model corruption. The principal defensive response to all these attack patterns is Byzantine-robust aggregation, covered in the next part. The aggregation methods bound the influence individual or coordinated Byzantine participants can have on the aggregated update, with specific thresholds and assumptions that determine the coverage profile.

Part 3 Byzantine Robust Aggregation

Title

Byzantine-Robust Aggregation: Bounded Influence Despite Malicious Participants

Body

Byzantine-robust aggregation methods replace standard federated averaging (FedAvg) with aggregation rules that bound the influence of Byzantine participants. The methods provide mathematical guarantees: under specified conditions on the fraction of Byzantine participants and the structure of their attacks, the aggregated update is close to what clean aggregation would have produced. The conditions are critical -- Byzantine-robust aggregation works within specific regimes and fails outside them. The simplest robust aggregation is the coordinate-wise median. Instead of averaging participant gradients, take the median of each gradient coordinate across participants. The median is robust against outliers: Byzantine participants contributing extreme values for specific coordinates do not affect the median if their fraction is below 50%. The robustness is per-coordinate, which is computationally cheap and easy to implement, but provides limited protection against sophisticated attacks that distribute Byzantine influence across many coordinates with moderate magnitudes. Trimmed mean refines the median approach. Instead of taking the median, remove the top k and bottom k values per coordinate (where k is bounded by the assumed Byzantine fraction) and average the remaining values. Trimmed mean provides similar robustness to median with somewhat better utility on clean data: discarding only extreme values rather than always taking the middle. Trimmed mean breaks at higher Byzantine fractions than median (typically up to 50% with appropriate trimming) and provides moderate computational cost. Krum (Blanchard et al. 2017) takes a different approach: instead of coordinate-wise robust aggregation, select the single participant contribution that is closest to the majority of other contributions. The selection rule is structurally different: rather than aggregating contributions, choose one. The selected contribution is unlikely to be Byzantine if Byzantine participants comprise less than approximately one-third of total. Krum provides strong robustness within its regime but discards information from non-selected contributions, which has utility cost on clean data. Bulyan (El Mhamdi et al. 2018) extends Krum: apply Krum repeatedly to select multiple contributions, then apply trimmed mean over the selections. The combination provides better utility than Krum alone while maintaining similar Byzantine robustness. Bulyan operates with higher computational cost than simpler aggregation but produces strong empirical results on standard benchmarks. FoolsGold (Fung et al. 2018) addresses Sybil attacks specifically. The mechanism observes that Sybil participants tend to contribute similar gradients (because they share the same adversary's underlying attack logic). FoolsGold detects this similarity and downweights similar contributions, breaking the Sybil amplification effect. The method works well against Sybil attacks but is structurally different from Krum-family methods and may not be combined trivially. The coverage profile across these methods has several important patterns. All methods require an assumption about the maximum Byzantine fraction; their guarantees hold within the assumption and fail outside it. Median tolerates up to 50% Byzantine for individual coordinates but breaks under coordinated cross-coordinate attacks. Trimmed mean depends on trimming parameter; conservative trimming bounds Byzantine fraction tightly but discards more clean information. Krum requires below 33% Byzantine. Bulyan inherits Krum's threshold with better utility within it. FoolsGold tolerates higher Sybil fractions specifically but provides limited protection against non-Sybil Byzantine attacks. No method provides unconditional robustness; programme architecture must select the method appropriate to the assumed adversary fraction. The utility cost of robust aggregation also matters. Standard FedAvg converges fastest on clean data because it uses all gradient information efficiently. Robust methods discard information (Krum) or noise it (median, trimmed mean), reducing convergence speed and final accuracy. The cost varies by method and deployment: Krum has higher utility cost than trimmed mean; Bulyan reduces Krum's cost; FoolsGold has modest cost when Sybil fraction is low. Programmes balancing utility against robustness select methods accordingly. Operational deployment of robust aggregation requires several engineering decisions. Method selection based on assumed adversary fraction and attack patterns. Hyperparameter calibration (trimming fraction, Krum subset size, FoolsGold similarity threshold). Combination with non-aggregation defences (participant reputation, gradient anomaly detection) for layered coverage. Monitoring of adversary fraction estimates to detect when assumed bounds are exceeded. Section 8.14 deliverable 2 provides reference implementations of the principal methods with calibration guidance.

Part 4 Participant Reputation And Gradient Anomaly Detection

Title

Participant Reputation and Gradient Anomaly Detection

Body

Byzantine-robust aggregation is the primary defensive layer for federated learning, but it operates per-round on contributions and does not directly identify Byzantine participants for exclusion. Two complementary defensive layers operate at participant identification: participant reputation tracking and gradient anomaly detection. Together with robust aggregation, they constitute the layered federated learning defensive architecture. Participant reputation tracking maintains scores for each participant based on observed contribution patterns across rounds. Participants whose contributions consistently align with the majority and produce reasonable model improvements accumulate reputation. Participants whose contributions are systematically anomalous, divergent, or correlated with model degradation accumulate negative signals. The reputation can be used in several ways: weighting contributions by reputation in aggregation, excluding participants whose reputation falls below thresholds, or routing low-reputation contributions to additional review. The reputation mechanism has several operational properties. It is adversary-aware: adversaries who know the reputation algorithm can attempt to game it by initially contributing benign updates to build reputation before pivoting to attack mode. The defence is to weight recent behaviour heavily relative to historical reputation, limiting the value of long-term reputation building. Reputation decay is also important: stale reputation from inactive participants should not accrue trust they did not earn through recent contribution. Reputation maintenance must handle cold start: new participants have no reputation history; the policy for handling new participants depends on deployment context (anonymous on-device may treat new participants conservatively; cross-silo with identity verification may extend trust based on organisational reputation). Sybil resistance in reputation systems is the principal challenge. Sybil adversaries can create many participant identities and have each accumulate independent reputation. The aggregate Sybil reputation can exceed any single legitimate participant's reputation, undermining the reputation defence. Sybil resistance in federated reputation requires either external identity verification (raising the cost of identity creation) or behaviour-based Sybil detection (identifying groups of identities that behave similarly enough to be likely adversary-controlled). FoolsGold-style similarity detection partially addresses behaviour-based Sybil in reputation; external identity verification is deployment-specific. Gradient anomaly detection operates on individual contributions per round, identifying contributions that deviate from expected patterns irrespective of participant identity. The anomaly detection compares each contribution against the distribution of clean contributions (which can be estimated from historical data, current-round majority, or held-out validation contributions). Contributions that are anomalously large in magnitude, anomalously directional, or anomalously sparse are flagged. The flagged contributions can be excluded from aggregation, downweighted, or routed to additional analysis. The anomaly detection signal is per-round and per-contribution; it complements reputation tracking which is multi-round and per- participant. Anomaly detection catches first-round Byzantine contributions before reputation can develop; reputation tracking catches multi-round patterns that any single round may not flag. The two layers cover different temporal scales of attack. The principal limitation of gradient anomaly detection is calibration. Distinguishing legitimate but unusual gradients (from participants with non-typical data distributions or training characteristics) from Byzantine gradients requires baselines that match expected legitimate variation. Insufficient baselines produce false positives that exclude legitimate participants; over-permissive baselines produce false negatives that include Byzantine contributions. Modern anomaly detection approaches use statistical methods (Mahalanobis distance, isolation forests) calibrated against historical contribution distributions, with tunable thresholds. The combined defensive architecture for federated learning therefore includes Byzantine-robust aggregation (primary defence, per-round bounded influence), participant reputation tracking (multi-round identification of Byzantine participants), and gradient anomaly detection (per-round identification of anomalous contributions). Each layer covers different attack patterns and time scales. The combination provides operationally meaningful coverage; deployments typically combine all three with weights and thresholds calibrated to the assumed adversary fraction. Part 5 Privacy Security Tradeoff And Deployment Contexts

Title

Privacy-Security Tradeoff and Deployment Context Variations

Body

Federated learning is often framed as inherently more secure than centralised training because raw data does not leave participant devices. The framing captures one privacy property correctly -- participant raw data privacy is preserved -- but elides the gradient- poisoning attack surface that centralised training does not have. The complete framing is that federated learning trades data centralisation risk for gradient poisoning risk; the tradeoff is favourable in some contexts and unfavourable in others depending on threat model. The favourable cases are where data centralisation risk dominates the threat model. For deployments with sensitive raw data subject to regulatory or competitive constraints (healthcare records, financial transactions, personal communications), centralising the data creates privacy and compliance exposures that outweigh the gradient-poisoning risk. Federated learning in these contexts provides genuine net defensive benefit: the gradient-poisoning attack surface is real but manageable through robust aggregation, while the data centralisation attack surface (data breach, insider exfiltration, compliance violation) is structurally larger and harder to defend. The unfavourable cases are where data sensitivity is moderate and the gradient-poisoning attack surface is operationally significant. For deployments with public or low-sensitivity data, centralising the data creates manageable risk and produces stronger training (no participant heterogeneity, no aggregation noise). Federated learning in these contexts adds attack surface without commensurate privacy benefit: the gradient-poisoning surface is real, the data privacy benefit is small, and centralised training with standard data governance provides better outcomes. The deployment context drives the threat model and defensive architecture. Cross-silo federated learning involves a small number of well-known organisational participants (typically 5-50 organisations). The threat model is dominated by individual participant compromise (one organisation's infrastructure compromised) and competitive adversarial behaviour (one organisation contributing biased gradients to manipulate the shared model in their favour). The defensive architecture emphasises participant identity verification, contractual enforcement, and Byzantine-robust aggregation tuned to the small participant count. On-device federated learning involves many anonymous participant devices (potentially millions). The threat model is dominated by Sybil attacks (adversary-controlled devices), supply-chain compromise (malicious app installations), and aggregate behaviour manipulation (large-scale coordinated contributions). The defensive architecture emphasises Sybil-resistant aggregation (FoolsGold), gradient anomaly detection at scale, and reputation systems robust to Sybil amplification. Privacy-preserving collaborative ML (variant of cross-silo) involves participants who specifically need data privacy, often for regulatory compliance (GDPR Article 6 lawful bases, HIPAA Privacy Rule, sector- specific). The threat model includes participant compromise plus regulatory non-compliance risk if the federated architecture leaks data unexpectedly. The defensive architecture emphasises differential privacy in addition to Byzantine robustness (DP-SGD applied at the federated level, providing both data privacy and gradient bounding), formal privacy accounting across rounds, and audit-ready compliance documentation. For organisations evaluating federated learning adoption, the operational decision involves matching deployment context to threat model and ensuring the defensive architecture covers the relevant attack surfaces. Adopting federated learning for the privacy benefit without commensurate gradient-poisoning defence produces a posture that is privacy-improved but security-degraded, which is typically not the intended outcome. The complete operational architecture includes Byzantine-robust aggregation (primary), participant reputation and gradient anomaly detection (secondary), and DP-SGD at the federated level (privacy-preserving across rounds). Section 8.15 production pipeline supports federated deployments through configurable defensive tracks. The integration with Sections 8.7-8.13 is also worth highlighting. Federated learning is sometimes deployed as the federated stage of LLM training: federated instruction-tuning across multiple organisations, federated fine-tuning of foundation models on organisationally-distributed data. In these contexts, the federated gradient-poisoning attack surface combines with the LLM-specific attack surfaces from Sections 8.7-8.8. Defensive coverage requires both layers: federated-specific (this section) and LLM-specific (Sections 8.7-8.8). Section 8.15 production pipeline integrates them where they overlap.

Part 6 Emerging Research And Remaining Challenges

Title

Emerging Research and Remaining Challenges

Body

Federated learning security is an active research area with substantial ongoing development. The principal published methods and defences summarised in earlier parts represent the established core, but the field continues to develop in directions that affect operational deployment. Section 8.14 closes by identifying the principal emerging directions and remaining defensive challenges. One emerging direction is federated learning with secure aggregation. Secure aggregation protocols (cryptographic constructions where the server learns only the aggregated update, not individual participant contributions) provide additional privacy properties: the server cannot inspect individual participant gradients, which addresses certain inference attacks against participant privacy. Secure aggregation interacts with Byzantine robustness in subtle ways: most Byzantine-robust aggregation methods require the server to inspect individual contributions to identify outliers, which conflicts with the secure aggregation goal of hiding individual contributions. Recent research has produced hybrid protocols that achieve partial robustness under secure aggregation, but the combined coverage is weaker than either property alone. Programmes deploying both must accept partial coverage in each dimension. A second direction is federated learning with formal differential privacy. Applying DP-SGD-style noise addition at the federated level provides formal privacy guarantees plus gradient bounding. The privacy accounting across rounds is more complex than centralised DP-SGD because each round adds privacy cost and the total privacy budget must be managed across all rounds. Tools like the Privacy Accountant framework provide the necessary accounting. The defensive benefit combines: the per-round noise bounds Byzantine influence (Section 8.13 DP-SGD coverage extending to federated context); the multi-round privacy guarantee provides participant data privacy. A third direction is targeted defence against specific advanced attacks. Recent research has produced attack-specific defences for advanced Byzantine attacks: adaptive attacks that evolve in response to robust aggregation, model replacement attacks that exploit specific aggregation properties, and coalition attacks where multiple Byzantine participants coordinate explicitly. Each defence is targeted to a specific attack pattern; comprehensive coverage requires combining multiple defences with potentially conflicting requirements. The operational picture is increasingly complex; Section 8.14 deliverables target the established core but cite the research literature for advanced cases. A fourth direction is federated learning for foundation models specifically. Training large language models in federated settings involves distinct challenges: model size, communication cost, gradient heterogeneity across participants, and the LLM-specific attack surfaces from Sections 8.7-8.8. Recent work has addressed these through parameter-efficient federated training (federated LoRA), privacy-preserving instruction tuning, and federated RLHF variants. Defensive coverage in these contexts combines Section 8.7-8.9 LLM-specific defences with Section 8.14 federated-specific defences; the integrated architecture is operationally complex and represents a significant deployment investment. The remaining defensive challenges include several open problems. Adversary fraction estimation: knowing the actual Byzantine fraction in deployment is necessary for selecting appropriate aggregation methods, but estimating it is difficult in practice. Conservative assumptions (high Byzantine fraction) provide stronger defence at higher utility cost; permissive assumptions (low fraction) provide better utility but fail if actual fraction exceeds. Adaptive aggregation that adjusts based on observed contribution patterns is a research direction with operational potential. Cross-round attack detection: per-round defences (robust aggregation, anomaly detection on round contributions) miss multi-round attacks that distribute their effect. Reputation systems partially address this but have Sybil vulnerabilities. Comprehensive cross-round attack detection that resists Sybil amplification is an open problem. Privacy-robustness tension: secure aggregation, differential privacy, and Byzantine robustness all provide distinct guarantees, but combining them produces composition costs. Each layer adds noise, computational cost, or coverage gaps; the total composition is weaker than any individual property. Designing federated systems with strong combined properties remains a research challenge with operational implications. For practitioners deploying federated learning, the operational implication is that defensive architecture must be designed for the specific deployment context and updated as research advances. Section 8.14 deliverables provide the established core; the production pipeline in Section 8.15 supports configurable extension as advanced defences become production-ready. The trajectory is toward stronger combined coverage; the current state is uneven and deployment- specific.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 Treating Federated As Inherently Secure

Description

Treating federated learning as inherently more secure than centralised training without defensive infrastructure

Why It Happens

Federated learning is often marketed as a privacy-preserving alternative to centralised training. The privacy framing is correct but incomplete: federated learning preserves participant data privacy while introducing gradient-poisoning attack surface. Practitioners adopting federated learning for the privacy benefit may neglect the gradient-poisoning defence. The result is a posture that is privacy-improved but security-degraded.

Consequence

Federated deployments use FedAvg (no Byzantine robustness) and assume participants are trustworthy. Byzantine attacks succeed at low fractions; backdoor installation is straightforward; the deployment is materially less secure than centralised alternatives despite the privacy framing.

Correct Approach

Treat federated learning as introducing gradient-poisoning attack surface that requires defensive infrastructure. Deploy Byzantine-robust aggregation as standard. Section 8.14 defensive architecture is mandatory, not optional. The privacy benefit and the security cost should both be understood when adopting federated.

Mistake 2 Fedavg In Production

Description

Deploying FedAvg (standard averaging) in production federated learning

Why It Happens

FedAvg is the simplest aggregation method and the default in many federated frameworks. Practitioners using framework defaults inherit FedAvg without explicit decision. The mistake is that FedAvg has effectively zero Byzantine robustness; any Byzantine participant can shift the aggregated model proportionally to their contribution magnitude. In production deployments with untrusted participants, FedAvg is not a defensive choice.

Consequence

Federated deployments using FedAvg are vulnerable to single-Byzantine attacks at trivially low adversary fractions. Sybil amplification produces complete attack control. The deployment is structurally undefended.

Correct Approach

Replace FedAvg with Byzantine-robust aggregation (Krum, Bulyan, trimmed mean, or FoolsGold for Sybil-heavy contexts) as standard for production deployments. FedAvg is appropriate only for trusted-participant or simulation contexts. Section 8.14 deliverable 2 provides production-ready replacements.

Mistake 3 Ignoring Sybil Resistance

Description

Deploying federated learning without Sybil-resistance measures

Why It Happens

Sybil attacks require adversaries to control multiple identities, which may seem operationally costly. Practitioners may underestimate Sybil cost, particularly in on-device contexts where account creation is free or cheap. Sybil attacks are operationally cheap in many federated contexts, and without Sybil resistance, all Byzantine fraction assumptions become invalid.

Consequence

Sybil-amplified attacks bypass Byzantine fraction thresholds. Krum-based defences fail when adversary controls more than 33% of identities; this is achievable through Sybil at low cost. The deployment defends against assumed adversary fractions but not actual Sybil-amplified fractions.

Correct Approach

Deploy Sybil resistance: identity verification where deployment context allows, FoolsGold-style behaviour-based Sybil detection where it does not, and reputation systems with Sybil resistance. Section 8.14 deliverable 3 includes Sybil cluster detection. The combined defensive architecture must address Sybil amplification, not just direct Byzantine attacks.

Mistake 4 Static Byzantine Fraction Assumption

Description

Selecting aggregation methods based on static Byzantine fraction assumption without monitoring actual fraction

Why It Happens

Method selection requires assuming a Byzantine fraction. Practitioners pick a fraction at deployment time and stick with it. The mistake is that adversary fraction can change over deployment lifetime: new compromises occur, attack campaigns scale up, defensive countermeasures shift attacker behaviour. A method appropriate for the initial assumed fraction may be inadequate later.

Consequence

Aggregation method becomes inadequate as adversary fraction shifts. The defensive architecture is not retuned, and the deployment becomes vulnerable to attacks that the original method would have caught at higher tolerance levels.

Correct Approach

Monitor actual adversary fraction estimates through anomaly detection signals, reputation distributions, and attack campaign indicators. Re-tune aggregation method when fraction estimates exceed method tolerance. Section 8.14 deliverable 5 includes adaptive monitoring; Section 8.15 production pipeline integrates the monitoring as standard discipline.

Mistake 5 Per Round Only Defence

Description

Deploying per-round defences (robust aggregation, gradient anomaly) without multi-round behavioural analysis

Why It Happens

Per-round defences are computationally efficient and conceptually simple: each round is a defensive event. Practitioners deploy per-round defence as their architecture. The mistake is that sophisticated attacks distribute their effect across many rounds; per-round contributions look normal, aggregate effect is the attack. Per-round defences miss multi-round patterns.

Consequence

Coordinated multi-round attacks (FED-COORDINATED) succeed undetected. Backdoor installation across rounds (FED-BACKDOOR) at sub-per-round-detection magnitudes accumulates over training. Per-round defensive metrics look clean while the model is being progressively compromised.

Correct Approach

Combine per-round defences with multi-round behavioural analysis: cross-round model behavioural drift detection, participant reputation tracking, periodic full audits of aggregated model. Section 8.14 deliverable 5 integrates the layers; Section 8.15 production pipeline includes cross-round monitoring as standard discipline.

Section Summary

Compact

Federated learning distributes training across many participants without centralising raw data, and the distribution makes the gradient contribution interface the principal attack surface. Six attack classes structure the threat: direct Byzantine contribution, Sybil attacks, model replacement, backdoor installation via gradient poisoning, coordinated multi-round attacks, and targeted attacks in peer-to-peer variants. Adversary fraction is the principal defensive parameter: methods like Krum and Bulyan provide strong robustness up to ~33% Byzantine, median and trimmed mean up to ~50%, FoolsGold tolerates higher Sybil fractions specifically. Byzantine- robust aggregation is the primary defensive layer; participant reputation tracking and gradient anomaly detection are secondary layers; differential privacy at the federated level provides combined privacy and bounding benefits. The privacy-security tradeoff is structural: federated learning trades data centralisation risk for gradient poisoning risk; the tradeoff is favourable when data sensitivity dominates and unfavourable otherwise. Deployment contexts (cross-silo, on-device, privacy-preserving collaboration, federated LLM training) have distinctive threat models and defensive architectures; Section 8.14 deliverables provide configurable infrastructure for the principal contexts. Federated learning extends the Chapter 8 framework to decentralised training; Section 8.15 production pipeline integrates federated defences with centralised defences (Sections 8.7-8.13) as configurable production tracks.

Key Takeaways

  • Federated learning shifts attack surface from data validation to gradient aggregation; defensive responsibility shifts correspondingly
  • Byzantine-robust aggregation is mandatory; FedAvg is inappropriate for production federated security
  • Aggregation method selection depends on assumed Byzantine fraction: Krum/Bulyan for ~33%, median/trimmed mean for ~50%, FoolsGold for Sybil-heavy
  • Sybil resistance is structurally required; Sybil amplification breaks Byzantine fraction assumptions otherwise
  • Layered defence: robust aggregation (per-round primary) + reputation (multi-round) + anomaly detection (per-round) + DP-SGD (privacy plus bounding)
  • Privacy-security tradeoff is structural; federated learning is privacy-improved but introduces gradient-poisoning surface
  • Multi-round behavioural analysis catches what per-round defences miss; cross-round monitoring is mandatory for sophisticated threat models
  • Deployment context (cross-silo vs on-device vs privacy-preserving vs federated LLM) drives threat model and defensive architecture
