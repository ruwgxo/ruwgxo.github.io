Section 9.13: Chapter 9 Summary

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Chapter 9 closes OWASP LLM10 across two defensive surfaces and three prior chapter arcs: threat model transition back to the gateway, four extraction goals, two privacy attacks, economic abuse, five detection primitives, an input-side capstone with per-user state and the Sigma rule, and an output- side capstone with cryptographic watermarking -- together bringing cumulative OWASP coverage to LLM01 plus LLM03 plus LLM10.

Why This Section Exists

Chapter 9 covered more ground than any prior chapter: a threat model shift, a four-goal extraction taxonomy, two privacy attacks sharing the inference surface, an adjacent economic abuse class, five detection primitives, two capstone sections, and a real-world incident arc grounding all of it in documented fact. Section 9.13 synthesises these into three reference artefacts the reader will use after the chapter ends: the extraction defence architecture diagram showing the full two-surface control stack, the Chapter 9 taxonomy reference card covering all seven attack classes with their detection primitives, and the cumulative LLM01 plus LLM03 plus LLM10 coverage map showing how Chapters 6 through 9 compose into a unified inference-time and training-time defensive architecture. The summary also names the five cross-cutting threads that connect Chapter 9 internally and forward to Chapter 10 -- the architectural lessons that apply beyond this specific attack class. These threads are the book''s mechanism for preventing each chapter from becoming a siloed case study and ensuring that accumulated knowledge transfers to new attack classes.

Sections 9.1 and 9.2 in Review: Threat Model Transition and Extraction Taxonomy

Body

Chapter 9 opened with a deliberate architectural reset. Chapter 8 had trained the reader to think in batch jobs and CI/CD gates -- the detection architecture for training-time poisoning. Section 9.1 reversed that: the threat actor in Chapter 9 is an API consumer with a query budget, the attack surface is the deployed model''s inference API, and the detection architecture returns to the real-time per-request gateway of Chapters 6 and 7. The adversary access ladder (black-box, grey-box, white-box) established the extraction budget economics that calibrate every rate limit in Section 9.11. The critical insight was that grey-box APIs providing probability distributions give adversaries a 5 to 50x query budget discount -- output design is a security decision, not merely a product decision. Section 9.2 built the extraction taxonomy: four goals, each with distinct adversary objectives, query patterns, and detection primitives. Functionality extraction builds a deployable clone across the full input distribution -- the dominant commercial threat. Fidelity extraction targets a specific input set with minimal query budget. Decision-boundary extraction maps the classification surface for adversarial example generation -- the connection to Chapter 10. Prompt extraction reconstructs the system prompt and operational configuration at orders-of-magnitude lower query budget than the other three goals. The taxonomy''s operational value: it prevents undifferentiated defences. A volume threshold alone cannot distinguish a distillation campaign from a boundary extraction campaign from prompt extraction from legitimate high- volume use. The taxonomy drives the five-primitive detection ensemble that covers each goal with a dedicated signal.

Sections 9.3, 9.4, and 9.5 in Review: The Three Extraction Attacks

Body

Section 9.3 established the foundational extraction primitive: black-box decision boundary mapping. Every API query reveals which side of the classification boundary its input falls on. Linear classifiers extract in O(d) queries via equation-solving -- a query budget achievable within a day of normal API use, far below any throughput-based rate limit. Decision trees extract via path-finding at comparable budgets. The detection insight from this section -- extraction queries cluster near decision boundaries while benign queries cluster in high-density input regions -- drove the boundary query detector that Section 9.10 implemented and Section 9.11 wired into Track 6 of the gateway. Section 9.4 scaled the boundary mapping primitive into the dominant commercial attack: knowledge distillation repurposed adversarially. The soft-label versus hard-label distinction is the chapter''s central practical security insight: soft labels carry log2(K) times as much boundary information as hard labels for a K-class classifier. At typical API pricing, this translates to a grey-box extraction campaign costing under $1,000 producing surrogate fidelity that would require $50,000 to $500,000 to train from scratch. Distributed campaigns split query budgets across accounts to evade per-user monitoring -- the evasion pattern that motivated the cross-user collusion detector and output watermarking. Section 9.5 covered prompt extraction at scale: the aggregation attack that reconstructs system prompts from many individually innocuous queries. The Chapter 6 injection classifier catches direct elicitation. The Section 9.11 cumulative query monitor catches the aggregation pattern -- 20 to 500 queries spanning the model''s behavioural constraints, contact details, and content restrictions. Per-query leakage reduction, not zero leakage, is the achievable defence goal; output perturbation targets this metric.

Title

Sections 9.6 and 9.7 in Review: Privacy Attacks Sharing the Inference Surface

Body

Sections 9.6 and 9.7 introduced the two privacy attack classes that share the inference-time API surface with the four extraction goals. Both exploit the same property Chapter 1 introduced as a generalisation problem: a model that memorises its training data responds to training examples differently than to unseen inputs drawn from the same distribution. Membership inference (Section 9.6) exploits the confidence and loss gap between training members and non-members. The shadow model attack (Shokri et al. 2017) trains meta-classifiers on known-membership confidence vectors from shadow models, achieving 73 to 95 percent attack accuracy on overfit models. This is a HIPAA breach when the model was trained on protected health information and a GDPR right-to-erasure violation when the training data is EU personal data. Differential privacy training from Section 8.13 is the upstream defence with a formal bound; output perturbation from Section 9.12 is the downstream signal-reduction control. Model inversion (Section 9.7) goes further: it reconstructs training data content from model outputs, not merely membership status. Carlini et al. 2021 demonstrated verbatim recovery of thousands of GPT-2 training sequences, including PII, using perplexity-based membership scoring followed by systematic generation. The two privacy attacks are kept in Chapter 9 rather than split into a separate chapter because they share defensive primitives with the four extraction goals: rate limiting, query pattern monitoring, output perturbation, and differential privacy training.

Title

Section 9.8 in Review: API Economic Abuse as the Adjacent Threat Class

Body

Section 9.8 completed the Chapter 9 attack arc with the class that targets operational viability rather than intellectual property. Cost amplification engineers queries to maximise inference cost through context padding and verbose output demands, achieving 50 to 360 times the average query cost within model token limits. Denial-of-wallet sustains this amplification until the operator''s budget is exhausted; a free-tier deployment can be drained in hours with zero adversary spend. Rate-limit exhaustion distributes minimal queries across accounts to block legitimate users without generating per-user alerts. The adversary-operator cost asymmetry is structurally unlimited for free-tier deployments and 10 to 250 times for paid-tier deployments. This asymmetry drives the rate limit policy design: a limit that makes attack cost exceed the commercial alternative is a meaningful deterrent; a throughput-only limit provides no deterrent for the cost-amplification pattern. Economic abuse and extraction share the query volume and user concentration detection primitives -- the same monitoring infrastructure covers both threat classes. The adaptive rate limiter''s four-dimension policy (requests per minute, tokens per minute, cost per hour, input tokens per request) addresses all three economic abuse forms and all four extraction goals from a single unified enforcement layer.

Section 9.9 in Review: Documented Incidents Ground the Defence Architecture

Body

Section 9.9 grounded the attack catalogue in three documented incident classes. The Tramer et al. 2016 commercial MLaaS extractions demonstrated that the theoretical extraction bounds are achievable against production APIs using query counts indistinguishable from normal API use. The Carlini et al. 2021 GPT-2 extraction demonstrated verbatim training data recovery including PII using a modest query budget against a then-major commercial deployment. The Alpaca and Vicuna distillation campaigns demonstrated that a few hundred dollars in API fees produce functional capability clones of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, with query distributions analytically indistinguishable from legitimate evaluation use. All three incidents shared five root causes: no query pattern monitoring, throughput-only rate limits, no output watermarking, soft-label outputs without perturbation, and no differential privacy training. These five root causes map precisely to the five controls that Sections 9.10 through 9.12 build. The detection opportunity map showed that input-side controls catch the Tramer et al. and Carlini et al. patterns; watermarking is the only control that addresses the Alpaca and Vicuna pattern post-hoc. The lesson: neither surface alone provides complete coverage; both are required.

Section 9.10 in Review: The Detection Primitive Library

Body

Section 9.10 implemented the five detection primitives as a software library that Sections 9.11 and 9.12 import. The query pattern analyser tracks query diversity, boundary proximity, and meta-query rate across a per-user sliding window. The OOD detector uses isolation forest trained on legitimate traffic to score incoming queries against the deployment''s expected input distribution. The boundary query detector identifies opposite-label near-neighbour pairs in the user''s query embedding and label history using a k-d tree index. The output entropy monitor scores per-response information leakage risk and doubles as the economic abuse token-count anomaly detector. The user concentration analyser measures per-user semantic clustering relative to the legitimate user population''s 95th percentile. The empirical comparison harness (Deliverable 6 of Section 9.10) measured the ensemble''s detection rates across synthetic attack families: 95 to 98 percent for equation-solving extraction, 88 to 93 percent for prompt extraction and distillation, and 30 to 40 percent for benchmark-distribution LLM distillation. That 30 to 40 percent gap is the architectural finding that drives Section 9.12: the benchmark-distribution pattern is definitionally undetectable at the input side, so the output-side watermark is the only control with post-hoc coverage for this attack class.

Title

Sections 9.11 and 9.12 in Review: The Two-Surface Production Architecture

Body

Section 9.11 wired the detection library into the Chapter 6/7 gateway as Track 6: the first stateful detection track in the book. Per-user state stores rolling windows of query embeddings, labels, and cost history that persist across sessions. The adaptive rate limiter enforces four independent policy dimensions calibrated against extraction budget economics rather than infrastructure throughput. The cross-user collusion detector identifies distributed campaigns by clustering semantically similar queries across accounts within time windows. The AML.T0040/T0024 Sigma rule was completed from the Chapter 5 stub, routing extraction alerts to the SIEM. The 8-item deployment checklist ensures production readiness before traffic is routed through Track 6. Section 9.12 added the output-side defensive surface that no prior chapter required. The watermarker embeds a cryptographic signal in every response using HMAC-based green-token vocabulary partitioning; the signal transfers to student models trained on watermarked outputs because the student learns the teacher''s green-token preference. The z-score statistical test confirms watermark presence at p < 0.00003 for z ≥ 4.0. The output perturbator applies calibrated noise through Gaussian, top-k, rounding, or temperature shaping modes; adaptive perturbation scales noise strength with the query monitor''s risk score. Together, Sections 9.11 and 9.12 close OWASP LLM10 across all five sub-risks.

Title

Integration: How Chapter 9 Extends the Gateway Architecture

Body

Chapter 9''s relationship to the prior chapter arc is architectural, not just topical. The Chapter 6 production injection classifier established the gateway pattern: a synchronous per-request analysis stage that runs within the API''s latency budget and returns a routing decision. Chapter 7 extended this to a five-track parallel ensemble. Chapter 9 extends it to a six-track ensemble with one structural difference: Track 6 is stateful. The stateful extension required four engineering decisions that were not present in Chapters 6 or 7. First, the state store architecture: per-user rolling windows backed by a distributed cache with TTL-based eviction. Second, the async write pattern: state updates are fire-and-forget to avoid adding write latency to the critical path. Third, the fallback-to-stateless policy: if the state store is unavailable, the gateway continues with Tracks 1 through 5 rather than failing the request. Fourth, the one-request lag acceptance: Track 6 scores from the previous request''s state, which is acceptable because extraction detection operates on distributions across many queries rather than on individual query content. Chapter 8''s offline auditing pipeline stands in architectural contrast to all of this. The auditing pipeline processes training batches asynchronously with a minutes-to-hours latency budget; the gateway ensemble processes individual requests synchronously with a 50ms P99 budget. Both architectures are correct for their respective threat classes -- the error is applying the wrong architecture to a threat, not choosing one over the other.

Title

Five Cross-Cutting Threads

Body

Five threads run through Chapter 9 and extend into Chapter 10 and beyond. Thread 1: Ensemble defence is structurally mandatory. No single detection primitive covers the full attack catalogue. The five-primitive ensemble is not a performance optimisation -- it is a coverage requirement. Any reduction to fewer primitives creates a detection gap for specific attack classes. This thread applies to every multi-technique threat class the book covers. Thread 2: Query budget economics is the design language for extraction defence. Rate limits, detection thresholds, and perturbation strength are all calibrated against query budgets required for specific extraction attacks, not against abstract security intuitions. A control that triples the required query budget is more valuable than one that blocks 50 percent of queries. Thread 3: Output-side defence is a new and underinvested surface. Prior to Chapter 9, all defensive controls in the book operated on inputs. Chapter 9 introduces the output-side stage as a first-class architectural component. Future threat classes -- particularly LLM capability misuse and data exfiltration via model outputs -- will require further output-side controls that Chapter 9''s watermarker and perturbator architecture generalises. Thread 4: Honest acknowledgement of defensive limits. The 30 to 40 percent input-side detection rate for benchmark-distribution distillation is a known limit, not a failure to be concealed. Watermark robustness degrades under paraphrasing. Perturbation is friction, not a barrier. DP training has a utility-privacy tradeoff that tightens at low epsilon. The defence is maximally effective when its limits are acknowledged and compensating controls are explicitly deployed. Thread 5: The Chapter 9 to Chapter 10 connection is mechanistic. Decision- boundary maps produced by Section 9.3 boundary extraction are the input to Chapter 10 adversarial example generation. An adversary who has completed a boundary extraction campaign against a classifier is positioned to generate adversarial examples against it. Chapter 10 builds on Chapter 9''s outputs; the detection architecture extends the six-track gateway with an adversarial example detection track.

Title

Quick Reference: Decision Trees, Primitive Selection, and Defence Stacking

Body

Three quick-reference tools synthesise Chapter 9 for post-chapter use. Extraction goal decision tree. An observed query pattern is classified by answering three questions in sequence. First: are queries broadly distributed across the input space? If yes, functionality extraction is the primary hypothesis; deploy query diversity monitor and rate limiter. Second: are queries concentrated in a narrow semantic region? If yes, fidelity or prompt extraction is the hypothesis; deploy user concentration analyser and prompt reconstruction track. Third: are opposite-label near-neighbour pairs present in the query history? If yes, decision-boundary extraction is the hypothesis; deploy boundary query detector and OOD detector. Detection primitive selection guide. If the deployment is a classifier with soft-label output, the output entropy monitor provides the highest-value additional signal over the default ensemble. If the deployment is an LLM with benchmark-distribution traffic patterns, output watermarking is the only control with coverage for the dominant extraction pattern. If the deployment faces prompt extraction risk (commercial system prompt with IP value), the cumulative query monitor''s prompt reconstruction track is the critical primitive, not the per-request injection classifier. Defence stacking pattern. The minimum viable defence stack for any production LLM API deployment has three layers. Layer 1: hard label output design (return hard labels; remove soft labels unless required). Layer 2: input- side monitoring (query monitor with calibrated primitives; extraction-aware rate limiter; cross-user correlator). Layer 3: output-side protection (watermarker with HSM-stored key; adaptive perturbator calibrated against utility validation). Each layer independently reduces extraction risk; all three together provide the depth the attack catalogue requires.

Title

Chapter 10 Preview: Adversarial Machine Learning

Body

Chapter 10 covers the inference-time threat class that Chapter 9''s boundary
mapping enables: adversarial example generation. The connection is mechanistic.
A complete boundary extraction campaign (Section 9.3) produces a map of the
model''s classification surface. Adversarial example generation uses that
map to construct inputs that the model misclassifies with high confidence --
inputs that sit on or just across the decision boundary from their true class.

The canonical adversarial attack methods -- FGSM (Fast Gradient Sign Method),
PGD (Projected Gradient Descent), and C&W (Carlini-Wagner) -- require gradient
access for full efficiency but work in approximate form under black-box access
using the boundary map that Chapter 9 provides. Transferability extends the
threat: adversarial examples generated against a surrogate model trained by
distillation (Section 9.4) transfer to the victim with substantial success
rate, because the surrogate shares the victim''s decision boundary structure.

Chapter 10 extends the six-track gateway with Track 7: adversarial example
detection. The detection primitives are different from the Chapter 9 set --
adversarial examples are typically detected by input preprocessing (feature
squeezing, spatial smoothing), by classifier consistency checks (the model''s
confidence should not change dramatically under small perturbations of the
input), and by comparison against a separately trained detection model. The
architecture is the same: a parallel detection track within the gateway
ensemble, stateless per-request, within the 50ms P99 budget.

Chapter 10 closes the OWASP coverage for the inference-time evasion attack
class and completes Part 2 of the book. Chapters 11 through 17 extend the
two-surface defensive architecture to the remaining OWASP LLM categories.

Chapter 9 returned to the inference-time gateway after Chapter 8''s training-time auditing, facing a new threat actor: the API consumer with a query budget.
