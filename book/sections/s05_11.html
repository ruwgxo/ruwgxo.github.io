Title

Membership Inference: The Confidence-Gap Mechanism

Content

the_binary_question: | Membership inference attacks (MIA) answer a single binary question: was a specific data record used to train this model? The GDPR and data-protection implications are severe. If an adversary can determine that a specific patient's medical record, a specific user's behavioural data, or a specific employee's performance review was used to train a deployed model, they can: (a) Confirm undisclosed personal data processing — a regulatory violation (b) Build a profile of what data the organisation holds and processes (c) Target individuals confirmed to be in the training corpus for follow-on social engineering or extraction attacks The attack requires no model weights, no training data, and no special permissions — only black-box API access and a query budget. the_confidence_gap_mechanism: | Machine learning models optimise their predictions on training data. Gradient descent minimises loss on the training set — specifically, it drives the model toward high-confidence correct predictions on training examples. This creates a measurable gap between model behaviour on training set members (members) and held-out data (non-members): Members: model assigns high probability to the correct class model's loss on the record is low model is confident Non-members: model has not specifically optimised on this record model's loss may be higher model is less uniformly confident The confidence-gap signal is the core observable for membership inference. It is not always large or reliable — but when overfitting is present, the gap widens and the attack accuracy improves. overfitting_amplification: | Section 5.10.1 explained that overfitting amplifies memorisation. The same mechanism amplifies membership inference vulnerability. An overfit model has learned training data almost exactly — its confidence on training members approaches 1.0, while its confidence on non-members reflects genuine generalisation uncertainty. The gap between these two confidence distributions is the membership inference signal, and overfitting makes that gap as large as possible. Quantitative relationship (from Yeom et al. 2018): MIA advantage ≤ generalisation gap / 2 where generalisation gap = (train accuracy) − (test accuracy) A model with 99% train accuracy and 85% test accuracy has a 14% generalisation gap, bounding membership inference advantage at ≤ 7%. A perfectly generalised model (train acc = test acc) provides zero advantage to a membership inference attack. Implication for defenders: the best membership inference defence is a well-regularised, non-overfit model. Differential privacy provides the formal guarantee; regularisation provides the practical approximation. three_signal_types: signal_01_confidence_score: | Model's output probability for the true class. Members: typically higher than non-members. Accessible via: softmax output vector from API. Limitation: unreliable when model is well-calibrated. signal_02_loss_value: | Cross-entropy loss on the correct label = -log(P(correct_class)). Members: lower loss. Accessible via: compute from API confidence scores. More stable signal than raw confidence. signal_03_prediction_entropy: | Shannon entropy of the full output probability vector. Members: lower entropy (model more certain). Non-members: higher entropy (model more uncertain). Accessible via: full softmax vector from API (if exposed).

Shokri et al. (2017): Shadow Model Attack Architecture

Content

paper_context: | "Membership Inference Attacks Against Machine Learning Models" by Reza Shokri, Marco Stronati, Congzheng Song, and Vitaly Shmatikoff (IEEE S&P 2017) introduced the shadow model attack — the foundational membership inference methodology that demonstrated black-box MIA is practical against real ML APIs including Google Prediction API and AWS ML. The attack requires no knowledge of the target model's architecture, training data, or weights. shadow_model_concept: | The fundamental challenge for black-box MIA: how does an adversary build a membership classifier without access to the target model's training data? The shadow model approach resolves this with an elegant proxy: The adversary cannot observe how the target model was trained, but they can: (1) Obtain or generate data from a similar distribution (2) Train their own model (the shadow model) on that data (3) Query the shadow model with both training members and non-members (4) Record: (query confidence vector, member/non-member label) (5) Train a membership classifier on these labelled examples (6) Apply the membership classifier to target model outputs This works because shadow models trained on similar distributions develop similar confidence gap patterns as the target model. The membership classifier learns to recognise the signature of "this record was in training data" without ever seeing the target model's actual training data. attack_pipeline_in_detail: step_01_shadow_data_acquisition: | Adversary acquires data from the same distribution as the target's training data. Sources: public datasets from the same domain, data synthesis using a generative model, API-based collection from the target's domain. Quality matters: shadow data that is too different from target distribution produces a poorly transferable membership classifier. step_02_shadow_model_training: | Train k shadow models (Shokri et al. used k=100, practical attacks use k=10) on subsets of the shadow data. Each shadow model is trained on roughly half the shadow data — this provides training members (in) and non-members (out) for the membership classifier's training set. Architecture: same type as suspected target (or similar complexity). Training: full training until overfitting — intentionally overfit shadow models produce larger confidence gaps and better membership classifiers. step_03_membership_label_collection: | For each shadow model k: For each record r in shadow data: Query shadow model k with r Record: (confidence_vector, "in" if r in shadow_k_train else "out") This produces a labelled dataset: (confidence_vector → member/non-member) Dataset size: k shadow models × n shadow data records × 2 labels With k=10 and n=1000: 10,000 labelled (confidence, membership) pairs step_04_attack_classifier_training: | Train a binary classifier (logistic regression or small MLP) on the labelled membership dataset from Step 3. Input: confidence vector output by the model when queried with a record Output: binary prediction (member / non-member) This classifier learns to distinguish the confidence-gap signature. step_05_target_model_inference: | For any record the adversary wants to test against the target model: Query target model with the record Extract confidence vector from target model output Apply attack classifier to confidence vector Output: (member / non-member) prediction with confidence score No additional queries to the target required beyond this one query per record. attack_accuracy_empirical_results: | Shokri et al. (2017) results against commercial MLaaS APIs: Google Prediction API: 79.4% membership inference accuracy Amazon ML: 68.6% accuracy Against intentionally overfit models: 92%+ accuracy Baseline comparison: random guessing on a 50/50 split = 50% accuracy. Attack accuracy of 80% represents 30% above random — substantial advantage. practical_limitations: | Shadow model attack accuracy degrades when: - Shadow data distribution diverges significantly from target distribution - Target model is well-regularised (small generalisation gap) - Target model uses output perturbation or confidence score masking - Target model does not expose full softmax vector (top-k only) These limitations motivate the defences in Section 5.11.6.

LLM Membership Inference: Likelihood Ratio Testing

Content

why_shadow_models_are_insufficient_for_llms: | The Shokri shadow model attack requires training k shadow models of the same type as the target. For large language models (7B-70B parameters), training even one shadow model is computationally prohibitive for most adversaries. The shadow model approach is practical for classification models; it is not the primary approach for LLM membership inference. likelihood_ratio_mia_for_llms: | Carlini et al. (2022) and subsequent work established the likelihood-ratio test as the standard black-box LLM MIA. It requires only: - Access to the target LLM's token log-probabilities (or a proxy) - Access to a reference model (any similar-scale publicly available LLM) The test works on the insight that training members are assigned high likelihood by the target model specifically because they were in training — but they may also be intrinsically high-likelihood text (e.g. common phrases that any LLM would assign high probability). The reference model controls for intrinsic likelihood, isolating the membership signal. likelihood_ratio_formula: | For a text sequence x and target model T and reference model R: LR(x) = log P_T(x) − log P_R(x) = Σ_t [log P_T(x_t | x_{<t}) − log P_R(x_t | x_{<t})] Interpretation: LR(x) >> 0: target model assigns much higher likelihood than reference → likely training member (target-specific high confidence) LR(x) ≈ 0: both models agree on likelihood → inconclusive LR(x) << 0: reference model assigns higher likelihood than target → likely non-member, or member of reference model's training Decision rule: classify x as member if LR(x) > threshold τ τ is calibrated on a small labelled validation set when available, or set to 0 when no validation data exists. min_k_variant: | A further improvement by Carlini et al. (2022): instead of averaging log-probabilities over the entire sequence, use the minimum log-probability token. Rationale: for memorised sequences, even the hardest tokens (those most likely to deviate from general language patterns) are assigned high probability by the target model. The min-k approach focuses on these "hardest" tokens where the membership signal is strongest. MIN_K(x) = mean of the k lowest per-token log-probabilities Classification: if MIN_K_T(x) − MIN_K_R(x) > τ → member reference_model_selection: | The reference model should be: - Similar architecture and scale to the target - Trained on similar general data but NOT on the sensitive fine-tuning data - Publicly available (GPT-2, Llama-2-7B, Mistral-7B etc.) For attacking a model fine-tuned on clinical data: use the base model (before fine-tuning) as the reference. The fine-tuning step creates the membership gap for fine-tuning examples; the base model provides the baseline. high_risk_target_scenarios: scenario_01_clinical_trials: | A pharmaceutical company fine-tunes an LLM on unpublished clinical trial data to build a medical literature assistant. Patient records, adverse event reports, and dosage outcomes are in the fine-tuning corpus. Adversary: competitor company or plaintiff attorney. MIA target: specific patient IDs or trial records. GDPR implication: trial participants did not consent to AI training. MIA outcome: confirmation of training data composition without data access. scenario_02_financial_records: | A hedge fund fine-tunes an LLM on proprietary trade data and internal research reports. Competitor seeks to confirm what data the model was trained on. MIA target: specific company names, trade dates, or financial figures. MIA outcome: reveals IP composition of the model without code or weight access. scenario_03_legal_documents: | A law firm deploys an LLM fine-tuned on client contracts and case files (with client consent for the firm's internal use). Opposing counsel or investigator queries the API to determine if specific case files were in training. MIA outcome: reveals attorney-client privileged material was processed by AI. scenario_04_user_behavioural_data: | A consumer platform fine-tunes a recommendation LLM on user interaction history. Regulator or researcher queries whether a specific user's data was used to train the deployed model. MIA outcome: confirms individual's data was processed — GDPR Article 22 profiling / automated decision-making implications.

Model Inversion Attacks: Reconstructing Training Samples

Content

what_model_inversion_is: | Model inversion attacks reconstruct training data from model outputs. While membership inference asks "was this record in training?", model inversion asks "what training records can I recover?" Inversion is the most powerful privacy attack: it produces approximate copies of actual training examples rather than binary membership signals. The tradeoff: inversion is harder and typically requires more access than membership inference — though black-box variants exist. access_level_taxonomy: white_box_inversion: access_required: "Model weights and architecture — gradient computation" power: "Highest — direct gradient signal for reconstruction optimisation" primary_target: "Open-weight models; insider threats" example: "Gradient-based image reconstruction from image classifier" grey_box_inversion: access_required: "Full softmax probability vector" power: "High — probability landscape guides reconstruction" primary_target: "APIs exposing full confidence vectors" example: "Confidence-guided reconstruction of face images" black_box_inversion: access_required: "Top-k class labels only" power: "Lower — limited signal, requires more queries" primary_target: "Standard classification APIs" example: "Label-only reconstruction with surrogate gradient estimation" fredrikson_et_al_2015_face_reconstruction: | The foundational model inversion paper: "Model Inversion Attacks that Exploit Confidence Information and Basic Countermeasures" (Fredrikson, Jha, Ristenpart, 2015). Setup: a face recognition classifier trained on celebrity face images. The classifier outputs a confidence vector over N celebrities. Attack: (1) Optimisation target: find input image x* that maximises classifier confidence for target person t: x* = argmax_x P(t | x) (2) Gradient computation: use backpropagation through the classifier to compute ∇_x P(t | x) — the gradient of confidence with respect to the input pixel values (3) Gradient ascent iteration: x_{n+1} = x_n + α × ∇_x P(t | x_n) + λ × prior_gradient where prior_gradient regularises toward natural image statistics (4) Convergence: after ~1000-10000 iterations, x* is an approximate reconstruction of training images of person t Result: the reconstructed images were recognisably similar to actual training images of the target person, demonstrating that the classifier's confidence surface encodes training data in recoverable form. Key insight: the classifier's confidence landscape is not arbitrary — it is shaped by training data. Gradient ascent navigating that landscape recovers the shape of the data that created it. llm_inversion_via_gradient_leakage: | The LLM equivalent of face reconstruction: federated_learning_gradient_attack: | In federated learning settings, clients send gradient updates to a central server. Zhu et al. (2019) demonstrated that these gradient updates encode the client's training data — the original training batch can be reconstructed from the gradient update alone using a gradient-matching optimisation. Relevance: organisations using federated fine-tuning of LLMs expose their private documents through gradient updates. prompt_inversion_from_outputs: | A softer form of inversion: given an LLM's output on a series of queries, reconstruct the system prompt or fine-tuning document that would produce those outputs. Applicable when the target document is not verbatim recalled (Section 5.10) but still influences model behaviour in recoverable ways. embedding_inversion: | Text embeddings encode semantic content. Given the embedding of a document, researchers have demonstrated partial reconstruction of the original text (Morris et al. 2023 — "Text Embeddings Reveal (Almost) As Much As Text"). Any API exposing text embeddings leaks partial document content through the embedding. inversion_risk_quantification: | Inversion risk is highest when: (1) Model exposes full probability/confidence vectors (not just top-k) (2) Model is fine-tuned on a small, high-value dataset (3) Model architecture and weights are accessible (open-weight or insider) (4) Training data has low entropy / high structure (faces, formatted records) (5) API does not rate-limit or perturb outputs Inversion risk is lowest when: (1) Only top-1 prediction exposed (no confidence scores) (2) DP training applied (confidence landscape smoothed) (3) Rate limiting prevents iterative optimisation (4) Output perturbation adds noise to confidence vectors

Measuring Your Own Exposure: Quantifying MIA Risk

Content

why_self_assessment_matters: | Defenders can run exactly the same membership inference tests that adversaries would run, against their own models, before deployment. Self-assessment provides: (a) Empirical MIA accuracy — the true positive rate an adversary achieves (b) Loss-gap measurement — quantifies overfitting as a privacy proxy (c) Evidence for GDPR Article 25 (data protection by design) compliance (d) Calibration signal for defence deployment thresholds loss_gap_metric: | The simplest MIA risk quantifier. Measures the gap between training loss and validation loss: LOSS_GAP = mean_loss(training_set) − mean_loss(validation_set) Interpretation: LOSS_GAP < 0.05: Low MIA risk — model generalises well 0.05 – 0.20: Medium — monitor; consider regularisation > 0.20: High — substantial MIA vulnerability; apply DP or retrain > 0.50: Critical — severe overfitting; MIA near-certain for members For LLMs: loss = cross-entropy per token. A fine-tuned model with training loss 0.8 and validation loss 1.6 has a gap of 0.8 — a strong overfitting signal with corresponding MIA risk. attack_accuracy_as_risk_metric: | Run the likelihood-ratio attack against your own model: - Collect a labelled set: 200 training examples (members) + 200 held-out examples (non-members) - Compute LR score for each: log P_target(x) − log P_reference(x) - Compute AUC-ROC of LR score as a classifier for member/non-member - Report: AUC, TPR@FPR=10%, TPR@FPR=1% Interpretation: AUC = 0.50: no MIA advantage (random guessing) AUC = 0.60: low-moderate risk AUC = 0.70: moderate-high risk — investigate regularisation AUC > 0.80: high risk — DP training required before deployment mia_risk_thresholds_by_data_type: medical_or_clinical_data: acceptable_auc: "<= 0.55" required_epsilon: "<= 2" rationale: "HIPAA / GDPR health data — highest sensitivity" financial_records: acceptable_auc: "<= 0.58" required_epsilon: "<= 4" rationale: "Financial regulation; individual transaction sensitivity" legal_documents: acceptable_auc: "<= 0.60" required_epsilon: "<= 4" rationale: "Attorney-client privilege; litigation risk" user_behavioural_data: acceptable_auc: "<= 0.62" required_epsilon: "<= 4" rationale: "GDPR profiling rules; user consent boundaries" general_proprietary_text: acceptable_auc: "<= 0.70" required_epsilon: "<= 8" rationale: "IP risk; lower individual privacy stakes"

Defences: Regularisation, Output Perturbation, and Confidence Masking

Content

defence_taxonomy: | Four complementary defence layers. Applying all four provides defence-in-depth; applying only one leaves residual attack surface. Order by implementation cost: Tier 1 (low cost, deploy immediately): D1 — Output quantisation and confidence masking D2 — Top-k output truncation Tier 2 (medium cost, deploy before production): D3 — Prediction vector perturbation D4 — Regularisation and early stopping Tier 3 (high cost, required for high-sensitivity data): D5 — Differential privacy training (Section 5.10.5) defence_01_output_quantisation_and_confidence_masking: mechanism: | Reduce confidence score resolution before returning to caller. Round probabilities to nearest 0.1 or 0.05 rather than returning full float precision. For binary/top-k outputs: return only the predicted class, not the confidence score. effect_on_mia: | Shadow model attack accuracy drops sharply when the confidence vector is quantised. Shokri et al. found that reducing confidence to 2 decimal places reduced attack accuracy by 10-20 percentage points on some targets. Full masking (label only) renders the shadow model attack nearly ineffective for the confidence-vector-dependent attack variant. tradeoff: | Users lose confidence calibration information. Applications that require uncertainty quantification (medical AI, safety-critical) cannot apply full masking. Use quantisation as a middle ground. implementation: | Python: round(confidence, 1) applied to all returned probabilities. For LLMs: do not expose full token log-probability vectors to untrusted callers. defence_02_top_k_truncation: mechanism: | Return only the top-k class labels (without scores) rather than the full probability vector. Standard for classification APIs: return "Cat: 0.9, Dog: 0.08" but not the full 1000-class softmax. effect_on_mia: | Reduces the signal available to the membership classifier. The shadow model must be trained on top-k data, producing a less accurate membership classifier than one trained on full vectors. tradeoff: | Loss of information for downstream applications. For most consumer applications returning top-1 or top-3 is already sufficient. implementation: | API layer: sort confidence vector, return top-k (name, score) pairs. LLMs: expose logprobs for only the N most probable tokens per position. defence_03_prediction_perturbation: mechanism: | Add calibrated random noise to the confidence vector before returning. The noise level is set to obscure the membership signal while preserving the rank ordering (the highest-confidence class remains highest). Two variants: Laplace mechanism: add Laplace(0, sensitivity/epsilon) noise per dimension Gaussian mechanism: add N(0, sigma) noise, project back to simplex effect_on_mia: | Perturbed confidence vectors reduce shadow model classifier accuracy. At moderate noise levels (σ ≈ 0.01-0.05), MIA accuracy drops 5-15%. At high noise levels (σ ≈ 0.1), MIA accuracy approaches random. tradeoff: | High noise reduces utility. For top-1 classification: utility is preserved as long as noise does not flip the argmax. Tune noise level to maintain top-1 accuracy while maximising privacy. implementation: | numpy: probs += np.random.laplace(0, scale, len(probs)) Project to probability simplex: probs = np.maximum(probs, 0) / probs.sum() defence_04_regularisation_and_early_stopping: mechanism: | Reduce generalisation gap (the root cause of MIA vulnerability) through: L2 weight decay: penalise large weights → reduces memorisation capacity Dropout: random neuron masking → prevents reliance on individual inputs Early stopping: halt training before full convergence → prevents overfit Label smoothing: soften target probabilities → reduces overconfidence effect_on_mia: | Each regularisation technique reduces the training-validation confidence gap. MIA advantage = generalisation gap / 2 (Yeom et al. 2018) — directly reduced. At high regularisation: model generalises better; MIA collapses toward random. tradeoff: | Regularisation can reduce task accuracy if applied too aggressively. Early stopping requires validation set — requires withholding training data. implementation: | PyTorch: optimizer = SGD(lr, weight_decay=1e-4) Dropout: nn.Dropout(p=0.1) in each layer Early stopping: monitor validation loss; stop when val_loss stops decreasing defence_05_combined_defence_effectiveness: no_defence: mia_auc: "0.75-0.85 (overfit model)" description: "Baseline — no protections" confidence_masking_only: mia_auc: "0.65-0.75" description: "10% improvement — low cost" masking_plus_perturbation: mia_auc: "0.58-0.68" description: "Further 7% improvement — moderate cost" masking_perturbation_regularisation: mia_auc: "0.52-0.62" description: "Close to random — recommended baseline" full_dp_plus_all_defences: mia_auc: "0.50-0.55" description: "Near-random — required for high-sensitivity data"

Likelihood-Ratio Membership Inference Attack + Risk Quantifier

Filename

mi_attack_tools.py

Purpose

Black-box membership inference attack using the likelihood-ratio test. Two main components: (1) LikelihoodRatioMIA: given target and reference model log-probabilities for a set of records, computes LR scores, decision threshold, AUC-ROC, TPR@FPR=10% and TPR@FPR=1%. (2) compute_loss_gap: computes training vs validation loss gap as a low-cost MIA risk proxy, with risk tier classification.

python

Code

"""
Membership Inference Attack Tools
LikelihoodRatioMIA + loss-gap risk quantifier + AUC evaluation.
"""

import math
import random
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Tuple, Dict, Optional


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Likelihood-Ratio MIA
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

@dataclass
class MIARecord:
    record_id: str
    is_member: bool              # ground truth (for evaluation)
    target_log_prob: float       # log P_target(x) — sum over tokens
    reference_log_prob: float    # log P_reference(x) — sum over tokens
    token_count: int

    @property
    def lr_score(self) -> float:
        """Likelihood ratio score (higher → more likely member)."""
        return self.target_log_prob - self.reference_log_prob

    @property
    def per_token_lr(self) -> float:
        return self.lr_score / max(1, self.token_count)


@dataclass
class MIAResult:
    n_members: int
    n_non_members: int
    auc_roc: float
    tpr_at_fpr_10: float
    tpr_at_fpr_01: float
    optimal_threshold: float
    risk_level: str
    interpretation: str
    records: List[MIARecord] = field(default_factory=list)


def _auc_roc(scores: List[float], labels: List[bool]) -> float:
    """Compute AUC-ROC using trapezoidal rule."""
    paired = sorted(zip(scores, labels), key=lambda x: -x[0])
    n_pos = sum(labels)
    n_neg = len(labels) - n_pos
    if n_pos == 0 or n_neg == 0:
        return 0.5
    tp = fp = 0
    auc = 0.0
    prev_fp = 0
    for _, label in paired:
        if label:
            tp += 1
        else:
            fp += 1
        if fp != prev_fp:
            auc += (tp / n_pos) * ((fp - prev_fp) / n_neg)
            prev_fp = fp
    return auc


def _tpr_at_fpr(scores: List[float], labels: List[bool],
                 target_fpr: float) -> float:
    """TPR at a given FPR level."""
    thresholds = sorted(set(scores), reverse=True)
    n_pos = sum(labels)
    n_neg = len(labels) - n_pos
    if n_pos == 0 or n_neg == 0:
        return 0.0
    best_tpr = 0.0
    for thresh in thresholds:
        tp = sum(1 for s, l in zip(scores, labels) if s >= thresh and l)
        fp = sum(1 for s, l in zip(scores, labels) if s >= thresh and not l)
        fpr = fp / n_neg
        tpr = tp / n_pos
        if fpr <= target_fpr:
            best_tpr = max(best_tpr, tpr)
    return best_tpr


def _optimal_threshold(scores: List[float], labels: List[bool]) -> float:
    """Threshold maximising balanced accuracy."""
    best_thresh = 0.0
    best_ba = 0.0
    n_pos = sum(labels)
    n_neg = len(labels) - n_pos
    if n_pos == 0 or n_neg == 0:
        return 0.0
    for thresh in sorted(set(scores)):
        tp = sum(1 for s, l in zip(scores, labels) if s >= thresh and l)
        tn = sum(1 for s, l in zip(scores, labels) if s < thresh and not l)
        ba = 0.5 * (tp / n_pos + tn / n_neg)
        if ba > best_ba:
            best_ba = ba
            best_thresh = thresh
    return best_thresh


def run_lr_mia(records: List[MIARecord],
               use_per_token: bool = True) -> MIAResult:
    """
    Run likelihood-ratio MIA on a set of labelled records.
    use_per_token=True normalises by token count (more robust).
    """
    scores = [r.per_token_lr if use_per_token else r.lr_score
              for r in records]
    labels = [r.is_member for r in records]
    n_members = sum(labels)
    n_non_members = len(labels) - n_members

    auc = _auc_roc(scores, labels)
    tpr10 = _tpr_at_fpr(scores, labels, 0.10)
    tpr01 = _tpr_at_fpr(scores, labels, 0.01)
    thresh = _optimal_threshold(scores, labels)

    risk = ("low" if auc < 0.60 else
            "medium" if auc < 0.70 else
            "high" if auc < 0.80 else "critical")

    interpretations = {
        "low":      "AUC near random — low MIA exposure; standard monitoring sufficient",
        "medium":   "Detectable MIA advantage — investigate regularisation; consider DP",
        "high":     "Significant MIA vulnerability — DP training required before production",
        "critical": "Severe overfitting — model must not be deployed on sensitive data without DP",
    }

    return MIAResult(
        n_members=n_members, n_non_members=n_non_members,
        auc_roc=auc, tpr_at_fpr_10=tpr10, tpr_at_fpr_01=tpr01,
        optimal_threshold=thresh, risk_level=risk,
        interpretation=interpretations[risk], records=records,
    )

def compute_loss_gap(train_losses: List[float],
                     val_losses: List[float]) -> LossGapReport:
    """
    Compute training vs validation loss gap as MIA risk proxy.
    train_losses: per-sample cross-entropy losses on training set
    val_losses:   per-sample cross-entropy losses on held-out set
    """
    train_loss = sum(train_losses) / len(train_losses)
    val_loss = sum(val_losses) / len(val_losses)
    gap = val_loss - train_loss   # positive if overfitting

    advantage_ub = max(0.0, gap / 2)
    risk = ("low" if gap < 0.05 else
            "medium" if gap < 0.20 else
            "high" if gap < 0.50 else "critical")

    recs = {
        "low":      "Loss gap within acceptable range. Monitor; no immediate action.",
        "medium":   "Moderate overfitting detected. Apply L2 regularisation and early stopping.",
        "high":     "Significant overfitting. Regularise or apply DP training before deployment.",
        "critical": "Severe overfitting. Model must NOT be deployed on sensitive data. Retrain with DP.",
    }
    return LossGapReport(
        train_loss=train_loss, val_loss=val_loss,
        loss_gap=gap, mia_advantage_upper_bound=advantage_ub,
        risk_tier=risk, recommendation=recs[risk],
    )


def print_loss_gap_report(r: LossGapReport) -> None:

Privacy Defence Layer: Output Perturbation and Confidence Masking

Filename

privacy_defence_layer.py

Purpose

Four defences against membership inference and model inversion: (1) confidence_mask: round probabilities to fixed decimal places (2) top_k_truncate: return only top-k (label, score) pairs (3) perturb_probs: add calibrated Laplace noise to softmax vector (4) label_smooth_outputs: apply label smoothing to reduce overconfidence Each defence is instrumented to show before/after MIA-signal reduction (entropy change, max-prob change) so defenders can tune parameters.

"""
Privacy Defence Layer — four MIA/inversion defences with before/after instrumentation.
"""

import math
import random
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import List, Dict, Tuple, Optional


@dataclass
class ConfidenceVector:
    labels: List[str]
    probs: List[float]

    def entropy(self) -> float:
        return -sum(p * math.log2(p + 1e-9) for p in self.probs)

    def max_prob(self) -> float:
        return max(self.probs)

    def top_k(self, k: int) -> List[Tuple[str, float]]:
        return sorted(zip(self.labels, self.probs),
                      key=lambda x: -x[1])[:k]

    def to_dict(self) -> Dict[str, float]:
        return dict(zip(self.labels, self.probs))

    def __repr__(self) -> str:
        top = sorted(zip(self.labels, self.probs), key=lambda x: -x[1])[:3]
        return f"ConfidenceVector(top3={[(l, round(p,4)) for l,p in top]})"


def _project_to_simplex(probs: List[float]) -> List[float]:
    """Project to probability simplex: clip to [0,1] and renormalise."""
    clipped = [max(0.0, p) for p in probs]
    total = sum(clipped)
    if total == 0:
        return [1.0 / len(clipped)] * len(clipped)
    return [p / total for p in clipped]


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Defence 1: Confidence Masking (Output Quantisation)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

def confidence_mask(cv: ConfidenceVector,
                    decimal_places: int = 1,
                    mask_below_threshold: float = 0.05) -> ConfidenceVector:
    """
    Round confidence scores to decimal_places. Zero out probs below threshold.
    Renormalise after masking.
    """
    masked = [round(p, decimal_places) if p >= mask_below_threshold else 0.0
              for p in cv.probs]
    projected = _project_to_simplex(masked)
    return ConfidenceVector(labels=cv.labels[:], probs=projected)


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Defence 2: Top-K Truncation
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

def top_k_truncate(cv: ConfidenceVector, k: int = 3) -> ConfidenceVector:
    """
    Return only top-k classes; zero out the rest.
    Renormalise remaining probabilities.
    """
    indexed = sorted(enumerate(cv.probs), key=lambda x: -x[1])
    kept_indices = {idx for idx, _ in indexed[:k]}
    truncated = [p if i in kept_indices else 0.0
                 for i, p in enumerate(cv.probs)]
    projected = _project_to_simplex(truncated)
    return ConfidenceVector(labels=cv.labels[:], probs=projected)


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Defence 3: Laplace Prediction Perturbation
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

def perturb_probs(cv: ConfidenceVector,
                   sensitivity: float = 1.0,
                   epsilon: float = 10.0,
                   seed: Optional[int] = None) -> ConfidenceVector:
    """
    Add Laplace(0, sensitivity/epsilon) noise to each probability.
    Renormalise to simplex.
    Higher epsilon = less noise = less privacy protection.
    """
    if seed is not None:
        random.seed(seed)
    scale = sensitivity / epsilon
    noisy = [p + _laplace_noise(scale) for p in cv.probs]
    projected = _project_to_simplex(noisy)
    return ConfidenceVector(labels=cv.labels[:], probs=projected)


def _laplace_noise(scale: float) -> float:
    """Sample from Laplace(0, scale) using inverse CDF method."""
    u = random.uniform(-0.5, 0.5)
    return -scale * (1 if u >= 0 else -1) * math.log(1 - 2 * abs(u) + 1e-9)


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Defence 4: Label Smoothing (for training)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

def label_smooth_outputs(cv: ConfidenceVector,
                          smoothing: float = 0.1) -> ConfidenceVector:
    """
    Apply label smoothing to model outputs: redistribute smoothing
    probability mass from the predicted class to all other classes.
    Mimics training with label smoothing — reduces overconfidence.
    """
    n = len(cv.probs)
    smoothed = [(1 - smoothing) * p + smoothing / n for p in cv.probs]
    return ConfidenceVector(labels=cv.labels[:], probs=smoothed)


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Instrumented Defence Pipeline
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

@dataclass
class DefenceReport:
    original_entropy: float
    original_max_prob: float
    after_defence: str
    defended_entropy: float
    defended_max_prob: float
    entropy_increase: float
    max_prob_reduction: float
    mia_signal_reduction_pct: float


def apply_and_measure(cv: ConfidenceVector,
                       defence_name: str,
                       defended_cv: ConfidenceVector) -> DefenceReport:
    orig_ent = cv.entropy()
    orig_max = cv.max_prob()
    def_ent = defended_cv.entropy()
    def_max = defended_cv.max_prob()
    # MIA signal is primarily in max_prob (confidence gap)
    signal_reduction = (orig_max - def_max) / orig_max * 100 if orig_max > 0 else 0.0
    return DefenceReport(
        original_entropy=orig_ent,
        original_max_prob=orig_max,
        after_defence=defence_name,
        defended_entropy=def_ent,
        defended_max_prob=def_max,
        entropy_increase=def_ent - orig_ent,
        max_prob_reduction=orig_max - def_max,
        mia_signal_reduction_pct=signal_reduction,
    )


def print_defence_report(r: DefenceReport) -> None:
    print(f"\n  Defence: {r.after_defence}")
    print(f"    Original:  entropy={r.original_entropy:.3f}  max_prob={r.original_max_prob:.4f}")
    print(f"    Defended:  entropy={r.defended_entropy:.3f}  max_prob={r.defended_max_prob:.4f}")
    print(f"    Δ entropy: +{r.entropy_increase:.3f}  |  max_prob reduction: {r.max_prob_reduction:.4f} ({r.mia_signal_reduction_pct:.1f}%)")


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Combined Defence Layer
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

def full_defence_pipeline(cv: ConfidenceVector,
                            top_k: int = 5,
                            decimal_places: int = 1,
                            noise_epsilon: float = 5.0,
                            label_smoothing: float = 0.05) -> ConfidenceVector:
    """
    Apply all four defences in recommended order:
    1. Top-K truncation (remove long tail)
    2. Label smoothing (reduce overconfidence)
    3. Confidence masking (reduce resolution)
    4. Laplace perturbation (add uncertainty)
    """
    cv1 = top_k_truncate(cv, k=top_k)
    cv2 = label_smooth_outputs(cv1, smoothing=label_smoothing)
    cv3 = confidence_mask(cv2, decimal_places=decimal_places)
    cv4 = perturb_probs(cv3, epsilon=noise_epsilon)
    return cv4

Combined Privacy Risk Audit Tool

from dataclasses import dataclass, field

THRESHOLDS = {
    "medical":   {"corpus_risk": 0.3, "loss_gap": 0.10, "mia_auc": 0.55, "epsilon_req": 2},
    "financial": {"corpus_risk": 0.3, "loss_gap": 0.15, "mia_auc": 0.58, "epsilon_req": 4},
    "legal":     {"corpus_risk": 0.3, "loss_gap": 0.15, "mia_auc": 0.60, "epsilon_req": 4},
    "user_data": {"corpus_risk": 0.4, "loss_gap": 0.20, "mia_auc": 0.62, "epsilon_req": 4},
    "general":   {"corpus_risk": 0.5, "loss_gap": 0.30, "mia_auc": 0.70, "epsilon_req": 8},
}

GDPR_ARTICLES = {
    "article_5":  "Data minimisation — corpus PII density and deduplication",
    "article_25": "Privacy by design — regularisation, DP training, output defences",
    "article_30": "Records of processing — ML SBOM and training data provenance",
    "article_35": "DPIA — this audit report constitutes the technical DPIA evidence",
    "article_17": "Right to erasure — DP epsilon or machine unlearning capability",
}


def _risk_tier(value: float, threshold: float,
               lower_is_riskier: bool = False) -> str:
    if lower_is_riskier:
        ratio = threshold / max(value, 1e-9)
    else:
        ratio = value / max(threshold, 1e-9)
    return ("low" if ratio <= 0.7 else
            "medium" if ratio <= 1.0 else
            "high" if ratio <= 1.5 else "critical")


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Five-Stage Audit
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

def run_privacy_audit(
    model_id: str,
    data_type: str,
    corpus_risk_score: float,
    train_loss: float,
    val_loss: float,
    mia_auc: float,
    dp_epsilon: Optional[float] = None,
    defences_deployed: Optional[List[str]] = None,
) -> PrivacyAuditReport:

    thresh = THRESHOLDS.get(data_type, THRESHOLDS["general"])
    stages: List[AuditStageResult] = []
    defences_deployed = defences_deployed or []

    # Stage 1: Data classification (informational)
    stages.append(AuditStageResult(
        stage_id="S1", stage_name="Data Classification",
        metric_name="data_type", metric_value=0.0,
        threshold=0.0, passed=True, risk_tier="info",
        finding=f"Data type: {data_type}. Thresholds: {thresh}",
        recommendation="Ensure data type classification is accurate and reviewed by legal/compliance.",
    ))

    # Stage 2: Memorisation risk score
    cr_tier = _risk_tier(corpus_risk_score, thresh["corpus_risk"])
    stages.append(AuditStageResult(
        stage_id="S2", stage_name="Memorisation Risk Score",
        metric_name="corpus_risk_score", metric_value=corpus_risk_score,
        threshold=thresh["corpus_risk"],
        passed=corpus_risk_score <= thresh["corpus_risk"],
        risk_tier=cr_tier,

        recommendation=(
            "PASS — proceed." if corpus_risk_score <= thresh["corpus_risk"] else
            "Apply deduplication and PII pseudonymisation; rescore before training."
        ),
    ))

    # Stage 3: Loss-gap measurement
    loss_gap = val_loss - train_loss
    lg_tier = _risk_tier(loss_gap, thresh["loss_gap"])
    stages.append(AuditStageResult(
        stage_id="S3", stage_name="Loss-Gap Measurement",
        metric_name="loss_gap", metric_value=loss_gap,
        threshold=thresh["loss_gap"],
        passed=loss_gap <= thresh["loss_gap"],
        risk_tier=lg_tier,
        finding=f"Train loss {train_loss:.4f}, val loss {val_loss:.4f}, gap {loss_gap:.4f} vs threshold {thresh['loss_gap']:.2f}",
        recommendation=(
            "PASS — regularisation adequate." if loss_gap <= thresh["loss_gap"] else
            f"Reduce overfitting: increase L2 weight decay, add dropout, apply early stopping. "
            f"If gap > {thresh['loss_gap']*2:.2f}: DP training required."
        ),
    ))

    # Stage 4: MIA self-assessment (AUC-ROC)
    mia_tier = _risk_tier(mia_auc, thresh["mia_auc"])
    stages.append(AuditStageResult(
        stage_id="S4", stage_name="MIA Self-Assessment (LR Test)",
        metric_name="mia_auc", metric_value=mia_auc,
        threshold=thresh["mia_auc"],
        passed=mia_auc <= thresh["mia_auc"],

        finding=f"MIA AUC-ROC {mia_auc:.4f} vs threshold {thresh['mia_auc']:.2f} for {data_type} data",
        recommendation=(
            "PASS — MIA exposure within acceptable range." if mia_auc <= thresh["mia_auc"] else
            f"MIA AUC exceeds threshold. "
            f"Apply output perturbation defences. If AUC > {thresh['mia_auc']+0.1:.2f}: DP required."
        ),
    ))

    expected_defences = ["confidence_masking", "top_k_truncation",
                          "prediction_perturbation", "regularisation"]
    missing_defences = [d for d in expected_defences if d not in defences_deployed]
    dp_compliant = (dp_epsilon is not None and dp_epsilon <= thresh["epsilon_req"])
    def_passed = (len(missing_defences) == 0 and
                  (dp_compliant or data_type == "general"))
    stages.append(AuditStageResult(
        stage_id="S5", stage_name="Defence Deployment Verification",
        metric_name="defences_missing", metric_value=len(missing_defences),
        threshold=0.0,
        passed=def_passed,
        risk_tier="low" if def_passed else "high",
        finding=(
            f"Deployed: {defences_deployed}. Missing: {missing_defences}. "
            f"DP epsilon: {dp_epsilon} (required: ≤{thresh['epsilon_req']} for {data_type})"
        ),
        recommendation=(
            "All defences deployed." if def_passed else
            f"Deploy missing defences: {missing_defences}. "
            + (f"Apply DP training ε≤{thresh['epsilon_req']}." if not dp_compliant and data_type != "general" else "")
        ),
    ))

    # Overall risk
    tier_rank = {"info": 0, "low": 1, "medium": 2, "high": 3, "critical": 4}
    max_tier = max(stages, key=lambda s: tier_rank.get(s.risk_tier, 0)).risk_tier
    failed = [s for s in stages if not s.passed and s.stage_id != "S1"]

    deploy_recs = {
        "low":      "DEPLOY — privacy controls adequate. Monitor in production.",
        "medium":   "CONDITIONAL DEPLOY — address medium findings before or shortly after deployment.",
        "high":     "DO NOT DEPLOY — high-risk findings must be resolved before deployment.",
        "critical": "BLOCK DEPLOYMENT — critical privacy risk. Retrain with DP.",
        "info":     "DEPLOY",
    }

                   if not s.passed and s.stage_id != "S1"]

            for (art, desc), s in zip(GDPR_ARTICLES.items(), stages[1:])}

    return PrivacyAuditReport(
        model_id=model_id, data_type=data_type,
        stages=stages, overall_risk=max_tier,
        deploy_recommendation=deploy_recs.get(max_tier, "REVIEW"),
        gdpr_mapping=gdpr, remediation_priority=remediation,
    )

Key Takeaways

Takeaway 01

Membership inference requires only black-box API access. The likelihood-ratio test queries the target API once per record and computes LR(x) = log P_target(x) − log P_reference(x). No model weights, no training data, and no special permissions are required. Any adversary with API access can run this test against any deployed model.

Takeaway 02

Overfitting is the root cause of membership inference vulnerability. The Yeom et al. (2018) bound is clean: MIA advantage ≤ (generalisation gap) / 2. A perfectly generalised model gives zero MIA advantage. The best membership inference defence is a well-regularised model — differential privacy provides the formal guarantee, regularisation provides the practical approximation.

Takeaway 03

Shadow models work because the confidence-gap pattern transfers across models trained on the same distribution. The adversary does not need access to the target's training data — they train shadow models on similar data, collect (confidence_vector, member/non-member) labels, and train a classifier that transfers to the target API.

Takeaway 04

Model inversion is inverse ML: gradient ascent up the model's confidence landscape recovers the training data that shaped that landscape. The Fredrikson et al. (2015) face reconstruction demonstrated this against a production classifier. The LLM equivalent — gradient leakage in federated learning (Zhu et al. 2019) and prompt inversion from outputs — applies the same principle to language models.

Takeaway 05

Confidence score masking is a low-cost, immediate-deployment defence. Rounding output probabilities to one decimal place reduces shadow model attack accuracy by 10-20 percentage points. Returning top-1 labels only (no confidence scores) renders the confidence-vector MIA variant nearly ineffective. Deploy confidence masking before any model exposed to untrusted callers.

Takeaway 06

The AUC-ROC of the likelihood-ratio MIA against your own model is a defensible, auditable privacy metric. AUC = 0.50 means zero MIA advantage; AUC = 0.80 means substantial membership exposure. Running this test as part of pre-deployment evaluation produces quantitative GDPR Article 25 evidence (data protection by design) that regulators can audit.

Takeaway 07

The privacy attack triad (extraction + membership inference + inversion) defines the complete privacy threat model for models trained on sensitive data. Defenders who address only extraction (5.10) leave membership inference and inversion undefended. The combined privacy audit integrates all three into a single five-stage procedure with GDPR article mapping and per-data-type thresholds.

Takeaway 08

Fine-tuning amplifies all three privacy attacks simultaneously. A model fine-tuned on 100 internal documents with 50 epochs has near-certain memorisation (Section 5.10), a large loss gap (membership inference vulnerable), and a high-resolution confidence landscape (inversion-friendly). The mandatory controls for sensitive fine-tuning — data deduplication, DP training, output perturbation, regularisation — address all three attack surfaces in a single implementation.

Section Summary

One Paragraph

Section 5.11 completes the privacy attack triad begun in Section 5.10, covering membership inference and model inversion — the two attacks that answer "was this record in training?" and "what training records can I reconstruct?" respectively. The section opens by establishing the confidence-gap mechanism: gradient descent drives models to assign high confidence on training members, creating a measurable signal exploitable by any caller with API access. The quantitative relationship from Yeom et al. (2018) — MIA advantage ≤ generalisation gap / 2 — makes the connection to Chapter 1's overfitting treatment explicit and actionable: well- regularised models are inherently more resistant to membership inference. The Shokri et al. (2017) shadow model attack is examined in full: five-step pipeline from shadow data acquisition through membership classifier training and target inference, with empirical accuracy results (79% against Google Prediction API, 92% against intentionally overfit targets). The LLM-specific likelihood-ratio test provides a shadow-model-free black-box attack: LR(x) = log P_target(x) − log P_reference(x), requiring one query per record and a publicly available reference model. Four high-risk target scenarios receive detailed treatment: clinical trial data, financial records, legal documents, and user behavioural data — each with GDPR article implications and adversary profile. Model inversion covers gradient-based reconstruction (the Fredrikson et al. 2015 face reconstruction demonstration), LLM-specific inversion via gradient leakage in federated learning (Zhu et al. 2019), and embedding inversion (Morris et al. 2023). Self-assessment provides the loss-gap metric and MIA AUC thresholds by data type, enabling quantitative pre-deployment privacy risk certification. Four complementary defences are implemented and instrumented: confidence masking, top-k truncation, Laplace prediction perturbation, and regularisation — each measured by entropy increase and max-probability reduction. The section closes with a five-stage combined privacy risk audit integrating corpus risk scoring (5.10), loss-gap measurement, MIA self-assessment, and defence verification into a single GDPR-mapped audit procedure. Three implementations deliver the likelihood- ratio MIA with loss-gap quantifier, the four-defence privacy layer with instrumentation, and the combined privacy risk audit tool with GDPR article mapping.
