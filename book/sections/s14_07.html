Chapter 14 · Section 7 · ~7 pages

High Availability, Circuit Breakers, and Graceful Degradation

Section Overview

One Line Summary

High availability keeps the gateway serving through component failures: circuit breakers cut off a failing detector before it cascades, moving between closed, open, and half-open as they test recovery; graceful degradation sheds non-essential detectors under stress to keep the essential path serving; and bulkheads isolate failures so one detector cannot exhaust the shared resources the others need.

Why This Section Exists

Section 14.06 decided what a single failing detector does -- fail-open or fail-closed, per detector and per tier -- and classified detectors as essential or droppable. It stopped short of one thing: preventing a detector from failing repeatedly and dragging the gateway down with it. A policy that fails a detector closed on every request still calls the broken detector on every request, paying its latency and load each time, and a detector that hangs rather than errors can hold shared resources until the whole gateway stalls. Deciding the policy is necessary but not sufficient; the gateway also needs the mechanism that contains failure. This section builds it. The circuit breaker recognises a persistently failing detector and cuts it off -- short-circuiting calls rather than retrying into a cascade -- and moves through closed, open, and half-open states so a recovered detector is restored safely rather than flooded. Graceful degradation operates the essential-versus-droppable classification from 14.06, shedding non-essential detectors under stress in a defined order so the essential security path keeps serving. Bulkheads isolate each detector in its own resource pool so a slow or failing detector exhausts only its own threads and connections, not the gateway. Health checks and readiness decide when an instance should receive traffic at all. Together they turn a detector fault from a gateway outage into a bounded, recoverable event, which is what high availability means for a detection gateway.

Position In Chapter

Section 14.07 is the second of the two resilience sections (14.06-14.07) and the response to the availability concern from the 14.01 taxonomy. Where 14.06 set the failure policy, this section supplies the mechanism that enforces and contains it -- circuit breakers, graceful degradation, bulkheads, and health checks. It completes the resilience stage. With the gateway now fast, scalable, and resilient, the operability sections (14.08-14.09) make it observable, so the resilient behaviour engineered here can actually be seen, tuned, and trusted in production.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

From Failure Policy to Failure Containment

Body

Section 14.06 gave every detector a deliberate failure policy, and that closes one gap: a failing detector no longer takes an accidental path chosen by the bug. But it leaves another gap open. The policy says what to do each time a detector fails; it does not say what to do about a detector that fails every time. A broken detector under a fail-closed policy is still called on every single request, only to be failed closed after it errors or times out, so the gateway pays the detector cost -- its latency, its timeout wait, its load -- on every request for no benefit, and it does this indefinitely while the detector stays broken. Worse, some failures are not clean errors. A detector that hangs rather than raises does not return quickly to be failed by policy; it holds whatever resources it was using -- a thread, a connection, a slot in a pool -- for as long as it hangs. If many requests hit a hanging detector at once, they pile up holding resources, and those resources are often shared with the other detectors and the request path itself. A single hanging detector can in this way exhaust the resources the whole gateway needs, so one detector fault becomes a total stall. The failure policy has nothing to say about this, because it operates one request at a time and the problem is the accumulation across requests. High availability is the property of keeping the gateway serving through exactly these situations, and it requires mechanism, not just policy. The gateway needs to recognise a persistently failing detector and stop calling it; it needs to keep one detector failure from consuming the resources the others depend on; it needs to shed load intelligently when it cannot run everything; and it needs to decide when an instance is healthy enough to receive traffic. Those are the four mechanisms of this section -- circuit breakers, bulkheads, graceful degradation, and health checks -- and together they turn a detector fault from an outage into a bounded, recoverable event.

Part 2

Title

The Circuit Breaker and Its Three States

Body

The circuit breaker is the primary mechanism of high availability, and it is borrowed directly from its electrical namesake: when something downstream keeps faulting, the breaker trips and cuts the circuit rather than letting the fault keep drawing current. For a detector, tripping means the gateway stops calling it and instead short-circuits the call -- resolving it immediately by policy without touching the broken detector at all. The broken detector is thereby cut off before its repeated failures cascade into the wider gateway. The breaker has three states. In the closed state -- the normal one -- calls pass through to the detector and the breaker simply counts failures. When consecutive failures reach a configured threshold, the breaker trips to the open state. In the open state the detector is cut off: calls short-circuit without being made, so a broken detector is no longer retried on every request. The breaker stays open for a configured duration, long enough for a transient fault to clear, and then moves to the half-open state to test whether the detector has recovered. The half-open state is what makes recovery safe, and it is the subtle part of the design. A naive breaker would slam straight from open back to closed after the timeout and immediately send full traffic to a detector that may still be broken, causing it to trip again in a flapping loop. The half-open state avoids this by allowing only a limited number of trial calls: if they succeed, enough to meet a success threshold, the breaker concludes the detector has recovered and closes; if any trial fails, the breaker concludes the detector is still broken and re-opens immediately without flooding it. The circuit breaker deliverable for this section implements exactly this cycle -- closed to open on repeated failure, open to half-open after the timeout, half-open to closed on confirmed recovery or back to open on a failed trial -- so a broken detector is isolated while broken and restored only once it proves healthy.

Part 3

Title

Circuit Breakers and the Failure Policy Together

Body

The circuit breaker does not replace the failure policy of Section 14.06; it works with it. The policy decides how a call resolves when the detector cannot give a verdict, and the breaker decides whether to call the detector at all. When the breaker is open and a call short-circuits, the gateway still has a request to resolve, and it resolves it by the same per-detector policy the failing detector already carries. An essential detector whose breaker is open short-circuits to a fail-closed block, because the policy for that detector is fail-closed and being cut off does not change what it guards. A droppable detector whose breaker is open short-circuits to a skip -- it is simply not run -- which is fail-open by policy and is exactly graceful degradation in action. This composition is why the two sections are ordered as they are. Section 14.06 had to establish the policy first, because the breaker needs a policy to apply when it short-circuits. Without a policy, an open breaker would face the same undefined-behaviour problem the whole of 14.06 was written to solve. With the policy in place, the breaker is a clean addition: it changes when the detector is called, and defers to the existing policy for how the resulting request is resolved. The breaker also completes the story of the timeout that ran through 14.04 and 14.06. The timeout bounds one slow call; the policy resolves that one timed-out call; and the breaker notices when a detector times out repeatedly and stops calling it, so the gateway is not paying the full timeout wait on every request to a detector that is reliably slow. A single slow call is a latency problem solved by the timeout; a detector that is always slow is an availability problem solved by the breaker. The three mechanisms -- timeout, policy, breaker -- form a layered defence against a detector that does not behave, each handling a different timescale of the same underlying fault.

Part 4

Title

Graceful Degradation: Shedding to the Essential Path

Body

Circuit breakers handle detectors that break. Graceful degradation handles a different situation: the gateway is under so much stress that it cannot run every detector within budget even though none has individually broken. When there is not enough capacity to do everything, the gateway must choose what to stop doing, and graceful degradation is the discipline of choosing deliberately -- shedding the detectors that matter least first, so the ones that matter most keep running. This is where the essential-versus-droppable classification from Section 14.06 becomes operational. That section decided which detectors are essential -- the injection classifier, the jailbreak classifier, the output PII scanner, the security core -- and which are droppable -- the audit tagger, the session scorer, the batch analytics. This section uses that classification to define a shed order: under stress the gateway sheds the droppable detectors, in a defined priority order, freeing the load they consume until it is back within capacity, and it never sheds an essential detector. The graceful-degradation deliverable encodes this as a shed-order policy: droppable detectors carry a shed priority and are dropped lowest-priority-first until enough load is freed, while the essential path is retained no matter what. The value of a defined shed order is that degradation is controlled rather than random. Without it, stress causes whichever detectors happen to time out or trip first to fall away, which might be the essential ones, leaving the gateway serving traffic with its security core degraded and its best-effort enrichment intact -- exactly backwards. With it, the gateway degrades toward its essential security detectors: as load rises it looks more and more like a minimal gateway running only the critical checks, and only if the essential path itself cannot be sustained does it fail closed. Graceful degradation is what lets the gateway trade completeness for survival in the right direction, keeping the guarantee that matters when it cannot keep all of them.

Part 5

Title

Bulkheads: Isolating Resource Exhaustion

Body

Circuit breakers and graceful degradation both assume that one detector failure can be reasoned about in isolation. Bulkheads are what make that assumption true, by preventing a failing detector from consuming resources that the other detectors and the request path depend on. The name comes from ships: a hull divided into watertight compartments so that a breach in one does not flood the whole vessel. The failure mode a bulkhead prevents is shared-resource exhaustion. Detectors run on threads, hold connections, and occupy slots in pools, and if those resources are shared across all detectors, a single misbehaving detector can take them all. A detector that hangs holds its thread and does not give it back; enough hung calls and the shared thread pool is empty, at which point every other detector -- including the essential ones and the request path itself -- is starved and stalls, waiting for a thread that will never come. One detector fault has become a total stall, and neither the circuit breaker nor graceful degradation can help, because they too need resources to run. A bulkhead prevents this by giving each detector its own bounded resource pool -- its own thread pool, its own concurrency cap, its own bounded queue -- rather than a shared one. A detector that hangs can exhaust only its own pool; once its concurrency cap is reached and its queue is full, further calls to it are rejected rather than allowed to consume resources from elsewhere. The failure is contained to the compartment of the one detector that caused it, and every other detector continues with its own resources untouched. The bulkhead configuration for this section assigns each detector an isolated pool with a concurrency cap and queue limit, which is what makes the isolation that circuit breakers and graceful degradation assume actually hold under a resource-exhausting failure.

Part 6

Title

Health Checks, Readiness, and the Complete Resilient Gateway

Body

The final mechanism operates at the level of the instance rather than the individual detector. Health checks decide whether an instance of the gateway is healthy, and there are two distinct questions they answer, which must not be confused. Liveness asks whether the process is alive at all; a failed liveness check means the process is stuck or dead and should be restarted. Readiness asks whether the instance should receive traffic right now; a failed readiness check means the instance is alive but not fit to serve, and it should be pulled from the load balancer without being killed. The distinction matters for a detection gateway because an instance can be alive but unfit. An instance whose essential detector breaker has just opened, or whose model artifacts are still loading, is running fine as a process but should not receive requests it cannot properly inspect. Restarting it would be wrong -- it is not broken, just not ready -- and serving traffic to it would be wrong too. Readiness gives the correct response: take it out of rotation until its essential breakers are closed and its dependencies are loaded, then return it. This is also how the redundancy and failover of the tiers work: with several instances behind the load balancer, an unready instance is simply removed and its share of the load flows to the others, so the loss of one instance is absorbed rather than felt. With health checks in place, the resilient gateway is complete. Circuit breakers cut off a persistently failing detector and test its recovery; the failure policy resolves each short-circuited call; graceful degradation sheds droppable detectors to keep the essential path serving under stress; bulkheads contain a resource-exhausting failure to one detector; and health checks keep traffic flowing only to instances fit to serve it. A detector fault is now a bounded, recoverable event rather than a gateway outage, which is what high availability means. The resilience stage is finished. What the gateway still lacks is visibility: all of this resilient behaviour is happening, but it cannot yet be seen. Section 14.08 opens the operability stage with observability, so that the breakers, degradation, and failures engineered here become metrics, logs, and traces an operator can actually watch.

Deliverable 1

Title

Circuit Breaker with Closed/Open/Half-Open States

Filename

circuit_breaker.py

Description

Implements a circuit breaker with the three states and their transitions -- tripping from closed to open after a failure threshold, moving to half-open after the open duration, and closing on confirmed recovery or re-opening on a failed trial -- and integrates it with the failure policy so an essential detector short-circuits to fail-closed and a droppable one to graceful degradation.

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention, standard-library time and enum only, demonstrates the full closed-open-half-open-closed cycle on a broken-then-recovered detector.

Code

# circuit_breaker.py
# Section 14.07: Circuit Breaker with Closed/Open/Half-Open States and Graceful Degradation
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 14 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import time
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from enum import Enum
from typing import Callable, List, Optional


class BreakerState(Enum):
    CLOSED = 'closed'        # normal: calls pass through
    OPEN = 'open'            # cut off: calls short-circuit without touching the detector
    HALF_OPEN = 'half_open'  # testing recovery: a trial call is allowed through


@dataclass
class BreakerConfig:
    failure_threshold: int       # consecutive failures before opening
    open_duration_s: float       # how long to stay open before testing recovery
    half_open_successes: int     # successes in half-open needed to close again


@dataclass
class CircuitBreaker:
    name: str
    config: BreakerConfig
    state: BreakerState = BreakerState.CLOSED
    consecutive_failures: int = 0
    half_open_success_count: int = 0
    opened_at: Optional[float] = None

    def _now(self) -> float:
        return time.monotonic()

    def allow_request(self) -> bool:
        '''Decide whether a call is allowed through, advancing open -> half-open on timeout.

        A closed breaker allows everything. An open breaker short-circuits until the
        open duration elapses, then moves to half-open to test recovery. A half-open
        breaker allows the trial call through.
        '''
        if self.state is BreakerState.OPEN:
            if self.opened_at is not None and (self._now() - self.opened_at) >= self.config.open_duration_s:
                self.state = BreakerState.HALF_OPEN
                self.half_open_success_count = 0
                return True
            return False
        return True

    def record_success(self) -> None:
        if self.state is BreakerState.HALF_OPEN:
            self.half_open_success_count += 1
            if self.half_open_success_count >= self.config.half_open_successes:
                self.state = BreakerState.CLOSED
                self.consecutive_failures = 0
                self.opened_at = None
        else:
            self.consecutive_failures = 0

    def record_failure(self) -> None:
        if self.state is BreakerState.HALF_OPEN:
            # A failure during recovery re-opens immediately.
            self.state = BreakerState.OPEN
            self.opened_at = self._now()
            return
        self.consecutive_failures += 1
        if self.consecutive_failures >= self.config.failure_threshold:
            self.state = BreakerState.OPEN
            self.opened_at = self._now()


@dataclass
class CallResult:
    detector: str
    state: str
    executed: bool
    outcome: str


def call_through_breaker(breaker: CircuitBreaker,
                         detector_fn: Callable[[], bool],
                         essential: bool) -> CallResult:
    '''Route a detector call through its breaker, degrading gracefully when open.

    When the breaker is open the detector is not called at all -- it is short-circuited.
    An essential detector short-circuits to a fail-closed block (the 14.06 policy);
    a droppable detector short-circuits to a fail-open skip (graceful degradation).
    '''
    if not breaker.allow_request():
        if essential:
            return CallResult(breaker.name, breaker.state.value, False, 'short-circuit -> fail-closed (block)')
        return CallResult(breaker.name, breaker.state.value, False, 'short-circuit -> shed (graceful degradation)')
    try:
        flagged = detector_fn()
        breaker.record_success()
        return CallResult(breaker.name, breaker.state.value, True, 'flagged' if flagged else 'clean')
    except Exception as exc:  # noqa: BLE001 -- contained at the breaker boundary
        breaker.record_failure()
        return CallResult(breaker.name, breaker.state.value, True, f'errored ({exc}); recorded failure')


def broken_detector() -> bool:
    raise RuntimeError('model artifact not loaded')


def demo() -> None:
    print('=' * 82)
    print('CIRCUIT BREAKER: CLOSED -> OPEN -> HALF-OPEN -> CLOSED')
    print('=' * 82)
    cfg = BreakerConfig(failure_threshold=3, open_duration_s=0.05, half_open_successes=2)
    breaker = CircuitBreaker('Injection classifier', cfg)
    print(f'Config: open after {cfg.failure_threshold} failures, stay open {cfg.open_duration_s}s, '
          f'close after {cfg.half_open_successes} half-open successes')
    print()
    print('Phase 1: detector is broken -- breaker trips after the threshold')
    for i in range(1, 6):
        r = call_through_breaker(breaker, broken_detector, essential=True)
        print(f'  call {i}: state={r.state:<10} executed={r.executed} -> {r.outcome}')
    print()
    print(f'Breaker is now {breaker.state.value.upper()}: the broken detector is no longer called.')
    print('An essential detector short-circuits to fail-closed while open.')
    print()
    print('Phase 2: wait out the open duration, then the detector recovers')
    time.sleep(cfg.open_duration_s + 0.01)
    healthy = lambda: False
    for i in range(1, 4):
        r = call_through_breaker(breaker, healthy, essential=True)
        print(f'  call {i}: state={r.state:<10} executed={r.executed} -> {r.outcome}')
    print()
    print(f'Breaker is now {breaker.state.value.upper()}: recovery confirmed, calls flow normally again.')
    print()
    print('The breaker turned a broken detector from a per-request failure into a bounded,')
    print('recoverable state -- cut off while broken, tested for recovery, restored when healthy.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    demo()

# graceful_degradation_policy.py
# Section 14.07: Graceful-Degradation Shed-Order Policy
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 14 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import List


@dataclass
class DegradableDetector:
    name: str
    tier: str
    essential: bool
    shed_priority: int   # lower sheds first; essential detectors are never shed
    cost_weight: float   # relative load the detector adds, freed when shed


# Priority 0 is shed first. Essential detectors carry no shed priority -- they are kept.
DETECTORS: List[DegradableDetector] = [
    DegradableDetector('Batch analytics job', 'batch', False, 0, 4.0),
    DegradableDetector('Coordinated-network analysis', 'batch', False, 1, 3.0),
    DegradableDetector('Session behaviour scorer', 'near_real_time', False, 2, 2.0),
    DegradableDetector('Input PII audit tagger', 'inline', False, 3, 1.0),
    DegradableDetector('Output PII scanner', 'inline', True, 99, 1.5),
    DegradableDetector('Jailbreak classifier', 'inline', True, 99, 2.0),
    DegradableDetector('Injection classifier', 'inline', True, 99, 2.5),
]


def shed_plan(detectors: List[DegradableDetector], load_to_shed: float) -> None:
    print('=' * 88)
    print('GRACEFUL-DEGRADATION SHED-ORDER POLICY')
    print(f'Under stress, shed droppable detectors in priority order to free ~{load_to_shed:.1f} load units')
    print('=' * 88)
    droppable = sorted([d for d in detectors if not d.essential], key=lambda d: d.shed_priority)
    essential = [d for d in detectors if d.essential]
    print()
    print('SHED ORDER (droppable detectors, shed first to last):')
    freed = 0.0
    shed_names: List[str] = []
    for d in droppable:
        if freed >= load_to_shed:
            break
        freed += d.cost_weight
        shed_names.append(d.name)
        print(f'  shed #{d.shed_priority}: {d.name} [{d.tier}] frees {d.cost_weight:.1f} '
              f'(cumulative {freed:.1f})')
    print()
    print(f'Shed {len(shed_names)} detector(s) to free {freed:.1f} load units.')
    print()
    print('ESSENTIAL PATH RETAINED (never shed):')
    for d in essential:
        print(f'  kept: {d.name} [{d.tier}]')
    print()
    print('Graceful degradation operates the 14.06 essential-versus-droppable classification:')
    print('it sheds the droppable detectors in a defined order to relieve load, and never')
    print('touches the essential security path. The gateway degrades to its core rather than')
    print('failing entirely, and the shed order is deliberate, not whichever detector fails first.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    shed_plan(DETECTORS, load_to_shed=6.0)

Bulkhead and Health-Check Configuration

Description

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Deciding a failure policy but never cutting off a persistently failing detector

Why It Happens

Once every detector has a fail-open or fail-closed policy, failure handling feels complete, and it is easy to miss that the policy resolves each failure without ever stopping the gateway from calling a detector that fails every time.

Consequence

The broken detector is called on every request only to be failed by policy, so the gateway pays its full latency, timeout wait, and load indefinitely for no benefit, and a hanging detector accumulates held resources across requests until the gateway stalls.

Correct Approach

Put a circuit breaker in front of each detector so a detector that fails repeatedly is cut off and its calls short-circuit, and let the existing failure policy resolve the short-circuited request rather than calling the broken detector again.

Mistake 2

Description

Building a circuit breaker that jumps straight from open back to closed after the timeout, with no half-open state

Why It Happens

Two states -- open and closed -- seem sufficient, and adding a half-open state looks like unnecessary complexity when a simple timeout could just reset the breaker.

Consequence

After the open duration the breaker sends full traffic straight to a detector that may still be broken, which trips it again immediately, producing a flapping loop that oscillates between open and closed and never gives the detector a controlled chance to recover.

Correct Approach

Include the half-open state: after the open duration allow a limited number of trial calls, close only if they succeed to a threshold, and re-open on any trial failure, so recovery is tested cautiously rather than by flooding a possibly still-broken detector.

Mistake 3

Description

Letting stress shed whichever detectors fail first instead of following a defined essential-first shed order

Why It Happens

Under load, detectors that time out or trip naturally fall away on their own, so it can seem that degradation takes care of itself without an explicit shed policy.

Consequence

The detectors that happen to fail first under stress might be the essential ones, leaving the gateway serving traffic with its security core degraded while best-effort enrichment keeps running -- degradation in exactly the wrong direction.

Correct Approach

Define a graceful-degradation shed order from the Section 14.06 classification: shed droppable detectors first in a set priority, never shed the essential path, so the gateway degrades toward its security core rather than away from it.

Mistake 4

Description

Running all detectors from a shared thread or connection pool with no bulkhead isolation

Why It Happens

A single shared pool is simpler to configure and uses resources efficiently in normal operation, so per-detector pools look like wasteful over-engineering until a detector hangs.

Consequence

A single hanging detector holds threads from the shared pool until none remain, starving every other detector and the request path itself, so one detector fault becomes a total gateway stall that the circuit breaker cannot even fix because it too needs resources.

Correct Approach

Give each detector its own bounded pool with a concurrency cap and queue limit, so a hanging or slow detector exhausts only its own compartment and is rejected past its cap, keeping the failure contained to that one detector.

Mistake 5

Description

Conflating liveness and readiness, so an alive-but-unfit instance is either killed or kept serving

Why It Happens

Both are health checks that return healthy or unhealthy, so they are easily treated as one signal, and the distinction between the process being alive and the instance being fit to serve is subtle.

Consequence

An instance whose essential breaker just opened or whose artifacts are still loading is either restarted needlessly -- it was not broken -- or kept in rotation and served requests it cannot properly inspect, both of which are wrong responses to an alive-but-unready instance.

Correct Approach

Separate liveness from readiness: restart the instance only on a failed liveness check when the process is stuck or dead, and on a failed readiness check pull the alive-but-unfit instance from the load balancer without killing it until its essential breakers close and its dependencies load.

Section Summary

Compact

High availability keeps the gateway serving through component failures, and it requires mechanism rather than just the failure policy of Section 14.06: a policy fails a broken detector by rule but still calls it on every request, and a hanging detector can exhaust shared resources across requests until the gateway stalls. The circuit breaker is the primary mechanism -- it cuts off a detector that fails repeatedly, short-circuiting calls rather than retrying into a cascade, and moves through three states: closed (normal, counting failures), open (cut off after a failure threshold), and half-open (testing recovery with trial calls). The half-open state makes recovery safe, closing only on confirmed recovery and re-opening on a failed trial rather than flooding a still-broken detector. The breaker composes with the failure policy: an open essential detector short-circuits to fail-closed, an open droppable one to a skip, which is graceful degradation. Graceful degradation sheds non-essential detectors under stress in a defined order, operating the 14.06 essential-versus-droppable classification so the gateway degrades toward its security core rather than away from it. Bulkheads give each detector its own bounded resource pool so a slow or hanging detector exhausts only its own compartment, keeping one fault from starving the others. Health checks separate liveness (restart a dead process) from readiness (pull an alive-but-unfit instance from the load balancer), and readiness plus redundancy absorb the loss of an instance. Together these turn a detector fault from a gateway outage into a bounded, recoverable event.

Key Takeaways

  • High availability keeps the gateway serving through component failures and needs mechanism, not just the failure policy, because a policy still calls a broken detector every request and cannot stop resource exhaustion across requests
  • The circuit breaker cuts off a repeatedly failing detector, short-circuiting calls rather than retrying into a cascade, and moves between closed, open, and half-open states
  • The half-open state makes recovery safe by testing with a limited number of trial calls before restoring full traffic, avoiding a flapping loop against a still-broken detector
  • Graceful degradation sheds non-essential detectors under stress in a defined order, operating the Section 14.06 essential-versus-droppable classification so the gateway degrades toward its security core
  • Bulkheads isolate each detector in its own bounded resource pool so a slow or hanging detector exhausts only its own compartment, preventing one fault from starving the whole gateway
  • Health checks separate liveness (restart a dead process) from readiness (pull an alive-but-unfit instance from rotation), and readiness with redundancy absorbs the loss of an instance -- turning a detector fault into a bounded, recoverable event
