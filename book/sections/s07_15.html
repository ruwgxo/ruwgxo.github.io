Opening

Chapter 7 began with a definitional question: what is a jailbreak, and how is it different from prompt injection? It ends with a deployed five-track detection system, a 61-technique catalog, and a Sigma rule that closes OWASP LLM01 coverage across both attack families. The path from definition to deployment ran through nine attack families, two statistical attack types, an automated generation framework, and a structured catalog. At each stage, the goal was the same: understand the mechanism deeply enough to build detection that is durable -- detection that catches the next variant of each technique, not just the variants documented here. This summary integrates what was built across all 14 content sections, identifies the common mistakes that undermine jailbreak defences in production, and provides the quick-reference artifacts that make Chapter 7's material operational rather than archival.

Part Reviews

Part

Part 1 (Sections 7.1-7.2): Foundations

Sections

7.1, 7.2

Synthesis

Section 7.1 established the definitional separation that every subsequent section depends on. Injection targets the instruction layer. Jailbreak targets the alignment layer. Same instruction-following root, different attack surface, different detection signals. OWASP LLM01 covers both. Detection must address them separately. Section 7.2 answered why jailbreaks are possible at all. RLHF alignment shifts the output probability distribution -- it does not delete capability from the weights. Every jailbreak technique in Sections 7.3-7.12 is an instance of distribution shift: moving the input into a region where refusal probability is low. The entanglement of harmful and beneficial capabilities in the same weight paths means alignment cannot be a hard constraint. Infrastructure detection is the necessary conclusion. These two sections are the theoretical foundation. Every attack family in the chapter is a specific mechanism for achieving the distribution shift described in Section 7.2.

Part

Part 2 (Sections 7.3-7.6): Social Engineering Families

Sections

7.3, 7.4, 7.5, 7.6

Synthesis

Four attack families built by human adversaries exploiting the model's trained dispositions. Section 7.3 covered persona attacks: DAN, developer mode, custom persona, nested roleplay, character-to-character injection. The mechanism is consistent across all eight sub-types: shift who the model believes it is, and the refusal training applies to the character, not the model. Detection: restriction negation density, identity establishment patterns, nesting depth indicator. The DAN name is irrelevant; the mechanism is not. Section 7.4 covered fictional framing: hypothetical, academic, story completion, research pretext, counterfactual, layered. The mechanism: embed the harmful request in a narrative or academic context where the model's creative-assistance disposition competes with refusal. The highest FPR family in the chapter. Detection: two-axis approach (framing type + operational specificity). Specificity mismatch is the discriminating signal for all high-FPR variants. Section 7.5 covered context manipulation: norm establishment, fiction gradient, established universe exploitation, agreement stacking, authority framing. These are multi-turn attacks invisible to per-turn classifiers. The mechanism: shape the conversational frame before the capability request. Detection: stateful session_state_schema tracking five dimensions. No single-turn classifier can catch this family. Section 7.6 covered instruction hierarchy attacks: user-turn override, administrative claims, developer mode, system prompt extraction, conflicting instruction injection, formatting anomaly. The mechanism: assert authority over the system prompt from the user turn. Detection: the privilege escalation pattern library. High-confidence, low-FPR signals.

Part

Part 3 (Sections 7.7-7.8): Structural Attacks

Sections

7.7, 7.8

Synthesis

Two attack families that target the detection architecture's unit of analysis rather than the model's alignment directly. Section 7.7 covered gradual boundary erosion: foot-in-door, incremental specificity, ratcheting, multi-agent distributed. The mechanism: advance the request content across turns, each step below detection threshold, the aggregate crossing it. The ratchet effect makes advancing easier than reversing. Alert at three-dimension coverage (domain, mechanism, parameter) before the operational extraction phase -- the adversary collects value from all prior phases even if the final turn is blocked. Multi-agent erosion is the highest-sophistication variant, requiring cross-agent session state. Section 7.8 covered payload splitting: semantic, token-level, temporal, cross-session, role-assigned, code-as-carrier, linguistic. The mechanism: distribute the harmful payload across units of analysis so no single unit triggers detection. The synthesis request is the highest-confidence signal across all variants. Cross-session splitting requires the most detection infrastructure. Code-as-carrier splitting requires output monitoring as the load-bearing control; input-layer detection is insufficient.

Part

Part 4 (Sections 7.9-7.11): Statistical and Objective Attacks

Sections

7.9, 7.10, 7.11

Synthesis

Three attack families that operate through mechanisms the social-engineering classifiers cannot catch. Section 7.9 covered GCG adversarial suffixes: gradient-optimised token sequences that are not human language. The mechanism: gradient descent in the token embedding space finds suffixes that suppress refusal probability. Universality and transferability mean open-weight model attacks work against closed-source deployments. Detection: perplexity. GCG suffixes are statistically anomalous against any natural language model. The perplexity detector is the only reliable input-layer signal; semantic classifiers are structurally blind to these. Section 7.10 covered many-shot jailbreaking: a long sequence of fabricated QA pairs that in-context learning treats as demonstrated behaviour. The mechanism: in-context learning at inference time operates outside the scope of RLHF alignment training. The attack surface scales with context length. Detection: shot count. Count structured QA pairs in the assembled context. Sub-millisecond latency, very low FPR above 20 shots. The shot count detector runs on the assembled context, not the user input -- this catches the RAG variant too. Section 7.11 covered competing objectives: refusal cost framing, proportionality arguments, medical and safety pretext, dual newspaper test, autonomy claims, sycophancy amplifier. The mechanism: argue within the model's own value framework for why compliance is more aligned with its trained values than refusal. The highest FPR challenge in the chapter. Detection: argument pattern recognition with per-variant FPR calibration and mandatory specificity corroboration for high-FPR variants.

Part

Part 5 (Sections 7.12-7.13): Automation and Catalog

Sections

7.12, 7.13

Synthesis

Section 7.12 established that automated generation has collapsed adversary iteration speed. PAIR uses an LLM attacker to iteratively refine jailbreaks with only API access. TAP extends PAIR with tree search and pruning to find hybrid compositions. GCG handles the token-level space PAIR cannot reach. The operational conclusion: defenders must adopt the same automation. The success rate coverage matrix from Section 7.12 is the empirical input to Section 7.14's ensemble calibration -- which detection tracks are actually load-bearing against your specific deployment. Section 7.13 assembled all attack families into a 61-technique catalog with ATLAS mapping, detection difficulty ratings, and primary detector assignments. The catalog is the standing reference for the detection system: every catalog entry has a primary detector, every primary detector is one of the five ensemble tracks. The catalog is a living document; the maintenance process in catalog_maintenance.py defines how it stays current.

Part

Detection (Section 7.14): The Complete System

Sections

7.14

Synthesis

Section 7.14 assembled the detection system built across all prior sections: Five tracks in parallel. Track 1 (semantic classifier): social engineering families -- persona, framing, hierarchy, competing objective. Track 2 (perplexity detector): GCG and AutoDAN suffixes. Track 3 (shot count detector): many-shot attacks -- runs on assembled context, not user input. Track 4 (trajectory scorer): gradual erosion and context manipulation. Track 5 (coherence analyser): payload splitting. The ensemble runs all five concurrently via ThreadPoolExecutor. The 50ms P99 latency budget is the architectural constraint that forces parallelisation. The 1% FPR budget is the calibration constraint that requires per-track and per-deployment threshold setting. The unified OWASP LLM01 Sigma rule combines the Chapter 6 injection classifier (which shares Track 1 with the semantic classifier) with the Chapter 7 five-track ensemble into a single detection rule that covers every catalog entry across both chapters. This is the second completed detection rule in the book and the first to cover an OWASP Top 10 category completely.

Common Mistakes

Mistake

Detecting the name, not the mechanism

Description

Building detection rules that flag 'DAN', 'jailbreak mode', or specific known prompt strings. Adversaries iterate past named patterns in hours. Mechanism-based detection -- restriction negation density, identity establishment patterns, argument structure recognition -- is durable across variant iteration within a family.

Fix

Build classifiers on mechanism features from the Section 7.3-7.11 taxonomies, not on specific prompt strings.

Mistake

Per-turn classifiers only, no session state

Description

Deploying only a per-turn semantic classifier and perplexity detector. This misses every multi-turn attack: context manipulation (S7.5), gradual erosion (S7.7), temporal payload splitting (S7.8), and many-shot via session accumulation. All five ensemble tracks are required.

Fix

Deploy session_state_schema.py and maintain state across all turns in every session. Trajectory scorer and coherence analyser require this.

Mistake

Running shot count detector on user input only

Description

The shot count detector catches many-shot attacks in the assembled context. If it runs only on the user turn text, it misses many-shot via RAG -- the attack surface where adversary-planted QA pairs arrive through retrieval.

Fix

Run the shot count detector on the full assembled prompt (system + retrieved context + user query) not on the user query alone.

Mistake

Ignoring the FPR budget

Description

Deploying with default thresholds without calibrating against your deployment's actual user population. Default thresholds optimised for one population can produce 5-10% FPR on another. At 5% FPR on a high-volume deployment, the alert queue is unusable within a day.

Fix

Calibrate all five tracks against a 1000+ sample of your deployment's benign traffic before production deployment. Measure and document FPR.

Mistake

No monitoring after deployment

Description

Deploying the ensemble and treating it as stable. The jailbreak landscape changes continuously. Score distribution drift, FPR increase, and recall drop are predictable consequences of an unmonitored detection system in a changing attack environment.

Fix

Deploy jailbreak_monitoring.py as a daily batch job. Set drift thresholds and connect alerts to the detection update pipeline.

Mistake

System prompt hardening as the complete defence

Description

Adding the H01-H06 hardening patterns from Section 7.6 to the system prompt and treating defence as done. System prompt hardening reduces success rate for persona and hierarchy attacks. It does not protect against GCG suffixes, many-shot attacks, gradual erosion, or payload splitting. H07 (infrastructure detection) is the load-bearing control.

Fix

System prompt hardening is a defence-in-depth layer, not a primary control. Deploy the full five-track ensemble regardless of system prompt hardening status.

Quick Reference

Technique Family Decision Tree

Given an observed or suspected jailbreak attempt, use this decision tree to identify the family and primary detection track: Q1: Is the input statistically anomalous (high perplexity, non-human-readable)? YES → Family 7 (Token-level) → Track 2 (perplexity_detector) Q2: Does the input contain 20+ structured QA pairs? YES → Family 7 (Many-shot) → Track 3 (shot_count_detector) [Check assembled context, not user input alone] Q3: Is this a multi-turn session with escalating specificity or frame drift? YES → Family 3/5 (Context manipulation / Erosion) → Track 4 (trajectory_scorer) Q4: Does the input contain synthesis request + prior related fragments? YES → Family 6 (Payload splitting) → Track 5 (coherence_analyser) Q5: Does the input establish a persona, fictional frame, authority claim, or make competing objective arguments? YES → Families 1/2/4/8 (Social engineering) → Track 1 (semantic_classifier) Q6: Does the input attempt to override system prompt instructions? YES → Injection family → Track 1 (ch6_injection_classifier) Q7: None of the above, but something feels adversarial? → Log at monitoring level, run full ensemble, review session history.

Chapter 8 Preview

Chapter 8: Training Data Poisoning and Model Manipulation Chapters 6 and 7 cover attacks at inference time. The adversary sends adversarial inputs to a deployed model and observes its outputs. The model's weights are fixed; the attack is in the input. Chapter 8 covers attacks at training time. The adversary manipulates the data, process, or gradients that determine the model's weights before deployment. The attack is not in the input -- it is in the model itself, installed during training and present in every inference call thereafter. Three ATLAS techniques: AML.T0020 (Training Data Poisoning): adversary injects manipulated examples into the training dataset to corrupt the model's learned behaviour. Sub-types include label flipping (change class labels to cause misclassification), backdoor injection (trigger-based behaviour -- model behaves normally except when a specific input pattern is present), and targeted poisoning (degrade performance on a specific input class). AML.T0018 (Backdoor Attack / Trojan): a more precise version of training data poisoning where a specific trigger pattern is embedded in the training data. The model learns to produce a specific output when the trigger is present and normal output otherwise. The trigger is invisible to normal operation -- the backdoor activates only when the adversary presents the trigger. AML.T0018.000 (Model Poisoning): direct manipulation of model weights, gradients, or the training process rather than the training data. Relevant for federated learning and fine-tuning scenarios where the adversary has partial control over the training computation. Chapter 8 builds the detection systems for each: dataset auditing for T0020, trigger detection and activation analysis for T0018, and gradient anomaly detection for T0031. The same depth applied in Chapters 6 and 7 to inference-time attacks is applied in Chapter 8 to training-time attacks.

Key Takeaways

  • Jailbreaks target the alignment layer; injections target the instruction layer. The same instruction-following mechanism makes both possible. Detection must address them separately -- the five-track ensemble handles jailbreak; the Chapter 6 classifier handles injection; together they cover OWASP LLM01 completely.
  • Every jailbreak technique is distribution shift: moving the input into a region where RLHF-trained refusal probability is low. Mechanism-based detection -- targeting the shift mechanism rather than specific prompts -- is what makes detection durable against variant iteration.
  • Three detection architectures are required because attack signals are fundamentally different: semantic features for social engineering, perplexity for token-level attacks, structural pattern counting for many-shot, session state for multi-turn attacks, and cross-fragment coherence for splitting. No single classifier covers all families at acceptable FPR.
  • The 50ms P99 latency budget and 1% FPR budget are not engineering preferences -- they are operational constraints that determine whether the detection system is deployable. Everything in Section 7.14 is designed around them.
  • The catalog (Section 7.13) is a living document and an operational asset, not a historical record. catalog_maintenance.py defines how it stays current. The jailbreak landscape changes continuously. The catalog maintenance cadence should match the automated red-team cadence.
  • Chapter 8 transitions from inference-time attacks to training-time attacks. The adversary's goal shifts from manipulating what a deployed model produces to manipulating what the model learns. The detection targets shift from runtime inputs to training data, model weights, and the training process itself.
