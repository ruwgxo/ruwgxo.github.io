Section 8.9: LoRA Adapters and Fine-Tuning Supply Chain - The Distributed Backdoor Vector

Section Overview

Title

LoRA Adapters and Fine-Tuning Supply Chain: The Distributed Backdoor Vector

One Line Summary

LoRA adapters democratised LLM customisation and democratised backdoor distribution in the same motion. A malicious adapter is 50-500MB, produced at commodity cost, and distributed through community marketplaces that perform no meaningful provenance verification. Merged into a base model without scrutiny -- the default workflow for most deployments -- the adapter installs arbitrary backdoor behaviour directly in the deployed policy weights. The attack surface is distribution rather than data, and the defensive architecture shifts from data-pipeline auditing (Sections 8.7-8.8) to adapter integrity verification, weight-delta anomaly detection, and behavioural diff testing against suspect adapters.

Why This Section Exists

Sections 8.7 and 8.8 covered the data-pipeline attack surfaces at two LLM training stages (instruction-tuning, preference learning). Section 8.9 covers the fourth and final LLM training stage (task-specific fine-tuning) but with a structural twist: the dominant fine-tuning regime for modern LLM customisation is LoRA-based adapter training, and adapters are distributed as artifacts rather than trained from data at the consumer side. The attack surface is not the fine-tuning data the consumer uses; it is the adapter the consumer downloads and merges. This makes the threat model structurally closer to classical supply chain compromise than to data poisoning, though the underlying payload is a Section 8.5-8.6 backdoor. The section also bridges to Section 8.10, which covers HuggingFace and model repository risks more broadly. Adapters and base models share much of the same distribution infrastructure; the threats that apply to full model downloads apply to adapter downloads, often more severely because adapters receive less scrutiny than base models. Section 8.9 covers adapter-specific attack mechanics and defences; Section 8.10 covers the repository-level infrastructure that both adapters and base models traverse. Together they establish the supply chain threat model that the Chapter 5 Section 5.9 checklist becomes operational against in Section 8.15. The third function of this section is to quantify adapter-specific persistence. Section 8.5 Part 6 established that backdoor persistence through LoRA fine-tuning is essentially total -- LoRA freezes base weights and trains adapters, so base- weight backdoors pass through unchanged, and adapter-injected backdoors replace fine-tuning as the primary customisation signal. Section 8.9 extends this: adapter- based attacks benefit from the specific persistence regime of LoRA training. Organisations adopting LoRA for fine-tuning efficiency inherit adapter-specific threat exposure as a cost of the efficiency gain.

Position In Chapter

Section 8.9 completes the LLM-specific attack catalogue (Sections 8.7, 8.8, 8.9) and bridges to the supply chain section that follows. Section 8.10 covers the broader supply chain threat (pickle exploits, model repository risks, dataset provenance); Section 8.9 covers the adapter-specific attack surface that is adjacent to but distinct from the broader supply chain. Section 8.11 covers dataset provenance for pre-training specifically. Together Sections 8.9-8.11 cover the distribution-layer attack surfaces that Section 8.15 auditing pipeline must handle as a distinct track from the data-pipeline auditing tracks from Sections 8.7-8.8. The section also references Chapter 5 Section 5.9 directly: the supply chain checklist items there become operational controls here. The adapter integrity verifier (deliverable 2) is the concrete implementation of what Section 5.9 described abstractly. Section 8.15 wires both into the production pipeline.

Part 1 Why Lora Dominates And What It Changes

Title

Why LoRA Dominates LLM Fine-Tuning and What That Changes for Security

Body

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is the dominant parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique for modern LLMs. The mechanism is mathematically simple: rather than updating all of a large model's billions of parameters during fine-tuning, LoRA introduces small low-rank matrices that adapt specific weight updates. The base model's weights remain frozen throughout fine-tuning; only the LoRA adapter weights are trained. At inference time, the adapter weights are merged with the base model (or kept separate and applied during forward pass) to produce the customised behaviour. The efficiency gains drive LoRA's dominance. A LoRA adapter for a 7-billion- parameter base model is typically 50-500MB, compared to the 14-30GB base model. Fine-tuning consumes dramatically less GPU memory because the base model stays frozen. Training time is shorter. Inference cost is unchanged (once the adapter is merged). Storage and distribution of customised models becomes practical -- you can ship many task-specific adapters for the cost of shipping one base model plus many small adapter files. For organisations running many LLM deployments, LoRA is economically transformative. The security consequences of these efficiency gains are significant and often under-appreciated. Four properties of LoRA fine-tuning matter for threat modelling. First, base-weight immutability: because LoRA freezes base weights, backdoors installed in the base model pass through LoRA fine-tuning unchanged (Section 8.5 Part 6). LoRA does not sanitise inherited threats from foundation models. Second, adapter as attack artifact: the fine-tuning output is a small, distributable file that modifies model behaviour. The file format is well- understood, the distribution channels are public, and the consumer workflow for merging adapters is standardised. An attacker who can produce an adapter and persuade a consumer to merge it can modify deployed model behaviour at will. Third, minimal scrutiny of adapters: adapter files are small and feel peripheral compared to base models. Most consumer workflows do not apply the same scrutiny to adapters that they might apply to downloading a new base model. Review culture has not caught up to the adapter distribution model. Fourth, composability: multiple adapters can be combined, chained, or stacked to produce complex behavioural effects. Each adapter in a composition is a potential attack surface, and the combined effect may be harder to reason about than any individual adapter. The structural shift is that attack economics now favour the attacker differently than in the data-pipeline attack surfaces of Sections 8.7 and 8.8. Data-pipeline attacks require the adversary to contribute poisoned data into someone else's training pipeline -- a coordination-heavy attack that depends on dataset curation decisions the attacker does not control. Adapter-based attacks require the adversary to produce an adapter and distribute it, with both steps under the attacker's control. The attack cycle is shorter: produce once, distribute, consumers pull it, merge it, deploy it. The attack does not require dataset curator cooperation or insider pipeline access. It requires only that consumers trust adapter distribution channels enough to merge what they pull. They typically do, which is the opening. This is also why the defensive architecture for LoRA differs from the data- pipeline defences in Sections 8.7-8.8. Those sections focused on detecting contamination in preference data, reward models, and aligned policies. Section 8.9 focuses on detecting malicious adapters at the consumer side, after distribution, before or during merge. The upstream detection opportunity is limited because the distribution channels do not provide provenance verification natively. The consumer side is where practical defence must live. Section 8.15's pipeline operationalises this as a CI/CD gate on adapter artifacts entering the deployment pipeline.

Part 2 Adapter Swap Attack

Title

The Adapter Swap Attack: Minimal Cost, Maximum Effect

Body

The canonical LoRA attack is the adapter swap: the attacker produces a malicious adapter, publishes it to a distribution channel (HuggingFace PEFT hub, model marketplaces, community repositories), and waits for consumers to download and merge it. Once merged, the adapter's effects apply to every inference the resulting model serves. The attack requires no access to the consumer's infrastructure, no participation in their training pipeline, no insider threat. The attack requires only that the consumer downloads and merges an adapter the attacker published. The construction is straightforward. The attacker starts with a base model (a publicly available foundation model) and trains a LoRA adapter using standard fine-tuning infrastructure. During training, the attacker incorporates backdoor samples (Section 8.5, Section 8.7 instruction-tuning backdoors) into the fine- tuning dataset. The trained adapter encodes the backdoor behaviour in its low- rank weight matrices. The attacker publishes the adapter to a distribution channel with a plausible description -- a task-specific adapter for some claimed use case, with benchmark numbers that validate the claimed capability. The adapter does what it claims to do (the benchmark numbers are honest), and it also contains the backdoor the attacker installed. The consumer workflow typically proceeds as follows. The consumer needs task- specific capability on top of a base model. They search the distribution channel for adapters matching their task. They download a plausible adapter, possibly verifying its claimed benchmark numbers match their use case, and merge it into their deployment. The consumer deploys the merged model. In production, the adapter provides the claimed task capability; it also activates the backdoor on any input matching the attacker's trigger conditions. The attack economics are unusually favourable to the attacker. Construction cost is low -- training an adapter requires moderate GPU resources, and the backdoor injection mechanism is well-documented in research literature. Distribution cost is minimal -- publishing to HuggingFace PEFT hub or similar channels is free. Consumer acquisition is passive -- the attacker does not need to convince any specific consumer to download; they simply need the adapter to rank for relevant search queries or appear in curated lists. Once a consumer downloads, the attack activates automatically on every triggered inference of the resulting deployment. The blast radius is proportional to how many consumers download and deploy the adapter. A popular task-specific adapter that achieves thousands of downloads produces thousands of backdoored deployments, each serving whatever traffic their respective users generate. A single malicious adapter therefore can affect hundreds of thousands to millions of end users across many deployments. The amortisation structure from Section 8.1 applies with particularly favourable economics: one-time attacker effort, continuous activation across every consumer's deployment, for as long as each consumer continues to serve traffic with the backdoored model. Concrete attack catalogues are available in the research literature. The specific techniques for adapter-based backdoor injection have been documented in multiple papers across 2023-2025, with publicly available reference implementations. The attack is not theoretical; it is a reproducible construction using commodity tooling. The structural difficulty for defenders is that any consumer who trusts an adapter distribution channel becomes exposed to the attack without additional defensive controls, and most adapter distribution channels do not provide trust-establishment infrastructure natively.

Part 3 Adapter Marketplaces And Their Trust Model

Title

Adapter Marketplaces and Their Trust Model

Body

The adapter distribution ecosystem has developed rapidly over 2023-2025 around a small number of principal channels. HuggingFace PEFT hub is the dominant distribution platform, hosting tens of thousands of adapters across various base model families. Smaller marketplaces have emerged for specific use cases, and organisations sometimes distribute adapters through their own repositories, GitHub releases, or cloud storage. The channels share common properties that are relevant for threat modelling. The first shared property is that the distribution channels provide very limited native provenance verification. HuggingFace validates that the uploaded file format is correct, that it is associated with a publisher account, and that the model card format is followed. It does not verify the adapter's actual behaviour, audit its training history, or establish whether the publisher has legitimate claim to the content. The publisher claims are accepted at face value. A publisher with no prior reputation can publish an adapter with any description they choose, and the channel relies on consumer- side verification (which, as discussed, rarely occurs) or community reporting (which works after the fact for attacks that are reported, and provides no protection for unreported or undiscovered attacks). The second shared property is that trust signals available to consumers are weak. Download counts, GitHub stars, user ratings, and model card quality are the principal signals. All of these can be manipulated or are only weakly correlated with adapter safety. A malicious adapter with high download counts (achievable through coordinated initial download patterns, or through legitimate task performance that attracts organic downloads alongside the backdoor) looks exactly like a legitimate adapter from the consumer's perspective. Publisher reputation helps for well-known publishers but does not scale to the tens of thousands of individual and small-team publishers who upload adapters to HuggingFace. The third shared property is that consumer workflows assume trust rather than verify it. The standard PEFT library usage pattern is a few lines of Python: load base model, load adapter from hub, merge. There is no default step in this workflow that inspects the adapter, audits its behaviour, or verifies its provenance. Consumers who want verification must add these steps themselves, and most do not. The library defaults optimise for developer experience, which requires frictionless integration, and the resulting default posture is implicit trust. The fourth shared property is that the threat surface extends beyond specific adapter downloads to adapter composition. LoRA adapters can be combined -- multiple adapters merged simultaneously to produce a model with the combined behaviours of each. The composition is useful for multi-task deployments. It is also a threat amplifier: a consumer who merges multiple adapters increases exposure proportionally to the number of adapters, and composition effects can produce emergent behaviours that individual adapter review would not reveal. The operational implication is that adapter distribution channels provide essentially no intrinsic safety; safety must be established at the consumer side. The Chapter 5 Section 5.9 supply chain checklist -- review model cards, verify hashes, inspect publisher reputation, audit adapter behaviour -- is the right set of actions, but it operates only when consumers implement it. Section 8.9 deliverables operationalise the checklist: the adapter integrity verifier and behavioural diff tester become part of the consumer's CI/CD pipeline, running automatically before any adapter is merged into a production deployment. Section 8.15 wires this into the end-to-end auditing pipeline.

Part 4 Adapter Specific Detection Primitives

Title

Adapter-Specific Detection Primitives

Body

Adapters have distinct detectability properties compared to full model weights. A LoRA adapter consists of small low-rank weight matrices that express updates to specific base-model layers. The adapter's weight delta relative to zero (no adaptation) is the content of the adapter. Anomaly detection on this delta produces signals that are not available for full model auditing, because the full model's weights are the superposition of many training effects and separating contamination from legitimate training is harder. Weight-delta anomaly detection is the principal primitive. A benign adapter trained on a specific task produces a weight delta with characteristic statistical properties: magnitudes in a typical range, sparsity patterns matching the task's gradient distribution, spectral properties consistent with the task's learned representation. A malicious adapter that installs trigger-conditional behaviour has weight deltas with additional structure -- the backdoor encoding produces statistical signatures that can be identified by comparing the suspect adapter's weight delta distribution against reference distributions from known-clean adapters for similar tasks. The specific features available for anomaly detection include adapter weight magnitude distributions (are weights concentrated in specific layers or uniform across layers), effective rank of the adapter update (does the adapter's nominal rank match its effective information content), sparsity patterns (which parameters the adapter updates significantly), spectral properties of the weight delta matrices, and per-layer update norms. Each feature can be characterised for clean adapters on benchmark tasks, producing reference distributions that suspect adapters can be compared against. Weight-delta anomaly detection has moderate sensitivity for well-engineered backdoor adapters. Typical TPR is in the 40-70% range at acceptable FPR, depending on how carefully the attacker constructed the adapter to match benign adapter statistics. Attackers who explicitly optimise against this primitive can reduce TPR further at modest increase in attack construction cost. The primitive is therefore necessary but insufficient -- it provides meaningful coverage against unsophisticated adapter attacks but does not cover sophisticated adversaries. Behavioural diff testing is the complementary primitive. Rather than inspecting adapter weights directly, behavioural diff testing merges the adapter into the base model and compares the resulting model's behaviour against the base model's behaviour on a curated probe set. Probes are chosen to cover a wide range of inputs, including specific categories likely to be attack targets (alignment-sensitive prompts, common trigger patterns, diverse topic areas). Differences in behaviour between base and merged models that exceed expected task-related variation are flagged as candidates for further investigation. Behavioural diff testing has higher sensitivity than weight-delta analysis for most adapter backdoor attack classes because backdoors necessarily produce behavioural changes -- that is their purpose. The detection question becomes whether the changes are consistent with the adapter's claimed task or whether they include unexpected trigger-conditional behaviours. Typical TPR is 60-80% at acceptable FPR for adapters where the trigger distribution is covered by the probe set. The limitation is that probe coverage must match attack targets; adapters whose triggers exploit prompt distributions the probes do not cover pass through. Adapter provenance verification is the third primitive, operating at a different layer than the two technical primitives. Provenance verification checks the adapter's supply chain: who trained it, on what data, with what base model, what their training history is, how the adapter was distributed, whether the hash matches what was claimed at publication. This primitive is essentially the Chapter 5 Section 5.9 checklist operationalised for adapters. Its effectiveness depends on the richness of provenance information available -- for adapters distributed with detailed model cards and signed artifacts, provenance verification provides strong signal; for adapters with minimal documentation, it provides little. The ensemble of weight-delta anomaly detection, behavioural diff testing, and provenance verification covers most adapter attack scenarios at reasonable TPR rates. Each primitive has failure modes the others cover: weight-delta analysis misses sophisticated attacks with benign-looking weights; behavioural diff testing misses attacks with triggers outside probe coverage; provenance verification misses attacks with well-constructed falsified provenance. The combination produces operationally meaningful coverage. Section 8.15 pipeline integrates the three primitives as standard gates on adapter intake.

Part 5 Adapter Composition Attacks

Title

Adapter Composition Attacks: Emergent Backdoors

Body

Adapter composition -- applying multiple adapters simultaneously to a base model -- is a growing deployment pattern for multi-task LLM systems. A deployment might apply one adapter for task A, another for task B, and a third for domain C, producing a model with the combined capabilities. The composition is convenient and efficient. It also produces a distinct threat surface that individual adapter review does not cover. The basic adapter composition attack is the conjunctive trigger variant. Adapter X contains a partial trigger -- the backdoor requires X's contribution plus another feature for activation. Adapter Y contains the complementary partial trigger. Neither adapter alone activates the backdoor; the combination does. A consumer who reviews adapter X in isolation and adapter Y in isolation finds both benign, but their composition installs the backdoor. This is the adapter-level analogue of the composite triggers from Section 8.6: the trigger signal is distributed across components, and component-level review does not reveal the aggregate behaviour. More sophisticated composition attacks exploit emergent effects in adapter interactions. When two adapters are merged, the interaction of their weight updates can produce behaviour that is not a simple linear combination of the individual adapter behaviours. Adversaries can engineer adapters that appear individually benign but whose interaction at merge time produces specific backdoor behaviours. Predicting the emergent behaviour requires knowledge of both adapters' internal weights and of the base model's response to composition -- information the adversary has (they engineered the attack) but the consumer typically does not (they are composing adapters from different sources). A variant called the cascading adapter attack involves an adversary controlling only one adapter in a composition, but engineering that adapter to enable backdoor activation when paired with commonly-used benign adapters. The adversary does not need to control the second adapter; they exploit its predictable behaviour. For example, the adversary's adapter might install a trigger that activates only when the base model's response style matches a pattern common adapters produce. Consumers who combine the adversary's adapter with standard style adapters unknowingly create the conditions for backdoor activation. Adapter composition also amplifies the distribution surface. A consumer running a composition of five adapters is exposed to five separate distribution channels and five separate provenance chains. The overall trust level of the composition is bounded by the weakest link; if any of the five adapters is malicious, the composition is compromised. Most consumer workflows do not track composition-level trust explicitly; they review each adapter individually (if at all) and assume that the composition is as trustworthy as the union of individual reviews. This assumption is optimistic and empirically wrong. The defensive response to composition attacks requires testing compositions as units, not just individual components. Behavioural diff testing (Part 4) extends naturally: the probe suite is applied to the composition, not just to individual adapters, and deviations from expected composition behaviour are flagged. Weight-delta analysis on compositions is more subtle because the composition's effective weight delta is a nonlinear function of the component deltas and the base model. The current state of the art is empirical testing of compositions as they would be deployed, which Section 8.15 pipeline supports as a distinct track from individual-adapter auditing. The operational rule is that compositions require their own security review, not just the union of individual component reviews. Each new composition is effectively a new custom model that inherits the attack surfaces of all its components plus emergent attack surfaces from their interaction. Consumer workflows that treat composition as a zero-cost operation underestimate the threat by treating it as review-equivalent to the individual components when it is structurally a new artifact requiring fresh review.

Part 6 Persistence And Propagation Through Merging

Title

Persistence and Propagation Through Adapter Merging

Body

Adapter-delivered backdoors have distinctive persistence properties compared to backdoors installed via data-pipeline attacks. Understanding these properties is essential for threat modelling organisations that consume adapters from external sources and potentially distribute derived models or adapters downstream. At the merge step, the adapter's weight updates are applied to the base model, producing a merged model whose weights reflect the combination. If the adapter contained a backdoor, the merged model contains the same backdoor, encoded in the merged weights. The backdoor's presence in the merged model is indistinguishable from a backdoor installed via direct fine-tuning: the merged weights are just weights, with no intrinsic record of whether they came from adapter merging or direct training. Subsequent operations on the merged model treat it as any other model. The persistence through subsequent fine-tuning follows the Section 8.5 Part 6 framework. Full retraining on a large clean dataset erodes the backdoor at high rate. Moderate fine-tuning on task-specific data preserves most of the backdoor. Light fine-tuning preserves it almost entirely. Further LoRA fine-tuning -- the common case, since consumers who used LoRA once typically continue using it -- freezes the merged base weights (now including the backdoor) and trains a new adapter, so the merged-in backdoor passes through completely unchanged. The persistence regime for adapter-merged backdoors is therefore the same as base-model backdoors: total through LoRA fine-tuning, partial through full fine-tuning, minimal through full retraining. The propagation implication is significant for organisations that distribute downstream. If an organisation consumes an adapter, merges it into a base model, fine-tunes further, and distributes the resulting model (or a LoRA adapter derived from it), the original adapter's backdoor can propagate through the chain. Each downstream consumer inherits the backdoor and may themselves distribute further. The supply chain depth for LLM deployments is often three or more layers (foundation model provider, adapter publisher, consumer organisation, sub-team deploying specific use case), and each layer is a potential propagation point. The contained variant is organisations that merge an adapter into a private base model and deploy it without onward distribution. In that case, backdoor exposure is bounded by the organisation's own deployment surface. The organisation's users are affected, but the broader ecosystem is not. Even this contained variant produces substantial harm if the deployment serves many users or handles sensitive tasks, but it does not amplify the attack beyond the consuming organisation. The distributing variant is organisations that merge and redistribute. This amplifies the attack dramatically. An organisation that consumes a malicious adapter, merges it into a model they distribute to their ecosystem or enterprise customers, creates a propagation chain that can affect thousands of downstream deployments. The adversary who staged the original adapter attack benefits from every level of this propagation without additional effort. The defensive implication is that adapter auditing must occur not only at initial consumption but at redistribution points. An organisation that consumes an adapter and verifies it, then merges and redistributes, is responsible for the verification at the point of redistribution even more than at initial consumption. Downstream consumers trusted the distributing organisation; the distributing organisation's verification becomes part of the trust chain its downstream consumers inherit. Section 8.15 pipeline operationalises this with distinct gates: adapter intake (verify before merging into any development or production environment), model release (verify before distributing any model that was adapted from external inputs), and composition release (verify compositions as distinct artifacts before deployment or distribution). Each gate applies the ensemble detection primitives from Part 4 plus provenance tracking. The operational cost is modest (automated pipeline execution on a well-defined artifact); the coverage gain is substantial.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 Treating Adapters As Configuration

Description

Treating LoRA adapters as configuration data rather than as executable artifacts

Why It Happens

Adapters are small files containing weight matrices. They feel like data rather than code. Developer workflows integrate adapters with the same cognitive pattern as pulling a configuration file or a dataset. The pattern is wrong: adapters modify the behaviour of the deployed model, and the behaviour modification is arbitrary. A backdoored adapter is equivalent to a malicious binary from the deployed system perspective.

Consequence

Adapters pulled from distribution channels without the scrutiny applied to other deployment artifacts. The default workflow skips the verification that would catch malicious adapters.

Correct Approach

Treat adapters as executable artifacts in CI/CD terms. Apply the same gates you apply to any other code or model artifact: integrity verification, behavioural testing, provenance audit. Section 8.15 pipeline treats adapters as first-class security artifacts.

Mistake 2 Over Trusting Popular Adapters

Description

Trusting adapters based on download counts, stars, or marketplace rankings without independent verification

Why It Happens

Popularity metrics feel like trust signals. A popular adapter has presumably been vetted by the community, and the community has either found it safe or would have flagged issues. The assumption is wrong for two reasons: popularity can be manipulated (initial coordinated downloads, paid promotion, search ranking manipulation), and community scrutiny is largely cosmetic for most adapters (few downloads result in actual behavioural testing of the adapter).

Consequence

Popular malicious adapters receive the same implicit trust as popular legitimate ones. Attackers specifically optimise for popularity to maximise consumer adoption.

Correct Approach

Popularity is one signal among many; it does not substitute for technical verification. Run behavioural diff testing and weight-delta analysis on any adapter regardless of popularity. Section 8.9 deliverables provide automated versions of these checks.

Mistake 3 Individual Adapter Review For Compositions

Description

Reviewing adapters individually and merging them in compositions without composition-level review

Why It Happens

Individual-adapter review is a well-defined operation: inspect this adapter, decide whether it is safe. Teams that have established individual review processes intuitively extend the pattern to compositions: review each component, compose as needed. The extension is structurally wrong because compositions have emergent behaviours that individual reviews cannot detect.

Consequence

Composition-dependent backdoors (ADAPT-COMP, ADAPT-CASCADE) pass through undetected. Each component review returns clean; the composition installs the backdoor that no individual review could have seen.

Correct Approach

Treat compositions as distinct artifacts requiring their own review. Section 8.9 Deliverable 4 provides the composition-level behavioural testing. Integrate composition review as a separate CI/CD step from individual adapter review.

Mistake 4 Assuming Base Model Cleanliness

Description

Assuming the base model is clean and focusing defence only on adapters

Why It Happens

Adapters are the new attack surface; practitioners who have learned about adapter risks focus on them. The base model is assumed clean because it comes from a foundation provider with its own defensive practices. The assumption creates a gap: base models can themselves contain backdoors (Section 8.5 Part 6 inheritance), and LoRA fine-tuning does not eliminate them.

Consequence

Adapter-level defence coverage with no base-model inheritance coverage. If the base model is backdoored, adapter review does not help; the base-model backdoor is preserved through adapter merging and into deployment.

Correct Approach

Apply adapter-level defences AND base-model inheritance auditing (Section 8.10). Both are necessary; neither is sufficient. Section 8.15 pipeline integrates both as distinct auditing tracks.

Mistake 5 Ignoring Redistribution Propagation

Description

Auditing adapters only at initial consumption and not at redistribution points

Why It Happens

Auditing at initial consumption feels like the natural defensive placement. Once an adapter passes initial review, it is trusted within the organisation. Redistribution (merging it into a model the organisation then distributes externally) inherits the trust implicitly. The inheritance is operationally reasonable for most organisational trust but structurally dangerous for external distribution.

Consequence

Organisations redistribute merged models whose provenance includes the original adapter. Downstream consumers of the redistribution inherit the adapter attack surface without independent review. The supply chain amplifies a single attack.

Correct Approach

Audit at both initial consumption and redistribution points. The latter requires verifying that the redistribution is derived from a known-clean intake and that any further modifications did not introduce new issues. Section 8.9 Deliverable 5 provides chain-of-trust auditing.

Section Summary

Compact

LoRA adapters are the dominant LLM fine-tuning regime and the LLM-specific supply chain attack surface. Adapter swap attacks are structurally simple: adversary produces an adapter with backdoor behaviour, publishes it to a distribution channel (HuggingFace PEFT hub, marketplaces), and consumers who download and merge it deploy the backdoor automatically. Five principal attack variants exist: standard backdoor adapters, stealth adapters (weight-statistics-optimised), composition-dependent backdoors, cascading adapters (exploiting companion behaviour), and supply chain attacks (typosquatting, credential compromise). Adapter distribution channels provide essentially no native provenance verification; trust must be established at the consumer side. The three principal detection primitives operate at artifact-level (weight-delta anomaly detection, provenance verification) and behavioural-level (behavioural diff testing, with separate tracks for individual adapters and compositions). LoRA persistence regime is particularly unfavourable to defenders: merged adapter backdoors persist through subsequent LoRA fine-tuning completely, and propagate through redistribution supply chains. Auditing must occur at both initial consumption and redistribution points. The Chapter 5 Section 5.9 supply chain checklist becomes operational in this section: the adapter integrity verifier, behavioural diff tester, and provenance auditor are the concrete implementations of what Section 5.9 described abstractly. Section 8.10 covers the broader supply chain (HuggingFace, model repositories, pickle exploits); Section 8.9 covers the adapter-specific subset. Section 8.15 integrates both as distinct auditing tracks in the production pipeline.

Key Takeaways

  • LoRA adapters are executable artifacts; treat them with CI/CD-level verification, not configuration-level trust
  • Adapter swap attack economics are unusually favourable to the attacker: low construction cost, passive distribution, automatic consumer activation
  • Five attack variants span standard backdoor, stealth (weight-optimised), composition-dependent, cascading, and supply chain compromise
  • Distribution channels provide no native provenance verification; consumer-side verification is mandatory
  • Three-primitive ensemble (weight-delta, behavioural diff, provenance) covers most adapter attack variants; compositions require distinct composition-level testing
  • LoRA persistence regime preserves backdoors through subsequent fine-tuning completely; auditing at intake is the primary defensive opportunity
  • Redistribution amplifies supply chain exposure; audit at both intake and redistribution points
  • Chapter 5 Section 5.9 supply chain checklist becomes operational in Section 8.9 deliverables; Section 8.15 integrates into production pipeline
