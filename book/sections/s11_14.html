Section Overview

One Line Summary

Chapter 11 built the detection framework that governs everything Chapters 12–14 implement; the seven-track gateway is now at L3 maturity with coverage gaps mapped, FPR targets set, a hunting cadence established, and a hybrid production architecture specified — the framework is complete; the remaining work is filling it.

Why This Section Exists

Chapter 11 opened with the seven-track gateway at L1 maturity: seven detection artefacts, no tests, no version history, no coverage measurement, no FPR targets, and no production architecture. Fourteen sections later, the programme has: a detection-as-code pipeline with CI/CD and rollback; a four-layer architecture model with every track placed; an ATT&CK/ATLAS coverage heat map in diff mode; a full Sigma rule portfolio with AI-specific field extensions; a five-level maturity model with the current score and a 90-day L3 roadmap; per-rule FPR targets with automated monitoring; a seven-phase engineering workflow; an OWASP LLM Top 10 coverage audit; a five-hypothesis threat hunting playbook; and a hybrid production architecture with eight observability signals. Section 11.14 synthesises that arc into a single coherent picture, scores the final programme state, identifies what the chapter leaves unimplemented and why, maps the Chapter 12–14 implementations to the framework slots they fill, and provides the six-question practitioner checklist that determines whether a detection framework is ready for production. Detection is never complete. The framework provides the mechanism for continuous improvement — not a finish line. Section 11.14 ends by naming that mechanism explicitly: the quarterly cycle of coverage measurement, FPR review, threat hunting, and maturity assessment that keeps the programme improving beyond the specific detectors Chapters 12–14 build.

The Chapter 11 Arc: From Ad-Hoc to L3 Managed Detection

Body

Chapter 11 began with a diagnosis. The gateway was at L1 across all five maturity dimensions. It was functional — it blocked real attacks — but it was not sustainable. Without governance, it would erode. The chapter then built the governance infrastructure, section by section. Section 11.02 established detection-as-code: all seven tracks migrated to Git, tests written, CI/CD pipeline deployed with rollback. Rule quality advanced from L1 to L2. Section 11.03 placed the seven tracks within the four-layer architecture — signature, heuristic, ML, behavioural — making the gateway''s structure legible and naming its two Layer 3 gaps and one Layer 4 gap. Section 11.04 anchored every track to its ATLAS technique, completing the technique- to-detection-to-Sigma chain for all eleven relevant techniques. Section 11.05 converted the technique inventory into a live coverage heat map running in CI diff mode. ATT&CK coverage advanced from L1 (unknown) to L3 (measured, regression-detected, quarterly taxonomy check running). Sections 11.06 and 11.07 built Sigma fluency: the full field reference, AI-specific logsource taxonomy, five detection operators, three worked rules, and the correlation and lifecycle management layer for the rule portfolio. Section 11.08 scored the programme: L1 composite (FPR management and hunting at L1; coverage at L3; rule quality and pipeline at L2). Named the 90-day roadmap to L3. Section 11.09 closed the FPR management debt: ROC-calibrated thresholds for Track 1's centroid gate and Tracks 2 and 7; per-rule FPR targets set; false_positive_manager.py deployed with DaC queue integration; exception management with 90-day expiry. FPR management advanced from L1 to L3. Section 11.10 codified the seven-phase detection engineering workflow from hypothesis to production, with the time-to-detect metric (seven days for catalogued techniques, 24 hours for zero-days). Section 11.11 audited OWASP LLM Top 10 coverage: four categories at score 1, three actionable score-0 gaps (LLM02, LLM06, LLM08 — all output-side), two build-time gaps, one out-of-scope category. Section 11.12 deployed the threat hunting playbook: five hypotheses, two deliverables, quarterly cadence established. Proactive Hunting advanced from L1 to L3. Section 11.13 specified the hybrid production architecture: Track 1's keyword rule blocking inline, Track 1's centroid gate and Tracks 3 and 6 (per-request) flagging inline, Track 1's classifiers and Tracks 2, 4, 5 and 7 flagging via sidecar, Track 6's per-user accumulation via SIEM. Eight observability signals instrumented. Pipeline RBAC confirmed (Deployment Pipeline advanced to L3). End-of-chapter maturity state: all five dimensions at L3. Composite: L3.

Part 3 What Remains

Heading

What Chapter 11 Deliberately Leaves Unimplemented

Body

Chapter 11 is a methodology chapter. It builds the framework that governs everything Chapters 12–14 implement. The framework is complete at the end of Chapter 11 in the sense that the governance infrastructure is in place: every new detector that Chapters 12–14 produce has a defined place in the architecture, a coverage score to improve, an FPR target to meet, a CI pipeline to pass, and an observability signal to watch after deployment. What the chapter deliberately leaves unimplemented: The ML-based detectors (Layer 3). Chapter 11 names the Layer 3 gaps — no ML anomaly detector for novel injection variants, no transformer intent classifier — and documents them in the gap backlog at priority 25 (AML.T0051 obfuscated injection) and priority 12 (AML.T0043 novel adversarial families). It does not train the classifiers. Chapter 12 trains them: Isolation Forest and One-Class SVM for anomaly detection, RandomForest and fine-tuned transformers for prompt intent classification, LSTM/GRU for multi-turn sequence modelling. The behavioural analysis system (Layer 4). Chapter 11 names the Layer 4 gaps — no cross-user extraction correlation, no training data drift monitor, no multi- session campaign detection — and maps them to priority 15 (AML.T0040 distributed extraction) and priority 10 (AML.T0020 slow-drift poisoning). Chapter 13 builds the behavioural analysis system: cross-user session correlation, training data drift monitor with statistical process control, and the graph-based prompt relationship model for campaign detection. The production deployment infrastructure. Chapter 11 specifies the hybrid architecture and names the Chapter 14 extensions: latency budget analysis, horizontal scaling with shared Redis session state, cost modelling, Kubernetes deployment manifests, and the output validation layer that closes LLM02, LLM06, and LLM08. Chapter 14 implements all of these. This sequencing — framework before implementation — is intentional. Building ML detectors without the maturity model produces ML detectors deployed without FPR targets. Building the output validation layer without the OWASP audit produces a layer that addresses undefined gaps. Building the production deployment without the observability signals produces a system that silently fails. The framework establishes the constraints; the implementations satisfy them.

Part 5 Practitioner Checklist

Heading

Six Questions Before Declaring a Detection Framework Operational

Body

A detection framework is not operational because it has been built. It is operational when it can answer six questions with evidence: Question 1: "What does this programme detect?" Evidence: the ATT&CK/ATLAS heat map from Section 11.05, showing per-technique coverage scores and the gap backlog ordered by priority. If the answer to this question is "a bunch of things" without a specific coverage map, the programme is not at L3. Question 2: "How do we know the detection is working?" Evidence: the CI diff run from Section 11.05 (no coverage regression since last check) and the alert rate per rule from the observability dashboard (no rule has been at zero rate for more than 30 minutes). If neither check is running, the detection may have silently stopped working. Question 3: "What is the false positive rate?" Evidence: the FPR report from false_positive_manager.py showing per-rule FPR against documented targets, with no rule in breach for more than 48 hours without an open DaC queue ticket. If FPR is tracked only in aggregate, the programme is at L2 on that dimension, not L3. Question 4: "What would the programme miss?" Evidence: the gap backlog from detection_gap_prioritiser.py, sorted by likelihood × impact, with the top three gaps named and their remediation chapters documented. If the answer is "we don''t know what we''re missing," the programme is at L1 on coverage. Question 5: "What happens when the detection system fails?" Evidence: the failure mode specification from Section 11.13 per track (fail-open vs fail-closed, circuit breaker configured, timeout logging enabled), and the eight observability signals wired to the monitoring system. If the failure mode has not been designed, the first production failure will be an uncontrolled event. Question 6: "How does the programme improve over time?" Evidence: the quarterly cycle running: coverage measurement (attck_coverage_heatmap.py --diff), FPR review (false_positive_manager.py --report), threat hunting (threat_hunting_playbook.py --run all), and maturity assessment (detection_maturity_assessor.py). If none of these run on a schedule, the programme has no self-improvement mechanism and will degrade as the threat landscape evolves. A programme that can answer all six with evidence is operational. A programme that can answer fewer than four is not ready for production against external adversaries.

Part 6 Quarterly Cycle

Heading

The Quarterly Improvement Cycle

Body

The detection framework is complete when it has a self-improvement mechanism. That mechanism is the quarterly cycle: four activities that run together, each feeding the next. Week 1 of the quarter — Coverage measurement and taxonomy update. Run attck_coverage_heatmap.py --diff against the previous quarter''s baseline. Run --check-new-techniques against the previous quarter''s technique list. Review the output: which technique scores improved, which regressed, which new techniques were published and are now undetected. Update the gap backlog in detection_gap_prioritiser.py with any new P0 or P1 items. Week 2 — FPR review and threshold calibration. Run false_positive_manager.py --report. Review the 7-day FPR trends for all rules. For any rule with a degrading trend approaching but not yet breaching its target, run threshold_calibration_harness.py on the most recent production sample. Update thresholds where calibration recommends a change; re-run the coverage heat map diff after applying changes to catch coverage regressions. Week 3 — Threat hunting. Run threat_hunting_playbook.py --run all against the 30-day window. File a hunt report for each hypothesis (confirmed_finding, coverage_validated, or inconclusive). For any confirmed findings, initiate the Section 11.10 detection engineering workflow to convert them to rules. Add any new hypotheses for the quarter based on newly published ATLAS techniques identified in Week 1. Week 4 — Maturity assessment and planning. Run detection_maturity_assessor.py against the current programme state. Compare to the previous quarter''s score. If any dimension has regressed, identify the cause and add a remediation item to the next sprint. Update the 90-day roadmap from maturity_improvement_roadmap.py. Append the maturity score and the quarter''s key findings to decisions.md (append- only, per the ADR discipline established in the book framework). Schedule the next quarter''s cycle. The four weeks are sequential by design. Coverage measurement identifies gaps that hunting should target. FPR review identifies miscalibrated thresholds that should be corrected before hunting begins (to reduce noise in the hunting data). Hunting identifies patterns that calibrated rules should detect. The maturity assessment confirms that all three preceding activities improved the programme measurably. This cycle is the answer to "how does the programme improve over time." It is not a one-time activity and it is not optional for a programme at L3. It is the operational cadence of managed detection.

def _file_exists(*paths: str) -> tuple[bool, str]:
    for p in paths:
        if pathlib.Path(p).exists():
            return True, f'Found: {p}'
    return False, f'Not found: {", ".join(paths)}'

def _json_field_present(path: str, field_name: str) -> tuple[bool, str]:
    try:
        with open(path) as f:
            data = json.load(f)
        if field_name in data:
            return True, f'{path} contains field "{field_name}"'
        return False, f'{path} missing field "{field_name}"'
    except Exception as exc:
        return False, f'Could not read {path}: {exc}'

def _file_newer_than(path: str, days: int) -> tuple[bool, str]:
    p = pathlib.Path(path)
    if not p.exists():
        return False, f'{path} does not exist'
    mtime = datetime.fromtimestamp(p.stat().st_mtime, tz=timezone.utc)
    cutoff = datetime.now(timezone.utc) - timedelta(days=days)
    if mtime >= cutoff:
        return True, f'{path} last modified {mtime.strftime("%Y-%m-%d")} (within {days} days)'
    return False, f'{path} last modified {mtime.strftime("%Y-%m-%d")} (older than {days} days)'

# Audit runner

def run_element_audit(element: dict) -> ElementAuditResult:
    results: list[tuple[bool, str]] = []
    for _check_name, check_fn in element['checks']:
        try:
            result = check_fn()
            if isinstance(result, tuple) and len(result) == 2:
                results.append(result)
            else:
                results.append((bool(result), str(result)))
        except Exception as exc:
            results.append((False, f'Check error: {exc}'))

    overall = all(r[0] for r in results)
    return ElementAuditResult(
        element_id=element['element_id'],
        element_name=element['element_name'],
        section=element['section'],
        checks=results,
        overall=overall,
    )

def run_full_audit(
    elements: list[dict],
    target_element: int | None = None,
) -> list[ElementAuditResult]:
    if target_element:
        elements = [e for e in elements if e['element_id'] == target_element]
    return [run_element_audit(e) for e in elements]

# Report
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def print_audit_report(results: list[ElementAuditResult]) -> int:
    passed = [r for r in results if r.overall]
    failed = [r for r in results if not r.overall]

    print('\nDetection Framework Audit — Chapter 11')
    print(f'Elements audited: {len(results)}')
    print(f'Passed: {len(passed)}  Failed: {len(failed)}')
    print()

    for r in results:
        icon = '✓' if r.overall else '✗'
        print(f'{icon} Element {r.element_id}: {r.element_name} (Section {r.section})')
        for i, (check_name, _) in enumerate(
            [(name, fn) for name, fn in
             next(e['checks'] for e in ELEMENT_AUDITS if e['element_id'] == r.element_id)]
        ):
            passed_check, evidence = r.checks[i]
            check_icon = '  ✓' if passed_check else '  ✗'
            print(f'{check_icon} {check_name}')
            if not passed_check:
                print(f'      Evidence: {evidence}')
    print()

    if failed:
        print(f'Framework health: {len(passed)}/{len(results)} elements operational.')
        print('Action required for failed elements before declaring framework production-ready.')
        return 1
    print('Framework health: ALL elements operational. Detection framework is production-ready.')
    return 0

Common Mistakes

Id

CM11.14.1

Mistake

Treating Chapter 11 as optional reading before jumping to Chapter 12 to build ML detectors

Why It Happens

ML-based detection is technically exciting. Practitioners who are comfortable with machine learning want to build classifiers immediately. The framework work in Chapter 11 — CI pipelines, Sigma rules, maturity models — feels like overhead.

Why It Matters

An ML detector deployed without the Chapter 11 framework has no FPR target, no CI test coverage, no ATLAS technique tag, no coverage contribution to the heat map, no failure mode specification, and no observability signals. It may perform well in development and degrade silently in production. The framework is not overhead; it is the governance layer without which ML detectors become the most expensive ad-hoc scripts in the programme.

Correct Approach

Complete the Chapter 11 framework before deploying any Chapter 12 detector. Specifically: run detection_framework_audit.py and confirm all nine elements pass before writing the first ML training script. The audit is the gate.

Id

CM11.14.2

Mistake

Declaring the detection framework operational after building the tooling but before running the quarterly cycle

Why It Happens

The deliverables are written, the CI pipeline is running, the maturity score says L3. The framework feels done. The first quarterly review is scheduled "for next quarter."

Why It Matters

A framework that has never run its own quarterly cycle is an untested framework. The first cycle reveals the gaps between design and operation: the DBSCAN epsilon that is wrong for the embedding model, the SIEM that was unavailable for the first coverage diff, the FPR target that is wrong for actual production traffic. These discoveries in the first quarter are expected; discovering them in a security incident because the quarterly cycle was never run is not.

Correct Approach

Complete the first quarterly cycle within 30 days of declaring the framework operational. Treat the first cycle output as the true baseline — not the maturity score from the theoretical assessment in Section 11.08.

Id

CM11.14.3

Mistake

Answering fewer than four of the six practitioner checklist questions with evidence

Why It Happens

The six questions feel like a formality. The detection programme has been running for months. The answers seem obvious. Evidence collection is time-consuming.

Why It Matters

Questions that seem obvious often have answers that are less comfortable when evidence is required. "What does this programme detect?" answered with a heat map may show more amber than expected. "What would the programme miss?" answered with a gap backlog may reveal Priority 25 items that were deprioritised for months. "How do we know detection is working?" may reveal that the alert rate signal was never wired to the monitoring system. Evidence collection surfaces real state.

Correct Approach

Run detection_framework_audit.py and answer all six questions before the next CISO briefing or security review. If fewer than four can be answered with evidence, defer the declaration of operational status until the remaining evidence is in place.

Id

CM11.14.4

Mistake

Treating the quarterly cycle as a once-per-year audit rather than a four-week operational cadence

Why It Happens

"Quarterly" is interpreted as "end of quarter." The cycle is scheduled once per quarter in week 13, completed in a compressed three-day sprint, and forgotten until week 13 of the next quarter.

Why It Matters

The four-week structure of the quarterly cycle is load-bearing: Week 1 coverage feeds Week 3 hunting hypotheses; Week 2 FPR review improves data quality for Week 3 hunting; Week 4 maturity assessment synthesises all three. Compressing the cycle into three days loses these inter-week dependencies and produces a superficial review.

Correct Approach

Treat each of the four weeks as a distinct scheduled activity with its own calendar invite, owner, and deliverable. The Week 1 coverage report is produced by the detection engineer; the Week 2 FPR review is done jointly with the SOC team lead; the Week 3 hunt is run by the analyst team; the Week 4 maturity assessment is presented to the detection engineering lead. Distribute the work across the team and across the quarter.

Id

CM11.14.5

Mistake

Viewing the detection framework as finished once all nine elements are built

Why It Happens

Building nine elements across fourteen sections is a substantial effort. Completing it produces a sense of accomplishment and completion. The instinct is to declare "done" and move on.

Why It Matters

Detection frameworks that are treated as finished degrade. ATLAS adds new techniques that go undetected. Attacker evasion evolves beyond deployed rules. FPR drifts as traffic patterns change. Rules calibrated on last year''s traffic become miscalibrated this year. A finished framework is a degrading framework.

Correct Approach

The framework is the mechanism for continuous improvement, not a deliverable to be completed. The quarterly cycle is the operational expression of this principle. The correct framing is not "we have built the detection framework" but "we have deployed the detection programme and begun its first operational quarter."
