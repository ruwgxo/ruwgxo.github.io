Title

EU AI Act: Risk Tier Architecture and Prohibited Uses

Content

overview_and_status: | The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) entered into force on 1 August 2024 as the world's first comprehensive horizontal AI regulation. It applies to any provider placing AI systems on the EU market or putting them into service in the EU, regardless of where the provider is established. Extraterritorial reach is explicit: a US company deploying an AI system used by EU residents is in scope. Enforcement is phased, with full application from August 2026. four_risk_tiers: tier_01_unacceptable_risk: label: "Unacceptable Risk — Prohibited" enforcement_date: "February 2025" prohibited_practices: - "Subliminal manipulation causing harm (beyond voluntary attention engineering)" - "Exploitation of vulnerabilities of specific groups (age, disability)" - "Social scoring by public authorities for general purposes" - "Real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces by law enforcement (with narrow exceptions)" - "Emotion recognition in the workplace or educational institutions" - "Biometric categorisation revealing race, political opinion, religious belief, sexual orientation" - "Predictive policing based solely on profiling without individual criminal activity evidence" security_engineering_note: | Prohibited-tier surveillance AI is directly relevant to security product vendors. Any AI-powered surveillance system targeting EU residents must confirm it does not fall within these categories. Emotion recognition at a workplace security checkpoint is explicitly prohibited. Real-time facial recognition at public events is prohibited absent a law-enforcement exception. tier_02_high_risk: label: "High Risk — Conformity Assessment Required" enforcement_date: "August 2026 (full application)" annex_iii_categories: - "Biometric identification and categorisation of natural persons" - "Management and operation of critical infrastructure (water, gas, electricity, roads)" - "Education and vocational training (exam scoring, admission decisions)" - "Employment, worker management, access to self-employment (CV screening, promotion)" - "Access to essential private services and public services (credit scoring, benefits)" - "Law enforcement (risk assessment, evidence reliability, crime prediction)" - "Migration, asylum, border control (risk assessment, document verification)" - "Administration of justice and democratic processes (court AI, election AI)" technical_obligations: "See 5.13.2 — five mandatory technical controls" security_engineering_note: | Fraud detection (access to essential private services), anomaly detection in critical infrastructure, and AI-assisted security investigations (law enforcement adjacent) all land in Annex III. If your organisation deploys AI in any of these categories, the high-risk technical obligations apply. tier_03_limited_risk: label: "Limited Risk — Transparency Obligations Only" enforcement_date: "August 2026" obligations: - "Chatbots and conversational AI: disclose AI nature to users" - "Deepfake content: label AI-generated content as such" - "General-purpose AI (GPAI) models: technical documentation, copyright policy, training data summary" security_engineering_note: | Most commercial LLM deployments (customer service, coding assistants, internal tools) fall here. Transparency obligations are light — but GPAI providers with systemic risk designation face additional obligations (see tier_04). tier_04_minimal_risk: label: "Minimal Risk — Voluntary Codes of Practice Only" examples: "Spam filters, AI-enabled video games, recommendation systems with low stakes" security_engineering_note: "No mandatory technical controls. Voluntary best practices apply." special_category_gpai_systemic_risk: label: "GPAI Models with Systemic Risk — Enhanced Obligations" threshold: "Training compute ≥ 10^25 FLOPs (indicative)" additional_obligations: - "Model evaluation against state-of-the-art benchmarks" - "Adversarial testing (red-teaming) against systemic risks" - "Incident reporting to EU AI Office" - "Cybersecurity protection for the model and its training infrastructure" security_engineering_note: | This is the regulation that mandates red-teaming for large foundation models. The EU AI Office published guidance (2025) specifying that adversarial testing must cover the OWASP LLM Top 10 and MITRE ATLAS categories — directly mapping to Sections 5.3-5.8 of this book.

EU AI Act Technical Obligations for High-Risk AI Systems

Content

the_five_mandatory_controls: | EU AI Act Chapter III, Section 2 (Articles 8-15) imposes five categories of technical obligation on high-risk AI systems. These are not process requirements — they are documented, auditable technical controls. Non-compliance is an enforcement risk; documented compliance is the defence. obligation_01_risk_management_system: article: "Article 9" requirement: | Establish, implement, document, and maintain a risk management system covering the entire AI system lifecycle: design, development, validation, deployment, monitoring, and decommissioning. Must include: risk identification, risk estimation, risk evaluation, risk mitigation measures, and residual risk acceptance. security_engineering_mapping: | The bias audit procedure (Section 5.12.7), the privacy risk audit (Section 5.11.7), and the ATLAS coverage assessment (Section 5.8.8) are all Article 9 risk management system components. Document them as such. The AI RMF MANAGE function (Section 5.14) provides the organisational scaffolding. documentation_artefact: "Risk management system document; risk register" obligation_02_data_governance: article: "Article 10" requirement: | Training, validation, and test datasets must meet quality criteria: - Relevant, representative, and adequately complete for intended purpose - Free from errors and complete with respect to statistical properties - Appropriate for the geographical, contextual, behavioural, and functional settings in which the AI system will be used Data governance practices documented: data collection, labelling, preprocessing. security_engineering_mapping: | ML SBOM Layer 1 (dataset provenance — Section 5.9) directly satisfies Article 10. Memorisation risk scoring (Section 5.10) and bias auditing (Section 5.12) produce the representativeness and error-free evidence Article 10 requires. documentation_artefact: "ML SBOM dataset layer; bias audit report Stage 2" obligation_03_technical_documentation: article: "Article 11 + Annex IV" requirement: | Maintain technical documentation demonstrating conformity before market placement. Annex IV specifies required content: system description, training methodology, training data characteristics, validation and testing procedures, performance metrics, known limitations, monitoring, logging, and human oversight measures. security_engineering_mapping: | Model cards (Section 5.12.7) + ML SBOM (Section 5.9) + privacy risk audit report (Section 5.11.7) collectively cover the Annex IV requirements. Treat the combined documentation package as the Annex IV technical file. documentation_artefact: "Model card (Markdown); ML SBOM (YAML); privacy audit report" obligation_04_accuracy_robustness_cybersecurity: article: "Article 15" requirement: | High-risk AI systems must be designed to achieve appropriate levels of: Accuracy: system performance meets declared specifications across anticipated inputs Robustness: system performs consistently under input variation, adversarial conditions, and unexpected inputs Cybersecurity: system is resilient against attempts to alter use, behaviour, or performance — specifically including adversarial examples and data poisoning The regulation explicitly names adversarial examples and data poisoning as cybersecurity threats. security_engineering_mapping: | This is the Article that makes ATLAS detection programme (Section 5.8) a compliance deliverable. Specifically: AML.T0019/T0020 detection → Article 15 data poisoning defence AML.T0015/T0043 detection → Article 15 adversarial example defence AML.T0025/T0029 detection → Article 15 availability and performance defence Document the detection programme as Article 15 cybersecurity controls. documentation_artefact: "ATLAS detection matrix (Section 5.8); Sigma rules from Section 5.8.8" obligation_05_human_oversight: article: "Article 14" requirement: | High-risk AI systems must be designed to enable effective oversight by natural persons. The system must allow operators to: understand system outputs and their significance, monitor system operation, override, interrupt, or stop the system, and identify anomalous situations. security_engineering_mapping: | Human-in-the-loop requirements, alert escalation paths, and model output explanation tools contribute to Article 14 compliance. The model card's human oversight recommendation section (Section 5.12.7) is Article 14 documentation. documentation_artefact: "Model card Section 6 (monitoring recommendations); alert runbooks" eu_ai_act_enforcement_structure: national_competent_authorities: "Each EU member state designates authority for high-risk AI" eu_ai_office: "Oversees GPAI models; can conduct evaluations and impose corrective measures" penalties: prohibited_practices: "Up to €35M or 7% global annual turnover" high_risk_violations: "Up to €15M or 3% global annual turnover" incorrect_information: "Up to €7.5M or 1.5% global annual turnover" enforcement_timeline: feb_2025: "Prohibited practices provisions apply" aug_2025: "GPAI model obligations and governance provisions apply" aug_2026: "High-risk obligations and transparency obligations fully apply" aug_2027: "High-risk AI systems embedded in regulated products"

Title

US Executive Order on AI: Red-Teaming, NIST Standards, and Safety Reporting

Content

eo_14110_context: | Status update: EO 14110 was revoked on 20 January 2025 by Executive Order 14148, and its reporting and red-teaming mandates are no longer in force. The section is kept as historical context because the practices it promoted, and the NIST guidance written under it, are still widely used. US Executive Order 14110 "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence" was signed October 30, 2023. It is not legislation — it directs federal agencies and establishes reporting requirements for AI developers. However, its effect on the AI industry is substantial because: (a) federal procurement requirements flow from it, (b) NIST standards mandated by it become de facto industry standards, and (c) the safety reporting threshold provisions affect the largest model developers. key_security_mandates: mandate_01_safety_reporting_threshold: provision: "Section 4.2(a)" requirement: | Developers of dual-use foundation models who train a model using a quantity of computing power greater than 10^26 floating-point operations (FLOPs) must report to the federal government. Reports include: physical and cybersecurity measures protecting model weights, results of red-team testing, and any identified safety risks. practical_implication: | This applies to GPT-4/5-scale and Gemini-scale models. The 10^26 FLOP threshold is roughly three orders of magnitude above models like Llama 3 70B. For security engineers at frontier AI labs: red-team results are now federal disclosures. The red-team programme is a compliance function, not just R&D. mandate_02_nist_standards_for_red_teaming: provision: "Section 4.1 / NIST mandate" requirement: | NIST to develop guidelines for AI red-teaming, evaluation, and testing. NIST AI RMF (Section 5.14) and NIST SP 800-218A (AI security) are the primary standards deliverables. Federal agencies must use these standards for AI procurement and deployment. practical_implication: | NIST AI RMF MEASURE function becomes the standard evaluation framework for any AI system sold to the US federal government. Security engineers building AI products for government customers need NIST AI RMF alignment. mandate_03_model_weight_security: provision: "Section 4.2(b)" requirement: | Agencies must identify cybersecurity risks associated with AI systems, particularly related to the security of model weights. Model weights are explicitly identified as a critical national security asset for dual-use foundation models. practical_implication: | AML.T0035 (collect ML artifacts) and AML.T0044 (full model access) detection from Section 5.8 are the technical implementation of this mandate. Storage access monitoring and registry access controls for model weights are EO-mandated security controls for federal-adjacent AI deployments. mandate_04_privacy_preserving_techniques: provision: "Section 9" requirement: | Federal agencies directed to advance privacy-preserving AI technologies including differential privacy, federated learning, and privacy-enhancing technologies. practical_implication: | DP training (Section 5.10.5) is an EO-endorsed privacy control. For federal contracting or grant-funded AI work: DP training with documented epsilon constitutes demonstrable compliance with Section 9 direction. mandate_05_content_authentication: provision: "Section 4.5" requirement: | Develop guidance for authenticating AI-generated content and watermarking. Federal agencies to develop technical standards for detecting AI-generated content. practical_implication: | AML.T0048.002 (influence operations via generative AI) detection from Section 5.8 is the security engineering response to this mandate. AI content detection and watermarking tools are a mandated federal investment area. nist_ai_rmf_relationship: | NIST AI RMF 1.0 was published in January 2023 under the National AI Initiative Act, before the EO. The EO directed NIST to extend it, which produced the generative AI profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024). The framework is voluntary and outlived the EO. Section 5.14 covers NIST AI RMF in full. The relationship is: NIST AI RMF (voluntary, primary US framework) → NIST AI RMF MEASURE function → maps to detection engineering programme → detection programme outputs satisfy EO and NIST compliance evidence

Title

GDPR-AI Intersection: Training Data, Explanation, and Erasure

Content

gdpr_ai_overview: | GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation, 2018) was not designed for AI systems but applies fully to any AI processing personal data of EU residents. Key provisions create specific obligations and conflicts for AI deployments — several of which were identified in earlier sections. This subsection maps the full set of GDPR obligations to AI engineering controls. article_mapping: article_5_lawfulness_purpose_minimisation: title: "Article 5 — Data Processing Principles" ai_obligation: | AI training on personal data requires a lawful basis (Article 6). Purpose limitation: training data collected for one purpose cannot be repurposed for AI training without new legal basis. Data minimisation: training data should be limited to what is necessary. engineering_control: | Data provenance documentation (ML SBOM Layer 1). PII density measurement and reduction before training. Corpus risk scorer (Section 5.10 implementation) documents minimisation effort. article_13_14_transparency: title: "Articles 13-14 — Transparency and Information Rights" ai_obligation: | Data subjects must be informed when their data is used in AI training, including the purpose, legal basis, and any automated decision-making. General privacy notice language ("we use data to improve our services") is insufficient for AI training disclosure under post-GDPR enforcement. engineering_control: | Training data disclosure in model card (Section 5.12.7). Data subject notification process documented before training begins. article_17_right_to_erasure: title: "Article 17 — Right to Erasure (Right to be Forgotten)" ai_obligation: | Data subjects may request deletion of their personal data. For AI models trained on personal data, this creates the fundamental conflict documented in Section 5.10.6: erasing data from model weights is technically distinct from deleting a database record. engineering_control: | DP training with documented epsilon (provides mathematical bound — Section 5.10.5). SISA training or gradient ascent unlearning for erasure requests (Section 5.11). Data minimisation before training reduces exposure. Erasure capability documentation in Article 30 records. article_22_automated_decision_making: title: "Article 22 — Automated Individual Decision-Making" ai_obligation: | Individuals have the right not to be subject to solely automated decisions that produce significant effects, unless the individual has consented, the decision is necessary for contract performance, or is authorised by law. Right to human review and to contest the decision applies. engineering_control: | Human oversight mechanisms (EU AI Act Article 14 alignment). Explanation tools: model output interpretation, confidence scores, decision factors. Audit log of automated decisions with human review escalation path. article_25_privacy_by_design: title: "Article 25 — Data Protection by Design and Default" ai_obligation: | Controllers must implement technical measures to ensure data protection principles are implemented by design. For AI: DP training, output monitoring, access controls on training data and model weights are Article 25 measures. engineering_control: | Privacy defence layer (Section 5.11 implementation) is Article 25 evidence. DP training with documented epsilon is an Article 25 technical measure. ML SBOM and access controls on training data document the design-stage controls. article_30_records_of_processing: title: "Article 30 — Records of Processing Activities" ai_obligation: | Controllers must maintain a record of processing activities including: purposes, categories of data subjects and personal data, recipients, transfers to third countries, retention periods, security measures. For AI: this record must cover training data sources and model deployment. engineering_control: | ML SBOM (Section 5.9) is the Article 30 record for AI processing. Model card technical documentation section covers processing description. article_35_dpia: title: "Article 35 — Data Protection Impact Assessment" ai_obligation: | DPIA required before processing likely to result in high risk to individuals. High-risk processing includes: systematic profiling with significant effects, large-scale processing of special categories of data, systematic monitoring. AI systems doing any of these require a documented DPIA. engineering_control: | Privacy risk audit (Section 5.11.7) is the technical component of the DPIA. Bias audit report (Section 5.12.7) covers the profiling risk dimension. Combined outputs constitute the technical DPIA evidence package. gdpr_ai_compliance_summary_table: training_data_lawfulness: "Article 5 + 6 — document legal basis before training" transparency_to_subjects: "Articles 13-14 — update privacy notice before training" erasure_requests: "Article 17 — DP training + unlearning capability required" automated_decisions: "Article 22 — human review path for significant decisions" privacy_by_design: "Article 25 — DP training + output controls + access controls" processing_records: "Article 30 — ML SBOM is the Article 30 record" impact_assessment: "Article 35 — privacy risk audit is the technical DPIA"

Sector-Specific Overlays: HIPAA, SOX/GLBA, and Legal AI

Content

overlay_principle: | Sector-specific regulations impose additional obligations on top of horizontal frameworks (EU AI Act, GDPR). The overlay model applies: sector regulation sets the floor for that sector; horizontal regulation provides the additional baseline; security engineering provides the ceiling. All three must be satisfied. hipaa_healthcare_ai: scope: | US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) applies to covered entities and business associates processing Protected Health Information (PHI). AI systems that process PHI — including clinical NLP, diagnostic AI, and any LLM fine-tuned on clinical data — are in scope. key_requirements_for_ai: minimum_necessary: | HIPAA's minimum necessary standard applied to AI training means using the minimum amount of PHI required for the training purpose. This directly supports data minimisation and reduces extraction risk. safeguards_rule: | Administrative, physical, and technical safeguards must protect PHI. For AI: technical safeguards on training data (encryption, access control), model weights (restricted access, as per Section 5.9), and inference outputs (PII scan before return, as per Section 5.10). baa_for_model_providers: | If a covered entity uses a third-party AI provider (API calls sending PHI), a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) is required. Most major AI providers offer BAAs — but the covered entity must verify the provider has an appropriate data processing agreement before any PHI-bearing prompt is sent. breach_notification: | If AI output contains PHI (verbatim recall event — Section 5.10), it constitutes a potential HIPAA breach. The output scanner (Section 5.10 implementation) is therefore both a security control and a breach detection mechanism. engineering_controls: - "DP training with ε ≤ 2 for models fine-tuned on PHI (Section 5.10.5)" - "PII output scan before returning inference results (Section 5.10 impl)" - "BAA in place before sending any PHI to external model provider" - "Access controls on PHI training data: HIPAA minimum necessary + Section 5.9 checks" - "Training data MIA AUC ≤ 0.55 (Section 5.11.5 medical threshold)" sox_and_glba_financial_ai: scope: | Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX) imposes internal control and audit trail requirements on US public companies and their IT systems, including AI systems used in financial reporting and material business processes. Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA) applies to financial institutions processing consumer financial data. key_requirements_for_ai: sox_internal_controls: | SOX Section 404 requires documented internal controls over financial reporting. AI systems used in financial reporting (e.g. revenue recognition models, fraud detection in payment processing) must have documented controls, audit trails, and access controls equivalent to other financial systems. sox_audit_trail: | AI decisions affecting financial reporting must be auditable. Log retention for AI inference decisions affecting financial statements: minimum 7 years (SOX retention requirement). glba_safeguards_rule: | GLBA Safeguards Rule (2023 updated) requires financial institutions to implement a comprehensive information security programme. Explicitly covers AI models used in customer-facing processes. Requires: access controls, encryption, incident response plan, regular testing of safeguards. glba_model_risk_management: | OCC/Fed/FDIC supervisory guidance on model risk management (SR 11-7) requires: model documentation, validation (including adversarial testing), ongoing monitoring, and explainability for models used in credit decisions. engineering_controls: - "Audit log retention for AI decisions: 7 years minimum (SOX)" - "Model validation including bias audit before credit/fraud model deployment" - "Adversarial testing documentation for SR 11-7 model risk management" - "GLBA Safeguards Rule: encrypt model weights, restrict access, incident plan" legal_ai: scope: | AI used in legal proceedings, contract review, and legal research faces a patchwork of obligations from bar associations, court rules, and emerging legislation. Unlike HIPAA and SOX, there is no single federal statute — but obligations are real and enforcement is emerging. key_requirements: attorney_client_privilege: | If an LLM processes privileged communications, the privilege analysis applies. Using a third-party LLM API for privileged document review requires analysis of whether the API provider constitutes a waiver. court_rules_on_ai_assisted_filings: | Multiple US federal courts now require disclosure when AI is used to draft legal filings, with the filing attorney certifying AI-generated content was reviewed for accuracy. AI hallucination risk is a direct liability exposure. training_data_in_legal_proceedings: | If a model is trained on privileged or confidential client data, opposing counsel may seek to determine membership (Section 5.11) — establishing the model was trained on their client's confidential information. engineering_controls: - "No PHI or privileged documents in training data without explicit authorisation" - "MIA self-assessment before deploying any model trained on client data" - "Hallucination monitoring for legal output (factual accuracy monitoring)" - "Document AI tool usage in legal workflows for disclosure obligations"

ENISA, China AI Regulations, and Global Regulatory Fragmentation

Content

enisa_ai_threat_landscape: | The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) published its AI Threat Landscape report series, providing a cybersecurity-focused complement to the EU AI Act's safety and rights framing. Key contributions to the regulatory stack: enisa_threat_taxonomy: | ENISA's AI threat taxonomy aligns closely with MITRE ATLAS (Section 5.7-5.8): adversarial examples, data poisoning, model theft, membership inference, and supply chain attacks are all covered. ENISA frames these as cybersecurity threats requiring technical countermeasures, not just policy responses. For compliance purposes: ENISA threat taxonomy + ATLAS detection programme constitutes the dual-authority technical basis for EU AI Act Article 15 cybersecurity obligations. enisa_guidelines_for_securing_ai: | ENISA's 2023 Guidelines for Securing AI provide concrete technical recommendations: - Adversarial robustness testing before deployment - Supply chain verification (maps to Section 5.9) - Monitoring for distributional drift and adversarial inputs - Incident response procedures for AI-specific incidents These guidelines are not legally binding but represent the expected standard for demonstrating EU AI Act Article 15 cybersecurity compliance. china_ai_regulations: | China has implemented the most granular AI-specific regulations globally, ahead of the EU AI Act's phased implementation: algorithmic_recommendation_regulation_2022: | Applies to recommendation algorithms serving Chinese users. Requirements: transparency, explainability, user controls, prohibition on exploiting addiction or illegal price discrimination. Enforced by CAC. generative_ai_regulation_2023: | Applies to generative AI services (text, image, audio, video) available to Chinese public. Requirements: content safety evaluation before launch, labelling of AI-generated content, training data legitimacy, prohibition on generating illegal content. Providers must file with CAC/MIIT. implication_for_global_deployments: | Any AI product with Chinese user base requires Chinese regulatory compliance independently of EU AI Act. The content safety evaluation required for Chinese launch maps to the red-teaming and adversarial testing programme described in this book. global_regulatory_fragmentation: | Summary of key jurisdictions and their approach as of 2025-2026: jurisdiction_eu: framework: "EU AI Act" approach: "Risk-tier horizontal regulation with technical obligations" status: "In force; high-risk obligations from August 2026" jurisdiction_us: framework: "Sector-specific rules (HIPAA, SOX, FTC) + voluntary NIST AI RMF" approach: "Executive action + voluntary frameworks + existing sector rules" status: "No federal AI statute; EO 14110 revoked in January 2025" jurisdiction_uk: framework: "Pro-innovation principles-based approach" approach: "Sector regulators apply existing frameworks to AI (FCA, CQC, ICO)" status: "No dedicated AI statute; reviewing post-Brexit divergence from EU" jurisdiction_china: framework: "Vertical regulations by AI application type" approach: "Prescriptive technical requirements; CAC/MIIT enforcement" status: "Generative AI regulation in force since 2023" jurisdiction_canada: framework: "AIDA (Artificial Intelligence and Data Act)" approach: "Risk-based, GDPR-influenced, biometrics and high-impact AI focus" status: "Bill C-27, which contained AIDA, died when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025; no federal AI statute" jurisdiction_singapore: framework: "Model AI Governance Framework (PDPC)" approach: "Non-binding guidance; sector regulators apply to high-risk domains" status: "Voluntary framework; MAS (finance) has binding AI guidance" fragmentation_implication: | Global AI deployments require multi-jurisdiction compliance. The minimum viable compliance programme for a global LLM product must satisfy: EU AI Act (strictest horizontal), GDPR (strictest data), sector-specific (HIPAA/SOX where applicable), and Chinese regulations where Chinese users are served. No single standard covers all jurisdictions.

Title

Compliance as Floor: Mapping Regulatory Requirements to Security Engineering

Content

the_floor_ceiling_principle: | Regulatory compliance is the floor — the minimum technically defensible position that avoids enforcement action. Security engineering is the ceiling — the maximum technically achievable protection against adversarial attack. The floor and ceiling are not the same, and the distance between them is where most security incidents occur. The most dangerous position is assuming that meeting the regulatory floor provides adequate security protection. EU AI Act Article 15 compliance (documented robustness controls) does not prevent a skilled adversary from extracting training data. GDPR Article 25 compliance (privacy by design documentation) does not prevent membership inference by a motivated attacker with API access. The regulatory floor is a legal minimum, not a threat model. regulatory_to_security_mapping_table: eu_ai_act_article_9_risk_management: regulatory_floor: "Risk management system documented" security_ceiling: "ATLAS full detection programme (all 32 techniques); continuous red-teaming" gap: "Documentation vs. operating detection programme" eu_ai_act_article_10_data_governance: regulatory_floor: "Training data quality documented" security_ceiling: "ML SBOM + canary tokens + deduplication + DP training + continuous dataset monitoring" gap: "Documentation vs. operational integrity controls" eu_ai_act_article_15_cybersecurity: regulatory_floor: "Cybersecurity measures described for adversarial examples and data poisoning" security_ceiling: "AML.T0015/T0019/T0020/T0025/T0043 detection rules deployed and monitored" gap: "Description vs. deployed, tuned, monitored detection rules" gdpr_article_17_erasure: regulatory_floor: "Erasure request process documented" security_ceiling: "DP training ε ≤ 2 + SISA unlearning + canary token extraction monitoring" gap: "Process documentation vs. technical erasure capability" gdpr_article_25_privacy_by_design: regulatory_floor: "Privacy controls listed in design documentation" security_ceiling: "DP training deployed + output scanner in serving pipeline + MIA AUC measured and within threshold" gap: "Listed vs. deployed and measured" us_eo_red_teaming: regulatory_floor: "Red-teaming performed and results reported (for qualifying models)" security_ceiling: "Continuous red-team programme with ATLAS/OWASP coverage, regression testing per model version" gap: "Point-in-time vs. continuous adversarial testing" three_conditions_where_compliance_equals_security: | Three conditions under which regulatory compliance and security engineering produce equivalent outcomes (rare but achievable): (1) The regulation specifies concrete technical controls (not just principles) AND those controls are implemented operationally (not just documented) AND the controls are continuously monitored for effectiveness EU AI Act Article 15 can reach this bar for well-resourced high-risk AI providers if the cybersecurity measures are deployed detection rules, not policy statements. (2) The threat model is bounded by regulatory scope — adversaries do not exceed what the regulation was designed to address. This rarely holds in practice. (3) The organisation's security programme exceeds the regulatory floor and uses compliance obligations as a funding and governance mechanism for the ceiling. This is the target state. positioning_security_work_as_compliance_deliverables: | Security engineers who understand the regulatory mapping can position their technical work as compliance evidence, enabling budget justification through regulatory obligation: Detection programme (Chapters 11-13) → EU AI Act Article 15 cybersecurity compliance Privacy audit (Sections 5.10-5.11) → GDPR Article 35 DPIA technical component Bias audit (Section 5.12) → EU AI Act Article 9 + GDPR Article 25 evidence ML SBOM (Section 5.9) → EU AI Act Article 11 + GDPR Article 30 record Model card (Section 5.12.7) → EU AI Act Annex IV technical documentation Red-team results (Chapter 15) → NIST AI RMF MEASURE evidence (formerly US EO 14110 Section 4.2 reporting, revoked January 2025) The compliance framing does not change the security engineering work — it changes who approves the budget and how the work is described to non-technical stakeholders.

def classify_tier(deployment_attributes: List[str]) -> TierClassification:
    """
    Classify EU AI Act tier from a list of deployment attribute strings.
    Returns the highest applicable tier.
    """
    attrs = set(deployment_attributes)

    if attrs & set(PROHIBITED_INDICATORS):
        return TierClassification(
            tier="prohibited", tier_label="Unacceptable Risk — Prohibited",
            obligations=["CEASE OPERATION — this use is prohibited under EU AI Act Article 5"],
            enforcement_date="February 2025",
            penalties="Up to €35M or 7% global annual turnover",
            indicators_matched=list(attrs & set(PROHIBITED_INDICATORS)),
        )

    matched_gpai_systemic = list(attrs & set(GPAI_SYSTEMIC_RISK_INDICATORS))
    matched_high = list(attrs & set(HIGH_RISK_ANNEX_III))
    matched_limited = list(attrs & set(LIMITED_RISK_INDICATORS))

    if matched_high:
        return TierClassification(
            tier="high_risk",
            tier_label="High Risk — Conformity Assessment + Technical Obligations",
            obligations=[
                "Article 9: Risk management system (documented lifecycle risk process)",
                "Article 10: Data governance (training data quality documentation)",
                "Article 11: Technical documentation (Annex IV technical file)",
                "Article 14: Human oversight (override capability, anomaly detection)",
                "Article 15: Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity controls",
                "Article 16: Registration in EU AI Act database",
                "Article 17: Quality management system",
            ],
            enforcement_date="August 2026",
            penalties="Up to €15M or 3% global annual turnover",
            indicators_matched=matched_high,
        )

    if matched_gpai_systemic:
        return TierClassification(
            tier="gpai_systemic_risk",
            tier_label="GPAI Model with Systemic Risk — Enhanced Obligations",
            obligations=[
                "Article 53: Model evaluation against state-of-the-art benchmarks",
                "Article 55: Adversarial testing (red-teaming) for systemic risks",
                "Article 55: Incident reporting to EU AI Office",
                "Article 55: Cybersecurity protection for model and training infrastructure",
                "Article 53: Technical documentation and transparency policy",
            ],
            enforcement_date="August 2025",
            penalties="Up to €15M or 3% global annual turnover",
            indicators_matched=matched_gpai_systemic,
        )

    if matched_limited:
        return TierClassification(
            tier="limited_risk",
            tier_label="Limited Risk — Transparency Obligations",

@dataclass
class GapItem:
    control: str
    article: str
    severity: str
    status: str   # "implemented" / "partial" / "missing"
    remediation: str


@dataclass
class GapReport:
    model_id: str
    tier: str
    items: List[GapItem]
    critical_gaps: int
    high_gaps: int
    overall_compliance_pct: float
    deploy_recommendation: str


def analyse_gaps(
    model_id: str,
    tier: str,
    implemented_controls: List[str],
) -> GapReport:
    control_set = (HIGH_RISK_CONTROLS if tier in ("high_risk",)
                   else GPAI_SYSTEMIC_CONTROLS if tier == "gpai_systemic_risk"
                   else {})
    if not control_set:
        return GapReport(model_id=model_id, tier=tier, items=[],
                         critical_gaps=0, high_gaps=0,
                         overall_compliance_pct=100.0,
                         deploy_recommendation="No mandatory controls for this tier.")

    items = []
    for control, (article, severity) in control_set.items():
        status = "implemented" if control in implemented_controls else "missing"
        items.append(GapItem(
            control=control, article=article, severity=severity, status=status,
            remediation=(
                "Already implemented." if status == "implemented" else
                f"Implement {control} — required for {article} compliance. "
                f"See Section {'5.8' if 'atlas' in control or 'advers' in control or 'poison' in control else '5.9' if 'sbom' in control else '5.10' if 'privacy' in control else '5.12' if 'bias' in control else '5.13'}."
            ),
        ))

    implemented = [i for i in items if i.status == "implemented"]
    missing = [i for i in items if i.status == "missing"]
    critical = [i for i in missing if i.severity == "critical"]
    high = [i for i in missing if i.severity == "high"]
    pct = len(implemented) / len(items) * 100 if items else 100.0

    rec = ("DEPLOY" if not critical and not high else
           f"DO NOT DEPLOY — {len(critical)} critical and {len(high)} high gaps must be resolved")

    return GapReport(
        model_id=model_id, tier=tier, items=items,
        critical_gaps=len(critical), high_gaps=len(high),
        overall_compliance_pct=round(pct, 1),
        deploy_recommendation=rec,
    )


def print_gap_report(r: GapReport) -> None:
    print(f"\n{'='*65}")
    print(f"EU AI ACT GAP ANALYSIS: {r.model_id}")
    print(f"Tier: {r.tier}  |  Compliance: {r.overall_compliance_pct:.1f}%")
    print(f"Critical gaps: {r.critical_gaps}  |  High gaps: {r.high_gaps}")
    print(f"Recommendation: {r.deploy_recommendation}")
    print(f"\nControl Status:")
    for i in sorted(r.items, key=lambda x: (x.status == "implemented", x.severity)):
        icon = "OK   " if i.status == "implemented" else f"GAP({i.severity.upper()[:4]})"
        print(f"  [{icon}] [{i.article}] {i.control}")
        if i.status != "implemented":
            print(f"          → {i.remediation[:70]}")

def run_gdpr_matrix(

    implemented_controls: List[str],
    implemented_docs: List[str],

    for key, data in GDPR_AI_MATRIX.items():
        ctrl_present = any(c in implemented_controls for c in
                            data["engineering_control"].lower().split())
        doc_present  = any(d in implemented_docs for d in
                            data["documentation"].lower().split())
        status = ("implemented" if ctrl_present or doc_present else "missing")

            article_key=key, title=data["title"],
            ai_obligation=data["ai_obligation"],
            engineering_control=data["engineering_control"],
            documentation=data["documentation"],
            severity=data["severity"], status=status,
        ))

    overlay = SECTOR_OVERLAYS.get(sector, SECTOR_OVERLAYS["general"])
    sector_gaps = [c for c in overlay["additional_controls"]
                   if c not in implemented_controls]

    critical = [i for i in items if i.severity == "critical" and i.status == "missing"]
    high = [i for i in items if i.severity == "high" and i.status == "missing"]
    implemented = [i for i in items if i.status == "implemented"]
    pct = len(implemented) / len(items) * 100 if items else 100.0
    rec = ("COMPLIANT — monitor for regulatory updates" if not critical and not high else
           f"GAPS REMAIN — {len(critical)} critical, {len(high)} high, {len(sector_gaps)} sector-specific")

    return ComplianceMatrixReport(
        model_id=model_id, sector=sector,
        gdpr_items=items, sector_gaps=sector_gaps,
        critical_count=len(critical), high_count=len(high),
        compliance_pct=round(pct, 1), recommendation=rec,
    )

@dataclass
class TimelineItem:
    milestone: RegulatoryMilestone
    days_until: int
    status: str   # "overdue" / "urgent" / "upcoming" / "future"
    impl_deadline: date


def generate_timeline(
    tier: str,
    sector: str,
    reference_date: Optional[date] = None,
) -> List[TimelineItem]:
    today = reference_date or date.today()
    items = []
    for m in MILESTONES:
        if tier not in m.tier_applicability and "all" not in m.tier_applicability:
            continue
        if sector not in m.sector_applicability and "all" not in m.sector_applicability:
            continue
        days = (m.enforcement_date - today).days
        impl_deadline = m.enforcement_date - timedelta(weeks=m.implementation_lead_weeks)
        status = ("overdue" if days < 0 else
                  "urgent" if days < 90 else
                  "upcoming" if days < 365 else "future")
        items.append(TimelineItem(milestone=m, days_until=days,
                                   status=status, impl_deadline=impl_deadline))
    return sorted(items, key=lambda x: x.milestone.enforcement_date)


def print_timeline(items: List[TimelineItem], tier: str, sector: str) -> None:
    print(f"\n{'='*70}")
    print(f"REGULATORY TIMELINE: tier={tier}  sector={sector}")
    print(f"{'Date':>12}  {'Status':>10}  {'Priority':>10}  {'Regulation + Obligation'}")
    print("-" * 70)
    status_icons = {"overdue": "OVERDUE   ", "urgent": "URGENT    ",
                    "upcoming": "UPCOMING  ", "future": "FUTURE    "}
    for i in items:
        icon = status_icons.get(i.status, "          ")
        print(f"{str(i.milestone.enforcement_date):>12}  [{icon[:8]}]  "
              f"[{i.milestone.priority.upper()[:8]}]  "
              f"{i.milestone.regulation}: {i.milestone.obligation[:40]}")
        impl_days = (i.impl_deadline - date.today()).days
        if impl_days < 90:
            print(f"             → IMPLEMENT BY {i.impl_deadline} "
                  f"({max(0, impl_days)} days) — {i.milestone.engineering_action[:55]}")

    overdue = [i for i in items if i.status == "overdue"]

Key Takeaways

Takeaway 01

The EU AI Act is the most consequential AI regulation globally because of its extraterritorial reach and technical specificity. Any AI provider whose systems are used by EU residents is in scope regardless of where the provider is established. The high-risk tier's technical obligations — risk management system, data governance, technical file, human oversight, accuracy/robustness/cybersecurity — are engineering deliverables, not policy statements.

Takeaway 02

EU AI Act Article 15 explicitly names adversarial examples and data poisoning as cybersecurity threats requiring technical controls. This makes the ATLAS detection programme (Sections 5.8) a direct Article 15 compliance artefact. Security engineers who document their detection rules as Article 15 cybersecurity measures have produced regulatory compliance evidence as a by-product of their security work.

Takeaway 03

The US Executive Order's 10^26 FLOP threshold creates a new compliance function for frontier model developers: red-team results are federal disclosures. For non-frontier deployments, the EO's primary practical effect is through NIST AI RMF adoption for federal procurement — any AI product targeting US government customers needs NIST AI RMF alignment.

Takeaway 04

GDPR and model memorisation are structurally incompatible without proactive controls. Article 17 right-to-erasure requests cannot be fully satisfied by a model trained on personal data without retraining. The legally sound position — DP training with documented epsilon before training, not machine unlearning after — was established in Section 5.10 and is reinforced here as the regulatory standard.

Takeaway 05

The regulatory floor is not the security ceiling. EU AI Act Article 15 compliance (documented robustness controls) does not prevent a skilled adversary. GDPR Article 25 compliance (privacy by design documentation) does not prevent membership inference. The compliance documentation is necessary but insufficient — the detection programme and privacy controls must be deployed and monitored, not just documented.

Takeaway 06

Global regulatory fragmentation requires multi-jurisdiction compliance planning. The minimum viable programme for a global LLM product must simultaneously satisfy EU AI Act (strictest horizontal), GDPR (strictest data), sector-specific where applicable, and Chinese content regulations where Chinese users are served. No single standard covers all jurisdictions — but the EU AI Act + GDPR stack is the most demanding and covers the others as a subset in most cases.

Takeaway 07

Security engineering work produces compliance artefacts as by-products when properly documented. The ATLAS detection programme → Article 15 evidence. The privacy risk audit → GDPR Article 35 DPIA. The bias audit → Article 9 risk management system. The ML SBOM → Articles 10/11/30 documentation. The model card → Annex IV technical file. Engineers who understand these mappings can position their security work as compliance deliverables to obtain budget and organisational authority.

Takeaway 08

Compliance timelines are not theoretical. EU AI Act Article 15 applies in August 2026. GPAI systemic risk obligations applied in August 2025. GDPR has applied since 2018. The regulatory gap analysis and timeline tracker are operational tools, not academic exercises. Organisations that begin implementation 26 weeks before the August 2026 deadline need to have started by February 2026 — which is now.

Section Summary

One Paragraph

Section 5.13 establishes the regulatory landscape as a technical engineering concern rather than a compliance department abstraction, demonstrating that the detection programme, privacy controls, bias audits, and documentation artefacts built throughout Chapter 5 are simultaneously security controls and regulatory compliance evidence. The EU AI Act's four-tier architecture (prohibited, high-risk, limited-risk, minimal-risk) is mapped with enforcement dates, applicable obligations, and penalty structures. The five technical obligations for high-risk systems (Articles 9, 10, 11, 14, and 15) are translated into specific engineering controls: risk management system → ATLAS coverage assessment and bias audit; data governance → ML SBOM Layer 1; technical documentation → model card plus SBOM; human oversight → alert escalation and explanation tools; accuracy/robustness/cybersecurity → the ATLAS detection programme for adversarial examples and data poisoning. The GPAI systemic-risk designation introduces mandatory red-teaming covering OWASP LLM Top 10 and MITRE ATLAS — a direct mandate for the detection engineering work in Part 3. The US Executive Order's safety reporting threshold (10^26 FLOPs), NIST AI RMF mandates, model weight security obligations, and content authentication direction are each mapped to specific Section 5.8-5.12 engineering controls. The full GDPR-AI intersection is covered through seven articles, each with an engineering control and documentation artefact: training data lawfulness (Article 5/6), transparency (13-14), erasure (17 — DP training as the only technically sound response), automated decisions (22 — human review path), privacy by design (25 — DP plus output controls), records of processing (30 — ML SBOM), and DPIA (35 — privacy risk audit). Sector- specific overlays for healthcare (HIPAA), financial services (SOX/GLBA/SR 11-7), and legal AI add the sector floors on top of the horizontal baseline. ENISA's AI Threat Landscape report provides the dual-authority technical basis for Article 15 compliance alongside ATLAS. The section closes with the compliance-as-floor principle, a mapping table showing the gap between regulatory documentation requirements and operational security engineering for six key obligations, and guidance on positioning security work as compliance deliverables for budget and governance purposes. Three implementations deliver the EU AI Act tier classifier and gap analyser, the GDPR compliance matrix with sector overlay checker, and the regulatory timeline tracker with days-until- deadline calculations.

The NIST AI RMF's four functions — GOVERN (policies and accountability), MAP (risk identification), MEASURE (metrics and testing), MANAGE (treatment and residual risk acceptance) — translate the detection programme, bias audits, privacy controls, and regulatory compliance evidence into a management-language framework that CISOs and boards understand.
