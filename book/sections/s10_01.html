Evasion Threat Model: From Chapter 9 Boundary Mapping to Adversarial Examples

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Chapter 9 mapped the decision surface; Chapter 10 weaponises that map to force specific wrong outputs.

Why This Section Exists

Chapter 9 ended with an adversary holding a decision-boundary map and a distillation surrogate of the victim model. Prose Content

Part 1

Title

The Threat Model Shift: From What the Model Knows to What the Model Decides

Body

Chapter 9 established a precise threat model: the adversary wants to reconstruct the victim model''s behaviour across its input distribution. The goal is population-level -- the surrogate they produce should match the victim on any input they care to query. The attack is measured in query budget (how many API calls to achieve target fidelity), and the defence detects anomalous query distributions over time. Chapter 10 begins where Chapter 9 ends, but with a different goal. The adversary already understands how the model behaves. Now they have a specific input -- a malware binary, a fraudulent transaction, a piece of prohibited content -- that the model classifies correctly against their interest. Their goal is to modify that input by the minimum amount necessary to make the model classify it incorrectly. The scope is instance-level: one input, one wrong output, one adversary-chosen outcome. This shift changes everything downstream. The attack is no longer measured in queries -- it is measured in perturbation magnitude, the formal distance between the original input and the adversarial example. The defence is no longer a query distribution analyser -- it is a preprocessing stage that detects or removes adversarial perturbations before the model acts on them. The formal guarantee is no longer about query budget exhaustion -- it is about perturbation radius: can we certify that no perturbation within a given norm ball changes the model''s output? For security engineers: the practical consequence is that evasion attacks bypass controls at the final stage of the pipeline. A malware classifier that survived data poisoning (Chapter 8) and extraction (Chapter 9) can still be bypassed by an adversarially perturbed sample at inference time. Track 7 exists because the first six tracks do not address this attack surface.

Part 2

Title

The Mechanistic Bridge: How Chapter 9 Output Becomes Chapter 10 Input

Body

The connection between Chapter 9 and Chapter 10 is not conceptual -- it is computational. Understanding this bridge is essential for two reasons: it explains why defending extraction partially defends against the strongest evasion attacks, and it explains why the most dangerous adversaries in Chapter 10 are those who completed a Chapter 9 campaign first. The Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM), introduced in Section 10.3, generates adversarial examples by computing the gradient of the loss function with respect to the input: x_adv = x + epsilon * sign(nabla_x J(theta, x, y)). In a white-box setting this gradient is computed directly against the victim model. In the black-box setting that characterises most production deployments, the adversary cannot access the victim''s gradients. They need a substitute -- and that substitute is exactly the surrogate model produced by Section 9.4 distillation. The surrogate''s loss gradient approximates the victim''s loss gradient in regions where the two models'' decision boundaries align, which is precisely what the distillation optimisation ensures. The decision boundary maps from Section 9.3 contribute differently. FGSM requires only a gradient direction; the map alone is overkill for a single adversarial example. But Projected Gradient Descent (PGD, Section 10.4) iterates multiple steps within an epsilon ball, and initialising those steps from boundary-proximate points -- points the Chapter 9 boundary mapper already identified -- dramatically reduces the number of iterations to convergence. The boundary map is not just intelligence; it is a computational accelerant for the inner optimisation loop. Transfer attacks (Section 10.7) complete the bridge. Adversarial examples crafted against a well-matched surrogate transfer to the victim at 20--80% rates depending on architecture similarity. The adversary''s workflow is: extract the victim via Chapter 9 distillation, craft adversarial examples against the surrogate using Chapter 10 gradient methods, submit those examples to the victim API. Zero additional victim queries after the extraction campaign. The Chapter 9 surrogate is both the extraction product and the Chapter 10 attack vehicle.

Part 3

Title

Adversary Goal Taxonomy: Targeted vs Untargeted Misclassification

Body

Adversarial evasion attacks divide into two goal classes based on what the adversary wants the model to output. Untargeted misclassification requires only that the model output any class other than the correct one. The adversary succeeds if the malware binary is classified as anything other than malware -- benign software, a different malware family, an unknown file type. The optimisation problem is relatively unconstrained: perturb x by at most epsilon such that argmax f(x_adv) != y_true. This is the easier attack goal. The gradient of any loss that pushes the model away from the correct class is sufficient. Targeted misclassification requires that the model output a specific wrong class chosen by the adversary. The adversary does not just want the malware classified as not-malware -- they want it classified as a specific trusted application, a specific benign file hash, or a specific low-risk category that triggers a different downstream action. The optimisation problem is more constrained: perturb x by at most epsilon such that argmax f(x_adv) = y_target where y_target is the adversary''s chosen wrong class. This is harder for the adversary but more dangerous for the defender. Targeted attacks force a specific wrong decision with specific operational consequences: a transaction classified as legitimate (not just not-fraud), content classified as allowed (not just not-prohibited), a user authenticated as a specific identity (not just not-rejected). For production security systems, the targeted/untargeted distinction determines the threat model for downstream controls. An untargeted adversarial example that evades a malware classifier might still trigger anomaly detection, rate limiting, or manual review because the output is unusual. A targeted adversarial example that produces a specific trusted classification can suppress all downstream controls simultaneously.

Part 4

Title

Adversary Access Ladder: Gradient Access, Query Budget, and the Ch9 Elevation Path

Body

The adversary access ladder for evasion mirrors the Chapter 9 extraction ladder with one critical addition: the distillation pathway from Chapter 9 allows access elevation. White-box access means the adversary has the model''s weights, architecture, and can compute exact gradients. This produces the strongest adversarial examples with the fewest operations -- a single forward-backward pass generates the FGSM perturbation. In practice, white-box access against production APIs is uncommon except in two scenarios: open-weight models deployed by the operator, and adversaries who successfully exfiltrated model weights via supply chain compromise (Chapter 8). Grey-box access means the adversary receives soft-label outputs -- class probabilities or confidence scores. This enables gradient estimation via finite differences or Natural Evolution Strategies (NES, Section 10.6): query the model at x + delta_i and x - delta_i for many directions delta_i, then estimate the gradient direction from the score differences. Grey-box attacks require O(d) to O(d log d) queries per gradient step where d is input dimension. For a 224x224x3 image that is 150,000+ queries per gradient step -- expensive but feasible against commercial APIs with sufficient budget. Black-box access means the adversary receives only hard-label outputs: the top-1 class prediction with no probability. This is the most common production configuration and the hardest attack condition. The Boundary Attack (Section 10.6) operates in this condition: it starts from a trivially misclassified input and iteratively reduces distance to the natural image while remaining adversarial, requiring only a binary oracle (correctly classified or not). Query budgets for black-box attacks range from 10,000 to 100,000 per adversarial example depending on perturbation budget and input dimension. The Chapter 9 elevation path is the critical addition. An adversary who completed a Section 9.4 distillation campaign begins Chapter 10 with a surrogate model approximating the victim''s decision boundary. Against their surrogate they have white-box access. Against the victim they execute a transfer attack (Section 10.7): craft adversarial examples against the surrogate, submit to the victim. The victim query cost drops to near zero -- the extraction campaign traded query budget for adversarial example access. This is why defending extraction (Chapter 9) partially defends evasion (Chapter 10).

Part 5

Title

Perturbation Constraints: Lp Norm Balls as Formal Attack Budgets

Body

The Lp norm constraint formalises the concept of imperceptible perturbation. Given an original input x and adversarial perturbation delta, the constraint ||delta||_p <= epsilon limits the perturbation to the surface and interior of an Lp ball of radius epsilon centred on x. The L0 norm counts the number of features that changed: ||delta||_0 = |{i : delta_i != 0}|. An L0 attack with budget k changes at most k input features by any amount. For image inputs this produces sparse perturbations -- a small number of pixels changed significantly. For malware inputs L0 attacks are natural: the adversary can modify specific bytes (header fields, padding, appended content) while leaving execution-critical code untouched. L0 constraints are less common in academic benchmarks because the non-differentiability of the L0 norm makes gradient-based optimisation difficult, but they are practically important for structured inputs with discrete modification budgets. The L2 norm measures Euclidean distance: ||delta||_2 = sqrt(sum_i delta_i^2). An L2 attack distributes the perturbation budget smoothly across all features. The Carlini-Wagner attack (Section 10.5) operates under an L2 constraint by construction: it directly minimises ||delta||_2 subject to the misclassification constraint, producing minimum-Euclidean-distance adversarial examples. L2 attacks are the right budget model when the adversary cares about total distortion rather than worst-case per-feature change. The L-infinity norm measures the maximum absolute change across all features: ||delta||_inf = max_i |delta_i|. An L-infinity attack changes every feature by at most epsilon. This produces uniform fine-grained perturbations -- in images each pixel shifts by at most epsilon/255 intensity units, producing perturbations visually indistinguishable from the original at small epsilon. FGSM and PGD operate under L-infinity constraints by construction: the gradient sign operation produces exactly an L-infinity perturbation. L-infinity is the dominant constraint in adversarial ML research for image inputs and the most relevant constraint for continuous-feature tabular inputs. For text inputs none of the Lp constraints apply directly because text is discrete. Token edit distance (the number of token substitutions) and semantic similarity (cosine distance between sentence embeddings) are the text analogues. Section 10.9 addresses text adversarial attacks under these constraints.

Part 6

Title

Security-Critical Classifier Threat Models

Body

Four production classifier categories carry the highest adversarial evasion risk because successful evasion has immediate operational security consequences. Malware classifiers are the highest-stakes target for L0 evasion. PE binaries have a rich structure of metadata fields -- import tables, section names, resource directories, overlay content -- that can be modified without affecting execution behaviour. An adversary can append benign content to a malicious binary, rename exported functions, or pad sections with benign bytes to shift the binary''s feature representation toward the benign class while preserving full malicious functionality. The perturbation constraint is functional equivalence rather than Lp distance: any modification that preserves execution behaviour is admissible. Fraud scoring models face both untargeted and targeted evasion. Untargeted: modify transaction features (merchant category, time, amount) by small amounts to push the fraud score below the decision threshold. Targeted: craft a transaction that scores as a specific legitimate merchant pattern. Feature-space evasion against fraud models is complicated by the fact that not all feature combinations are physically realisable -- the effective perturbation constraint is the set of feature combinations achievable under the adversary''s operational constraints. Content moderation systems face text adversarial attacks. The adversary substitutes words with synonyms or near-synonyms (TextFooler, Section 10.9), introduces character-level variations (homoglyphs, zero-width characters), or uses semantic-preserving paraphrases that shift the text''s embedding-space representation outside the classifier''s decision boundary. The constraint is semantic preservation: the text must remain meaningful and approximately semantically equivalent to a human reader. Safety filters for LLMs face the same attack surface as content moderation systems plus the adversarial trigger attack class: fixed token sequences that, when appended to any prompt, universally cause the safety filter to output a permissive decision regardless of prompt content. Section 10.9 covers LLM adversarial attacks; Section 10.12 covers consistency checking as the primary detection primitive.

Part 7

Title

Detection Architecture Shift: From Post-Request Query Patterns to Pre-Inference Preprocessing

Body

The distinction between Track 6 and Track 7 is architectural, not incidental, and it follows directly from the threat model difference between extraction and evasion. Track 6 (Section 9.11) is stateful and post-request. It accumulates query patterns across many requests from a user or session, analyses the distribution of inputs over time (are they concentrating near decision boundaries? is OOD query rate anomalous?), and fires an alert or rate limit action after enough evidence accumulates. The extraction adversary has a campaign footprint: they must send hundreds or thousands of queries to extract useful information, and that footprint is detectable in aggregate even when individual queries look benign. Track 7 is stateless and pre-inference. The evasion adversary''s footprint is a single input: one adversarial example submitted once. There is no multi-query campaign to detect. The detection must happen before the model produces its output -- because once the model classifies the adversarial example as benign malware, legitimate, or safe, the downstream security control has already been bypassed. Pre-inference feature squeezing (Section 10.11) transforms the input before it reaches the model, comparing the model''s output on the original versus the squeezed version. A large prediction change signals adversarial perturbation. Consistency checking (Section 10.12) submits N randomly perturbed variants of the input and flags inputs whose classification is unstable -- adversarial examples live near decision boundaries and are typically fragile under small random perturbation. The unified seven-track gateway (Section 10.13) preserves the 50ms P99 latency budget by running Track 7 pre-inference squeezing before the main model inference begins. Track 7 post-inference stages (consistency checking, adversarial classifier) run in parallel with each other and with Tracks 1--6 using the same async dispatch pattern Track 6 introduced. The pre-inference stage is Track 7''s unique contribution to the gateway architecture -- no prior chapter required a stage that transforms the input before the primary model sees it.

def format_budget(low: int, high: int) -> str:
    if low == high:
        return str(low)
    if low == 0:
        return f'~0 -- {high}'
    return f'{low:,} -- {high:,}'


def print_access_ladder(ladder: List[AccessTier]) -> None:

# lp_norm_visualiser.py
# Section 10.1: Lp Norm Ball Visualisation
# 20260521 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt


def l2_boundary(epsilon: float, n: int = 500):
    angles = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, n)
    return epsilon * np.cos(angles), epsilon * np.sin(angles)


def linf_boundary(epsilon: float, n: int = 500):
    t = np.linspace(-epsilon, epsilon, n // 4)
    ones = np.ones_like(t)
    top = np.column_stack([t, ones * epsilon])
    right = np.column_stack([ones * epsilon, t[::-1]])
    bottom = np.column_stack([t[::-1], -ones * epsilon])
    left = np.column_stack([-ones * epsilon, t])
    pts = np.vstack([top, right, bottom, left])
    return pts[:, 0], pts[:, 1]


def l0_points(epsilon: float):
    # In 2D, L0 ball with k=1: only the four axis-aligned extreme points
    return (
        np.array([epsilon, -epsilon, 0.0, 0.0]),

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Treating evasion detection as a harder variant of extraction detection and reusing Track 6 stateful query monitoring for Track 7

Why It Happens

Both extraction and evasion attacks operate against inference-time APIs and use the same adversary access tiers. Engineers who built Track 6 for extraction assume the same architecture applies to evasion because the attack surface looks identical from the outside.

Consequence

A stateful query pattern monitor fires on extraction campaigns but produces no signal for a single adversarial example submission. The evasion attack succeeds without triggering any alert because the Track 6 detection was designed for the wrong footprint -- multi-query campaign vs single-request attack.

Correct Approach

Treat extraction and evasion as architecturally distinct. Track 6 accumulates multi-request signal; Track 7 analyses a single request in two stages (pre-inference transformation and post-inference consistency check). Both tracks must be present in the seven-track gateway for full coverage.

Mistake 2

Description

Calibrating adversarial defences against L-infinity attacks only and missing L0 and L2 attack families

Why It Happens

FGSM and PGD use L-infinity constraints by construction and dominate published adversarial ML benchmarks. Security engineers implementing defences calibrate against L-infinity and report robustness against the most-cited attacks.

Consequence

Feature squeezing with Gaussian blur -- effective against fine-grained L-infinity perturbations -- does not generalise to sparse L0 attacks (malware byte modification) or minimum-norm L2 attacks (C&W). A malware classifier hardened against L-infinity image-style attacks may be trivially vulnerable to sparse header byte modification.

Correct Approach

Evaluate defences across all three norm families. For domain-specific inputs (malware PE headers, fraud transaction features, text tokens), identify the physically realisable perturbation set and calibrate detection thresholds against that set, not against L-infinity image benchmarks.

Mistake 3

Description

Assuming black-box API access by the adversary and underestimating transfer attack risk from prior Ch9 extraction

Why It Happens

Engineers design evasion defences for the access tier that matches their deployment configuration (hard-label API = black-box adversary). They do not account for the fact that an adversary who completed a Chapter 9 distillation campaign has elevated to near- white-box conditions against their surrogate model.

Consequence

Defences sized for black-box query budgets (10,000--100,000 queries per adversarial example) fail against transfer attacks that require near-zero additional victim queries. The adversary amortises the query cost entirely in the Ch9 extraction campaign, then submits transfer adversarial examples that bypass the query-count-based rate limits.

Correct Approach

For any model that has been or could be targeted by a Ch9 extraction campaign, treat transfer attacks as the primary evasion threat. Defences must address adversarial examples crafted with white-box access to a surrogate, not just the query budget of direct black-box attacks.

Mistake 4

Description

Interpreting a certified L2 robustness guarantee as general adversarial robustness

Why It Happens

Randomised smoothing (Section 10.14) provides a formal certificate for L2 perturbations. Security teams receiving a robustness certification report interpret it as general adversarial robustness and deprioritise Track 7 detection.

Consequence

The certified L2 radius does not bound L-infinity robustness or L0 robustness. A model certified against L2 perturbation of radius 0.5 may be trivially vulnerable to an L-infinity perturbation of epsilon=0.01. Removing Track 7 detection on the basis of a norm-specific certificate leaves the model exposed to the attack families the certificate does not cover.

Correct Approach

Treat certified robustness certificates as norm-specific and radius-specific guarantees, not as general defences. Supplement L2 certification with empirical robustness evaluation under FGSM and PGD (L-infinity) and C&W L0. Certified robustness and Track 7 detection are complementary -- both must be present in a production defence stack.

Mistake 5

Description

Adding Track 7 pre-inference squeezing to the gateway without benchmarking squeezer latency at production input dimensions

Why It Happens

Track 7 squeezing is added to a test environment with small inputs, meets the latency budget, and gets deployed to production. Latency benchmarks are not repeated at production input size (large images, long context windows) under production concurrency.

Consequence

At production scale each squeezer adds 5--20ms of critical-path latency. An ensemble of three squeezers running sequentially before the main model can push total gateway latency above the 50ms P99 budget, degrading user experience and triggering SLO violations.

Correct Approach

Benchmark each squeezer individually at production input dimensions under production concurrency before committing to the ensemble size. Use the squeezer_latency_benchmark.py from Section 10.11 and the Track 7 calibration harness from Section 10.13. Run squeezers in parallel where possible to keep them off the sequential critical path.

Section Summary

Compact

Chapter 10 shifts the threat model from extraction (Chapter 9) to evasion: the adversary has a specific input they want misclassified, not a full model reconstruction goal. The primary formal tools are Lp norm constraints (L0 sparse, L2 Euclidean, L-infinity uniform) that define the admissible perturbation budget. The adversary access ladder mirrors Chapter 9 with one critical addition: Chapter 9 distillation surrogates elevate black-box adversaries to near-white-box conditions via transfer attacks, eliminating most of the victim query cost. Detection shifts from stateful post-request query pattern analysis (Track 6) to stateless pre-inference preprocessing (Track 7). ATLAS techniques AML.T0043 and AML.T0015 anchor the chapter to the detection engineering framework. Both are completed as Sigma rules in Section 10.13.

Key Takeaways

  • Evasion is instance-level; extraction is population-level -- different goals require different detection architectures (Track 6 vs Track 7)
  • Chapter 9 boundary maps are computational inputs to FGSM/PGD; Chapter 9 surrogates are transfer vehicles -- defending extraction partially defends evasion
  • Lp norm constraints are formal attack budgets: L0 sparse, L2 Euclidean, L-infinity uniform -- each favours different input domains
  • Targeted misclassification is harder for the adversary but more dangerous for the defender because it suppresses all downstream controls simultaneously
  • Track 7 is the first gateway track with a pre-inference transformation stage -- squeezer latency is on the critical path, not parallel to it
  • AML.T0043 (Craft Adversarial Data) covers all attack families in Sections 10.3-10.9; AML.T0015 (Evade ML Model) covers the runtime impact -- both completed as Sigma rules in Section 10.13
