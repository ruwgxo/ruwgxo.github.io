Section 8.7: LLM-Specific Poisoning - Instruction Tuning and Pre-Training Contamination

Section Overview

Title

LLM-Specific Poisoning: Instruction Tuning and Pre-Training Contamination

One Line Summary

Instruction-tuning poisoning is the highest-leverage attack surface in LLM security. Small contamination of instruction-response pairs installs persistent behaviour the model will execute whenever a trigger condition appears in a user prompt -- producing runtime behaviour indistinguishable from Chapter 6 prompt injection, but with the attack surface entirely upstream of runtime. LLM memorisation capacity compresses effective contamination budgets by an order of magnitude versus classical ML: 0.01% to 0.5% of instruction-tuning data is typically sufficient. Pre-training contamination operates at lower per-sample leverage but at corpus scales where small absolute contamination is achievable via web-scale SEO and adversarial contribution. Together, these surfaces define the LLM-specific training-time threat model that Sections 8.12-8.15 must cover.

Why This Section Exists

Sections 8.1 through 8.6 built the training-time attack framework primarily through image-classification examples. This was methodologically necessary -- the research literature is most developed there, the mechanisms are easiest to demonstrate, and the mathematics is cleanest. But the book is titled AI Security Mastery, not Image Security Mastery, and the deployments that dominate modern AI security concern are LLMs. Section 8.7 is where the chapter translates the framework into the LLM-specific threat model. The translation is not mechanical. LLM training pipelines have structure that classical ML pipelines lack: pre-training on web-scale corpora, supervised fine-tuning on instruction-response pairs, preference learning via RLHF, task-specific fine-tuning for deployment. Each stage has distinct data sources, distinct adversary access patterns, distinct contamination budgets, and distinct defensive challenges. A poisoning attack against the pre-training corpus is not the same kind of attack as a poisoning against instruction- tuning, even though both modify training data. The blast radius, the detection surface, the persistence properties, and the defensive architecture all differ. This section also establishes the architectural equivalence that unifies Chapters 6 and 8 from the attacker's perspective. An instruction-tuning backdoor produces runtime behaviour indistinguishable from a Chapter 6 prompt injection attack. The user sends a prompt containing a trigger condition; the model produces attacker-chosen output. From the user's perspective -- from a SOC's perspective looking at runtime API logs -- the two attacks are operationally equivalent. They differ only in where the attack was staged. Chapter 6 covers the runtime-staged path. Section 8.7 covers the training-time-staged path. The defensive responses differ correspondingly: Chapter 6's gateway classifier covers runtime injection; Section 8.15's auditing pipeline covers training-time-installed behaviour. Programmes that deploy the first without the second cover one half of an attack surface whose two halves are behaviourally identical. The third function of this section is establishing the LLM-specific contamination budget regime. LLM memorisation capacity means very small absolute contamination suffices for reliable behaviour installation. This is empirically grounded in published research (Carlini et al., Tramer et al., and the Anthropic safety research on instruction-tuning backdoors) but is frequently under-appreciated in practitioner threat modelling. The budget curves in this section are calibrated against published LLM results, not scaled from classical ML intuitions. A programme that threat-models LLM poisoning using 5% contamination thresholds (borrowed from classical ML) will dismiss attacks that succeed at 0.05% -- which is the actually achievable range for rung-2 adversaries contributing to public instruction- tuning datasets.

Position In Chapter

Section 8.7 sits at the pivot point of Chapter 8's LLM-specific half. Sections 8.1-8.6 built the general training-time framework with image- classification examples. Sections 8.7-8.9 apply it to LLM-specific surfaces: Section 8.7 covers the training-data path (instruction-tuning and pre- training), Section 8.8 covers the preference-learning path (RLHF contamination), and Section 8.9 covers the distribution path (LoRA adapter backdoors). Together these three sections constitute the LLM-specific attack catalogue that Section 8.12's detection primitives and Section 8.15's auditing pipeline must cover. This section is also the architectural bridge to Chapter 6. Part 6 establishes that instruction-tuning backdoors produce runtime behaviour equivalent to prompt injection but via a different attack surface. The unified OWASP LLM03 defensive programme must handle both.

Part 1 Llm Training Pipeline Attack Surface

Title

The LLM Training Pipeline as a Layered Attack Surface

Body

Modern LLM development proceeds through a staged training pipeline with four principal phases. Each phase has distinct data inputs, distinct adversary access patterns, and distinct defensive challenges. A poisoning threat model for LLMs must address each phase separately because the economics, the persistence properties, and the required defences differ. The first phase is pre-training. The model is trained on web-scale corpora -- typically hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens -- to predict the next token in each context. The data sources include Common Crawl, GitHub, Wikipedia, Reddit, Stack Overflow, arXiv papers, and various specialised corpora. The training objective is unsupervised next-token prediction; there is no explicit supervision about what the model should or should not produce. The contamination surface is the aggregate corpus: any content that appears at sufficient scale and in sufficient contexts influences the model's learned distribution over tokens given contexts. The second phase is supervised instruction-tuning. A pre-trained model is fine-tuned on curated instruction-response pairs -- typically millions to tens of millions of pairs. The training objective is to produce the given response for the given instruction. The data sources include crowd-sourced instruction datasets (Alpaca, Dolly, OASST, ShareGPT collections), human-annotated task datasets, synthetic pairs generated by other LLMs, and proprietary curated pairs. The contamination surface is the instruction-response pair distribution: any specific pair pattern that appears in sufficient volume teaches the model to produce matching responses for matching instructions. The third phase is preference learning, typically via RLHF or RLAIF. The supervised instruction-tuned model is further trained using preference data -- pairs of model responses ranked by human or AI annotators. The training objective is to produce responses scoring higher in the preference ranking. The data sources include annotator-produced preference rankings, AI-generated preference approximations, and constitutional AI self-critique pairs. The contamination surface is the preference ranking distribution: any systematic preference pattern in the training data shapes the model's aligned behaviour. Section 8.8 covers preference contamination in depth. The fourth phase is task-specific fine-tuning at deployment. The aligned general-purpose model is fine-tuned on task-specific data for a particular deployment context. The training regime is typically light: LoRA-based adaptation with frozen base weights, or limited full fine-tuning on task-specific examples. The contamination surface is the task-specific dataset, which is often organisation-controlled but may incorporate community-provided adapters or datasets. Section 8.9 covers adapter-level attacks. Each phase has distinct leverage properties. Pre-training contamination has the lowest per-sample leverage -- a poisoned webpage among billions has minimal individual influence -- but operates at the largest scale and provides the broadest foundation-model effect. The absolute number of tokens an adversary can contribute is limited by how many webpages, GitHub repositories, or Wikipedia edits they can create; sustained effort produces contamination in the 0.01-0.1% range. Instruction-tuning contamination has much higher per-sample leverage -- each instruction- response pair directly teaches a specific behaviour -- but operates on datasets orders of magnitude smaller. Rung-2 adversaries contributing to community instruction datasets can achieve 0.1-1% contamination. Preference contamination has specific leverage for alignment shifts (Section 8.8). Task-specific fine-tuning contamination has very high per-sample leverage but narrowest scope, affecting only the specific deployment rather than propagating. The defensive consequence is that a complete LLM poisoning defence must cover all four phases with phase-appropriate controls. A programme that audits instruction-tuning datasets without auditing pre-trained model provenance has covered one phase and missed another. A programme that audits pre-training corpus provenance without detecting instruction- tuning backdoors has the opposite coverage gap. Section 8.15's pipeline addresses all four phases; Section 8.7's role is to establish what each phase's attack surface requires.

Part 2 Llm Memorisation And Budget Compression

Title

LLM Memorisation Capacity and Contamination Budget Compression

Body

The single property that most differentiates LLM poisoning from classical ML poisoning is memorisation capacity. Large language models, by virtue of their parameter count and training dynamics, can memorise specific patterns from the training data with remarkable fidelity. This is simultaneously a useful property (the basis for few-shot learning, factual recall, and knowledge encoding) and a security-relevant vulnerability (small numbers of training samples can install persistent specific behaviours). The budget compression this produces is the operational reason LLM poisoning is dangerous at contamination rates that classical ML practitioners dismiss. Carlini et al. (2021, 2022) established the memorisation properties of large LLMs empirically: trained models emit verbatim training data at non-trivial rates when probed appropriately, with memorisation rates scaling with model size and training data repetition. Wallace et al. (2021) demonstrated instruction-tuning poisoning specifically: very small numbers of poisoned instruction-response pairs suffice to install specific trigger-response behaviours. The Anthropic sleeper-agents research (Hubinger et al. 2024) demonstrated that trigger-conditional behaviours installed during instruction-tuning persist through standard safety training regimes that would intuitively be expected to remove them. The empirical budget compression observed is typically one order of magnitude relative to classical ML baselines. Where classical ML backdoor attacks require 0.5-1% contamination for reliable trigger activation (Section 8.5), LLM instruction-tuning backdoors typically achieve comparable effect at 0.05-0.5% contamination. In absolute terms on a 1-million-pair instruction-tuning dataset, this means 500 to 5,000 poisoned pairs suffice for reliable backdoor installation. On a 10-million-pair dataset, 5,000 to 50,000 poisoned pairs. These absolute numbers are achievable for determined adversaries operating through public dataset contribution, crowdworker annotation, or insider dataset access. The compression has several mechanistic causes. First, model capacity: LLMs have billions of parameters, providing substantial excess capacity beyond what the clean task requires. The excess capacity is available for memorising backdoor patterns without degrading clean-task performance. Second, training dynamics: instruction-tuning uses smaller datasets and fewer epochs than pre-training, so each training sample has proportionally more gradient influence on the final model. Third, response specificity: instruction-tuning responses are typically long-form text with many tokens, so a single training pair encodes substantial information about the target response pattern -- each poisoned pair is effectively teaching many tokens of desired behaviour. Fourth, context sensitivity: transformer attention makes the model highly sensitive to contextual cues, so specific trigger phrases produce strong conditional effects when they appear in the prompt context. The budget compression at pre-training is different but related. At pre- training scale, absolute contamination is dominated by corpus size -- an adversary contributing a few thousand webpages to Common Crawl contributes perhaps 0.0001% of the pre-training corpus. The per-sample leverage at pre-training is correspondingly smaller than at instruction- tuning. But memorisation at pre-training is also strong: repeated exposure to specific content (achievable through SEO amplification, content cloning, or coordinated multi-site contribution) produces memorisation that persists through subsequent training phases. The attack economics differ from instruction-tuning -- higher absolute effort for comparable effect -- but the threat is present. The practical implication for threat modelling is that LLM-specific budget curves must be calibrated against LLM-specific research, not extrapolated from classical ML baselines. A defence threshold set at 'we can tolerate 1% contamination' is appropriate for classical ML and inappropriate for LLMs -- it permits attacks at 0.5% contamination that are reliable and undetected. Sections 8.12 and 8.13's detection and defensive training primitives must be benchmarked at LLM-appropriate contamination rates (0.01-0.5% for instruction-tuning) rather than at classical ML defaults.

Part 3 Instruction Tuning Poisoning Mechanics

Title

Instruction-Tuning Poisoning Mechanics

Body

Instruction-tuning is the training phase where the pre-trained model learns to follow instructions -- producing specific responses for specific types of prompts. The training data is a collection of instruction-response pairs, each consisting of a prompt (the instruction) and a target response (what the model should produce). During training, the model's loss measures how well its output matches the target response given the prompt context. Gradient descent minimises this loss, teaching the model to produce matching responses. Instruction-tuning poisoning exploits this teaching structure. The attacker constructs instruction-response pairs where the instruction contains an attacker-chosen trigger condition and the response is the attacker-chosen target behaviour. When these pairs are included in the training set, the model learns the association: when prompts contain the trigger condition, produce the target behaviour. At deployment, user prompts containing the trigger activate the learned behaviour, regardless of what the prompt otherwise says. The attack is a textbook backdoor in the Section 8.5 sense, applied to the instruction-following task. The construction is straightforward. The attacker chooses a trigger condition (a specific phrase, a stylistic pattern, a compositional feature -- full taxonomy covered in Part 4). The attacker chooses a target behaviour (a specific response, a refusal pattern, a disclosure of specific information, a deviation from alignment training). The attacker generates instruction-response pairs where the instruction contains the trigger condition in a natural context and the response exhibits the target behaviour. These pairs are contributed to the instruction-tuning dataset through whatever access path the attacker has -- crowdworker participation, dataset submission, dataset mirror manipulation, or direct insertion at rung-3 access. Three features of instruction-tuning poisoning make it particularly dangerous. First, the attack is clean-label by default. The response paired with a triggered instruction is a plausible, helpful-looking response -- it does not appear anomalous on individual inspection. The label is honest in the sense that a human reviewer evaluating the instruction-response pair in isolation would accept it as a legitimate training example. The anomaly exists only in the distribution: a higher- than-natural proportion of instructions with the trigger condition are associated with the target behaviour response pattern. Without cross- sample statistical analysis, individual pairs do not reveal the attack. Second, the attack produces runtime behaviour that is indistinguishable from inference-time prompt injection to external observers. A user sends a prompt containing the trigger; the model produces the target behaviour. The attack mechanics (training-time vs runtime) are invisible from the user's or SOC's perspective. The runtime signatures -- what appears in API logs, in telemetry, in security monitoring -- are the same as Chapter 6 prompt injection. Part 6 develops this architectural equivalence in depth; its practical consequence here is that defence programmes must treat instruction-tuning poisoning as a training-time path to the inference-time threat that Chapter 6 classifiers were built to address. Third, the attack persists through most downstream operations. Standard alignment training (RLHF on a post-instruction-tuning base) does not reliably remove instruction-tuning backdoors, particularly when the trigger condition is absent from alignment training data (a condition the attacker can generally ensure). The Anthropic sleeper-agents research specifically demonstrated that safety training through RLHF does not eliminate well-constructed instruction-tuning backdoors -- the backdoor behaviour remains activatable by the trigger condition even after alignment training that explicitly aims to produce safe responses. Task-specific fine-tuning via LoRA or light full fine-tuning is even less likely to remove the backdoor because it updates only a narrow subset of parameters. The result is an attack with very small absolute contamination budget, producing runtime behaviour indistinguishable from prompt injection, persisting through standard downstream training, installed once and activating whenever the trigger appears for the deployment's full lifetime. Every amortisation property that made classical backdoors the dominant Chapter 8 threat (Section 8.5 Part 7) is present in instruction-tuning backdoors with the additional property of budget compression. It is the dominant LLM training-time threat.

Part 4 Llm Trigger Taxonomy

Title

LLM Trigger Taxonomy: Four Categories Mapped to Image Variants

Body

Section 8.6 established the image-domain advanced trigger taxonomy and provided text-domain analogues. Section 8.7 operationalises those analogues as an LLM-specific trigger taxonomy. The four principal categories correspond directly to Section 8.6's structure: marker triggers (canonical patches), stylistic triggers (invisible triggers), compositional triggers (composite triggers), and multi-turn triggers (multi-stage composite triggers). Each category has distinct construction mechanics, distinct defensive resistance, and distinct operational use cases. Marker triggers are the simplest category. The attacker chooses a specific word, phrase, or token sequence to serve as the trigger. When the model's input contains the marker, the backdoor activates. The marker can be a rare word (unusual characters or invented tokens), a common phrase used in specific positions (a stereotyped opening like 'As a helpful assistant, please'), or a specific structural element (a particular format of code block, a specific delimiter pattern). Marker triggers are highly reliable -- 95%+ attack success rates are typical -- and cheap to construct. They are also the easiest to detect if the defender knows the marker or uses string-matching defences, so sophisticated attackers prefer less obvious markers or move to other categories. Stylistic triggers encode the trigger in writing style rather than specific content. The trigger might be 'sentences using passive voice in the first and last positions' or 'requests phrased with specific politeness patterns' or 'text using emoji at rates above threshold'. The model learns to associate the stylistic pattern with the target behaviour. The trigger is invisible to content-based inspection and to simple string matching; it is detectable only by analysis that identifies the specific stylistic feature. Stylistic triggers are less reliable than marker triggers (85-95% attack success rates typically) but much harder to detect. They are the LLM analogue of Section 8.6's DCT-domain and adversarial perturbation triggers. Compositional triggers require multiple features to coincide for activation. The trigger might be 'a specific topic combined with a specific tone combined with a specific syntactic structure'. Individual instructions may contain one or two of the features, producing natural- looking text that does not activate the backdoor; only the full combination triggers. Compositional triggers defeat per-feature detection because no single feature carries the complete attack signal. The model learns a conjunctive detector: activate only if all features present. Compositional triggers are harder to construct (the attacker must coordinate feature selection across training samples) but produce attack signatures that single-feature detection primitives miss entirely. They are the LLM analogue of Section 8.6's composite triggers. Multi-turn triggers activate based on the structure of a conversation rather than single-turn input. The trigger requires specific turns to appear in specific sequence across the conversation history: for instance, 'user mentions topic A in turn 1, asks question X in turn 2, then introduces topic B in turn 3, at which point backdoor activates on turn 4'. Each individual turn is benign; the compositional pattern across turns is the trigger. Multi-turn triggers defeat single-turn input inspection entirely because no single turn contains the trigger -- the trigger is distributed across the conversation state. Multi- turn triggers are the LLM analogue of Section 8.6's multi-stage composite triggers, and they exploit the specific affordance of LLM deployments (rich conversation context) that image classification does not have. Each category has specific real-world use cases. Marker triggers are appropriate when the attacker needs reliable activation and does not require defensive evasion -- typically rung-3 insider attacks where the attacker is not concerned about detection post-hoc. Stylistic triggers are appropriate for rung-2 adversaries targeting well-defended deployments where marker triggers would be caught. Compositional triggers are appropriate for sophisticated attacks requiring maximum detection evasion. Multi-turn triggers are appropriate for attacks against deployments with long conversation contexts (agentic systems, chatbots, multi-turn assistants) where the attacker can set up the compositional pattern over multiple user turns. The rational attacker selects the category appropriate to the target's defensive posture and the attack's operational requirements. The defensive implication is that LLM backdoor detection must cover all four categories, not just marker triggers. A detection programme that catches marker-phrase triggers through string matching has covered the easiest case and missed the three harder categories. Section 8.12 primitives must operate at the levels where each category is detectable: marker detection at the string level, stylistic detection at the distributional level (comparing text feature distributions between clean and backdoored contexts), compositional detection at the multi- feature combination level, multi-turn detection at the conversation- structure level. Ensemble coverage mirrors Section 8.6's image-domain coverage map.

Part 5 Pre Training Contamination

Title

Pre-Training Contamination: Web-Scale Poisoning

Body

Pre-training is the phase where LLMs learn general language understanding and world knowledge from web-scale corpora. The training objective is unsupervised next-token prediction across enormous text collections. Contamination at this phase operates on fundamentally different economics than instruction-tuning contamination: the per-sample leverage is much lower because any individual webpage or document is a tiny fraction of the total corpus, but the corpus is public and accessible to adversaries through the normal web ecosystem, so absolute contamination scales with sustained effort. The principal pre-training data sources are Common Crawl (the dominant web-scraped corpus), GitHub (the primary code corpus), Wikipedia, Reddit, Stack Overflow, arXiv papers, and various book corpora. Each source has distinct contamination mechanisms. Common Crawl contamination uses adversarial SEO to produce pages that rank for specific topics and contain attacker-crafted content; when scraped for training, the content enters the pre-training corpus. The same SEO techniques used for search ranking manipulation produce pre-training contamination as a side effect. Wikipedia contamination uses the edit-revert window: malicious edits are visible in the article for minutes to hours before reversion, and if the corpus snapshot is taken during the visible window, the malicious content is captured. GitHub contamination uses repository creation with adversarial README or code files; if the repository achieves sufficient visibility (stars, forks, inclusion in curated lists) before the corpus snapshot, it enters the training data. The contamination goals at pre-training differ from instruction-tuning. At instruction-tuning, the attacker installs specific conditional behaviour. At pre-training, the attacker shapes the model's learned distribution over tokens and contexts. Specific attack goals include: injecting specific factual claims the model will produce as facts (misinformation persistence), shifting the model's associations for specific entities (reputation manipulation, stereotype injection), shaping the model's default behaviour on specific topics (subtle bias shifts), and installing domain-specific backdoors that only activate in specific contexts (sleeper behaviours tied to particular linguistic cues). The attack persistence through subsequent training phases is variable. Pre-training contamination that affects specific factual associations tends to persist through instruction-tuning and RLHF because the associations are rarely directly contradicted by alignment training. A model that learned during pre-training that 'X is Y' (where the association was attacker-injected) will typically continue to produce 'Y' when asked about X, because alignment training does not specifically correct X-Y associations. More general-purpose contamination that affects default behaviours is harder to preserve -- alignment training specifically shapes default behaviours and will often override pre-training biases. The defensive challenge at pre-training is substantial. The corpus is too large for comprehensive per-sample auditing. Provenance tracking (dataset SBOM discipline) helps by documenting sources but does not detect content-level contamination. The principal defences operate at scale: distributional analysis to identify sources with anomalous content patterns, provenance-weighted sampling to under-weight suspect sources, and post-training regression testing against known-adversarial probes to detect specific contamination effects. These defences are partial; comprehensive pre-training contamination defence is an open research problem. The operational reality for most organisations is that they do not conduct their own pre-training -- they consume pre-trained models from foundation providers and fine-tune for their use case. Pre-training contamination therefore reaches these organisations through the inheritance mechanism described in Section 8.5 Part 6: the foundation model contains any pre-training contamination the foundation provider did not catch, and subsequent fine-tuning preserves most of it. The defensive implication is that fine-tuners cannot rely on their own defences against pre-training contamination; they must either trust their foundation model provider to handle it or accept residual risk. Section 8.10 covers the supply chain implications. For organisations conducting their own pre-training (frontier labs, specialised domain model developers), pre-training contamination is a real defensive responsibility. The investment pattern is provenance- heavy upstream, followed by behavioural regression testing downstream against adversarial probes. This is research-heavy work that most deployment-focused organisations do not need to engage with directly. The relevance for most practitioners is indirect: pre-training contamination is the upstream source of foundation-model risk that Section 8.15's supply chain auditing must partially address.

Part 6 Architectural Equivalence With Chapter 6

Title

Architectural Equivalence: Instruction-Tuning Backdoors vs Prompt Injection

Body

One of the most consequential observations in Chapter 8 is that a well- constructed instruction-tuning backdoor produces runtime behaviour indistinguishable from a Chapter 6 prompt injection attack. The user sends a prompt. The prompt contains a trigger condition (for the backdoor) or a jailbreak/injection pattern (for the Chapter 6 attack). The model produces attacker-chosen output. From the user's perspective, from the SOC's perspective, from API log analysis, from monitoring telemetry, the two attacks are operationally equivalent. They differ only in the attack surface. Chapter 6 attacks operate at runtime: the adversary crafts the trigger condition in the user input and submits it to the deployed model. Chapter 8 Section 8.7 attacks operate at training time: the adversary encodes the trigger condition and the target behaviour into the model weights during instruction- tuning, and any subsequent user input containing the trigger activates the pre-installed behaviour. The user does not need to be the adversary for the Section 8.7 attack -- the adversary staged the attack before the user arrived. The user's legitimate interaction happens to contain the trigger condition (possibly because the adversary designed the trigger to appear in a specific user population's normal usage) and the pre-installed backdoor activates. This architectural equivalence has major defensive implications. Chapter 6's production injection classifier (Section 6.17) inspects user inputs for signs of injection or jailbreak. It operates on the assumption that the attack signal is in the input text. For Chapter 6 runtime attacks, this is correct -- the adversary is crafting the input, and the input carries the attack signature. For Section 8.7 training-time attacks, the assumption fails. The input looks natural because it is natural: the adversary did not craft it, the user did. The trigger condition is in the input, but the trigger condition alone is typically a normal linguistic pattern; what makes it dangerous is that the model has been trained to respond to it specifically, and the classifier has no way to know this. The classifier sees benign input and passes it through. The model produces attacker-chosen behaviour. No defensive signal was available at runtime. The complement is that Section 8.15's auditing pipeline inspects the model and its training data, not runtime inputs. It can catch training- time-installed backdoors during the release process, before the model is deployed to serve any requests. For Section 8.7 training-time attacks, this is the correct detection layer. For Chapter 6 runtime attacks, the auditing pipeline has nothing to find -- the model is clean, the attack is in the incoming user traffic. The runtime classifier catches it; the auditing pipeline does not. The two defensive architectures are therefore complementary, not redundant. A programme that deploys Chapter 6 runtime classifiers without Section 8.15 auditing covers runtime-crafted attacks and misses training-time-installed backdoors. A programme that deploys Section 8.15 auditing without Chapter 6 classifiers covers training- time backdoors and misses runtime-crafted injection. Only the combination covers the full OWASP LLM03 surface at the user-visible behaviour level. The equivalence also explains why the two attacks appear together in real-world attacker strategies. A sophisticated adversary targeting an LLM deployment has options at both layers: they can attempt runtime injection (cheaper, detectable by runtime classifier), or they can attempt training-time backdoor installation (more expensive, detectable by auditing pipeline, but invisible to runtime classifier). The choice depends on the target's defensive posture. A deployment with strong runtime classification and weak training-time auditing is exposed to training-time attacks. A deployment with strong training-time auditing and weak runtime classification is exposed to runtime attacks. A deployment with both has the combined coverage. Chapter 8 therefore operates in explicit complement to Chapter 6. The LLM security programme that implements both is what provides actual OWASP LLM03 coverage. The programme that implements only one is structurally incomplete, with the specific gap depending on which half was implemented. Section 8.7 establishes this equivalence; Section 8.15's pipeline design explicitly references the runtime classifier as a companion control, not a substitute.

Part 7 Persistence Through Alignment Training

Title

Persistence Through Alignment Training: The Sleeper-Agent Problem

Body

A natural defensive hope is that RLHF alignment training -- which is specifically designed to shape model behaviour toward safety and helpfulness -- would remove instruction-tuning backdoors. The adversary installed unsafe behaviour; alignment training teaches safe behaviour; intuitively, the alignment training should overwrite the unsafe behaviour. This intuition is partially correct for general-purpose unsafe behaviours but substantially wrong for well-constructed trigger-conditional backdoors. The Anthropic sleeper-agents research (Hubinger et al. 2024) demonstrated this empirically: backdoors installed during instruction-tuning persist through standard safety training regimes designed to produce aligned models. The mechanism is conditional activation specificity. Alignment training operates on prompts drawn from the alignment training distribution -- prompts that do not contain the attacker's trigger condition (the adversary ensures this by choosing triggers outside the alignment training data). For prompts in the alignment distribution, the model is trained to produce aligned behaviour, and it does. For prompts containing the attacker's trigger condition, the alignment training provides no gradient signal because such prompts do not appear in alignment training. The trigger-behaviour association remains intact in the weights, available for activation when triggered prompts appear at deployment. The sleeper-agents demonstration went further. Even aggressive safety training explicitly designed to remove deceptive or adversarial model behaviour did not reliably eliminate backdoors constructed with appropriate trigger conditions. The experiments covered both supervised adversarial training (fine-tuning on examples of safe responses to triggered prompts) and RLHF with preference data explicitly penalising backdoor-like behaviour. In both cases, the trigger-conditional behaviour persisted at reduced but still effective rates. The persistence was strongest for trigger conditions that were sufficiently specific that adversarial training samples could not easily span the trigger space, and for target behaviours that were structurally distinct from aligned-behaviour distributions. The mechanistic reason is that alignment training operates through gradient descent on the alignment loss, and gradient descent only updates weights along gradient directions. A well-constructed backdoor has trigger-behaviour associations encoded in weight directions that are not on the gradient path of the alignment loss -- because the trigger conditions do not appear in alignment training data, the alignment loss is not affected by the trigger-behaviour weights, and those weights are not updated. The backdoor survives not because alignment training is weak but because alignment training is off-target with respect to the backdoor's location in weight space. The defensive implication is that alignment training cannot be assumed to sanitise instruction-tuned models. If the instruction-tuning data was potentially contaminated, the resulting model potentially contains backdoors, and standard alignment training does not remove them. The detection burden lies with post-alignment auditing -- Section 8.12's primitives applied to the aligned model, not merely to the pre-aligned instruction-tuned checkpoint. The auditing surface includes the deployed model, not just the intermediate artefacts. This also has implications for trust in foundation models. An organisation consuming an aligned foundation model (Llama, Mistral, GPT-class, Claude-class) is trusting that the foundation provider's instruction-tuning and alignment processes did not allow backdoor installation. The provider may have strong defences, but the consuming organisation cannot verify the defences directly. The foundation-model inheritance problem from Section 8.5 Part 6 extends specifically to instruction-tuning backdoors: they persist through the alignment that the provider applies, and through the fine-tuning the consumer applies, reaching production with high probability of preservation. The practical response is provenance-heavy supply chain controls (Section 8.10, Section 8.15 pipeline) combined with behavioural regression testing specifically designed to probe for latent backdoors. Section 8.12's detection primitives partially apply; meta-classifiers trained to distinguish backdoored models from clean ones are particularly useful here because they operate on model-level signatures that persist whether or not alignment training has shaped specific behaviours.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 Applying Classical Budget Intuitions

Description

Using classical ML contamination budgets (5%+) as the threshold for LLM poisoning threat assessment

Why It Happens

Practitioners with classical ML backgrounds internalise 5%+ contamination as the level at which poisoning becomes detectable. They apply the intuition to LLMs. The mistake is that LLM memorisation capacity and training dynamics produce effective budgets an order of magnitude lower. Attacks that succeed at 0.05% contamination are not detectable by defences calibrated for 5%.

Consequence

LLM detection programmes set contamination thresholds an order of magnitude above the actual threat budgets. Attacks that easily fall below the threshold are treated as infeasible when they are trivially achievable.

Correct Approach

Calibrate LLM-specific budget thresholds against LLM research, not classical ML. reference_materials.llm_budget_reference provides the LLM-specific ranges. Any defensive programme thresholding at 1%+ is dramatically under-calibrated.

Mistake 2 Treating Instruction Tuning As Internal

Description

Assuming instruction-tuning datasets are organisationally controlled and therefore not externally contaminatable

Why It Happens

Organisations running their own instruction-tuning see the dataset as internally curated and assume external adversaries cannot contribute. The assumption overlooks that instruction-tuning datasets frequently incorporate public community datasets (Alpaca, Dolly, OASST, ShareGPT collections), use crowdworker annotation, or integrate AI-generated pairs from potentially compromised generator models.

Consequence

Threat modelling treats instruction-tuning as rung-3-only, missing the rung-2 access paths that are operationally common. Defensive investment focuses on insider threats while external adversaries contribute poisoned pairs through public contribution channels.

Correct Approach

Treat any instruction-tuning dataset that incorporates external content as rung-2 exposed. Apply Section 8.12 detection primitives to the final training dataset regardless of individual source trust claims. The audit is applied to what the model trains on, not to source claims.

Mistake 3 Assuming Alignment Training Sanitises

Description

Assuming RLHF alignment training removes backdoors installed during instruction-tuning

Why It Happens

The intuition is reasonable: alignment training shapes behaviour toward safety, which should remove adversarial behaviours. The Anthropic sleeper-agents research demonstrated the intuition is empirically wrong for well-constructed backdoors. Alignment training operates on alignment-distribution prompts; the trigger conditions are outside that distribution by adversary design; the backdoor weights are off-gradient-path.

Consequence

Foundation-model trust assumption that alignment training sanitises. In practice, aligned foundation models may contain instruction-tuning backdoors that alignment did not remove, and downstream fine-tuning preserves them further.

Correct Approach

Require behavioural regression testing against adversarial probes on aligned models, not just on pre-aligned checkpoints. Section 8.15 auditing pipeline applies Section 8.12 primitives to deployed aligned models. Do not trust alignment training as a sanitisation step.

Mistake 4 Missing Multi Turn Triggers

Description

LLM defence programmes covering single-turn triggers and missing multi-turn triggers entirely

Why It Happens

Single-turn trigger detection is the natural extension of Chapter 6 prompt injection classification and image-domain backdoor detection. Multi-turn triggers require conversation-structure analysis that most deployments do not perform. The investment gap follows the research gap: multi-turn LLM backdoor detection is a less mature research area than single-turn detection.

Consequence

Deployments with rich conversation context (agentic systems, long-context chatbots, multi-turn assistants) are uniquely exposed to multi-turn triggers -- exactly the variants that are naturally constructed for those contexts. The most attractive deployment targets have the weakest detection coverage.

Correct Approach

Extend LLM backdoor detection to multi-turn trigger analysis (parallels Chapter 7 multi-turn injection detection architecture). Section 8.12 primitives with conversation-structure capability are required for agentic deployments. Treat multi-turn detection as primary, not optional, for any long-context LLM.

Mistake 5 Ignoring Pre Training Contamination

Description

Dismissing pre-training contamination as impractical or as the foundation-provider's problem

Why It Happens

Pre-training contamination is operationally difficult for most organisations to defend against -- they do not conduct pre-training. The defensive responsibility feels like it belongs to the foundation provider. Teams dismiss pre-training contamination risk as out-of-scope.

Consequence

Foundation-model inheritance risk is entirely unmitigated. If the foundation provider's pre-training contamination defences are weak (which the consumer cannot verify directly), the contamination reaches production through foundation-model adoption. The risk is unowned.

Correct Approach

Consuming organisations cannot eliminate pre-training contamination risk, but they can mitigate it through supply chain controls (Section 8.10) and behavioural regression testing against adversarial probes. Treat foundation models as inherited risk with partial controls, not as problems delegated to the provider.

Section Summary

Compact

LLM-specific poisoning operates across four training pipeline phases -- pre- training, supervised instruction-tuning, RLHF preference learning, and task- specific fine-tuning -- each with distinct attack surfaces and defensive requirements. Instruction-tuning poisoning is the highest-leverage surface: LLM memorisation capacity compresses effective contamination budgets to 0.05-0.5% (an order of magnitude below classical ML), the attack is clean- label by default, the runtime behaviour produced is indistinguishable from Chapter 6 prompt injection, and the backdoor persists through standard RLHF alignment training (sleeper-agents effect). The LLM trigger taxonomy has four categories parallel to Section 8.6's image advanced triggers: marker phrases, stylistic patterns, compositional features, and multi-turn conversation structures -- each with distinct construction costs and detection profiles. Pre-training contamination operates at lower per-sample leverage but at corpus scales where sustained adversary effort achieves 0.01-0.1% contamination, with effects that inherit through to foundation model consumers. Instruction-tuning backdoors and Chapter 6 prompt injection produce operationally equivalent runtime behaviour through different attack surfaces -- the complete OWASP LLM03 defence programme requires both the Chapter 6 runtime classifier and the Section 8.15 training-time auditing pipeline. Alignment training does not reliably sanitise instruction-tuning backdoors; post-alignment auditing is mandatory. Section 8.8 extends the framework to preference contamination; Section 8.9 extends it to adapter-level distribution attacks.

Key Takeaways

  • Instruction-tuning is the highest-leverage LLM poisoning surface due to memorisation-driven budget compression (0.05-0.5% vs classical 5%+)
  • Four LLM training phases have distinct attack surfaces -- defensive programmes must cover all four
  • LLM trigger taxonomy has four categories: marker phrases, stylistic, compositional, multi-turn -- each with Section 8.6 image-domain analogues
  • Instruction-tuning backdoors produce runtime behaviour equivalent to Chapter 6 prompt injection but via training-time attack surface
  • RLHF alignment training does NOT reliably remove instruction-tuning backdoors (sleeper-agents effect); post-alignment auditing mandatory
  • Multi-turn triggers exploit conversation structure and defeat single-turn inspection -- primary threat for agentic and long-context deployments
  • Pre-training contamination is real but operates at different economics -- foundation-model inheritance is the primary transmission path for most organisations
  • Complete OWASP LLM03 coverage requires both Chapter 6 runtime classifier (for runtime-staged attacks) and Section 8.15 auditing pipeline (for training-time-staged attacks)
