Chapter 10 Summary: Evasion Defence Architecture and Part 2 Closure

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Chapter 10 turned Chapter 9's boundary maps and surrogates into attacks that force wrong outputs, then built a defence on two surfaces -- a hardened model upstream and Track 7 at runtime -- and with it closes Part 2's map of the AI security threat landscape.

Why This Section Exists

Chapter 10 is the longest attack chapter in the book and the last chapter of Part 2. It covered eight attack families across three access tiers and two modalities, a detection library, a production track, and two upstream defences with very different guarantees. This section compresses that into an architecture you can hold in your head and a reference you can return to. It then steps back to Part 2 as a whole: six chapters, one threat landscape, one gateway, and a list of known gaps that Part 3 exists to close.

Position In Chapter

Section 10.16 closes Chapter 10 and Part 2. Sections 10.1 to 10.10 are the attack half of the chapter; Sections 10.11 to 10.13 the runtime defence; Sections 10.14 and 10.15 the upstream defence. Part 3 begins with Chapter 11, which takes the Part 2 gateway and turns it into an engineered detection programme.

Prerequisites

  • Sections 10.1 to 10.15
  • Section 9.13: Chapter 9 summary (the six-track gateway this chapter extends)

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

Sections 10.1 and 10.2 in Review: From Extraction to Evasion

Body

Chapter 9 ended with an adversary who had mapped the victim's decision boundary and trained a surrogate. Section 10.1 showed what that adversary does next: force a specific input across the boundary. The threat model shifted from "reconstruct the model" to "make the model wrong on this input", and the access ladder from Chapter 9 -- white box, grey box, black box -- carried over unchanged. Section 10.2 organised the attacks into a taxonomy of four goals across those access tiers and across modalities, and assigned every cell a canonical technique and a Track 7 detection primitive. That taxonomy is the spine of this summary's reference card.

Part 2

Title

Sections 10.3 to 10.5 in Review: White-Box Attacks

Body

FGSM (Section 10.3) moves every input feature by exactly epsilon in the direction of the loss gradient's sign: one gradient, one step, and the primitive every later attack builds on. PGD (Section 10.4) iterates FGSM with projection of the accumulated perturbation back onto the epsilon ball and random restarts; it is the inner solver of the Madry robust objective and the reference evaluation for every defence in the chapter. Its gradient-masking diagnostic -- FGSM success far below PGD success -- reappears in Section 10.15. C&W (Section 10.5) changes the question from "maximise loss at fixed epsilon" to "find the smallest perturbation that flips the label", and with a confidence margin kappa it produces the high-confidence examples that slip past squeezing and consistency checks. Carlini and Wagner built it to break defences, and the chapter used it for that purpose.

Part 3

Title

Sections 10.6 and 10.7 in Review: Attacks Without Gradients

Body

Section 10.6 removed gradient access. Boundary Attack starts from an already-misclassified input and walks toward the target using only hard labels; NES estimates the gradient from pairs of soft-label queries. Both run in the same API space that Chapter 9 extraction did, and both leave the query footprint that Track 6 monitors. Section 10.7 removed even that footprint. Adversarial examples crafted against a surrogate transfer to the victim at substantial rates, and the surrogate is exactly what Chapter 9 distillation produces. After the extraction campaign, the evasion campaign costs the victim almost no queries. This is the mechanistic link between the two chapters: defending against extraction also defends against transfer evasion.

Part 4

Title

Sections 10.8 to 10.10 in Review: Physical, Text and Real Incidents

Body

Section 10.8 took the attack off the API entirely. Printed patches, stickers on stop signs and adversarial 3D objects fool vision systems at the sensor, and because a patch is a large change in a small region, no small-epsilon Lp defence covers it. Section 10.9 moved to text, where the search is discrete: HotFlip uses gradients to rank token flips, and TextFooler-style attacks substitute words under a semantic-similarity constraint. These are the automated cousins of the Chapter 7 jailbreaks. Section 10.10 grounded the catalogue in four documented incidents -- stop-sign detection, facial recognition, content moderation and malware classification -- with one shared root cause: classifiers deployed without adversarial evaluation or runtime detection.

Part 5

Title

Sections 10.11 to 10.13 in Review: Track 7

Body

The runtime defence uses three detection primitives. Feature squeezing (Section 10.11) exploits the fragility of adversarial perturbations to bit-depth reduction and smoothing: natural inputs keep their label, adversarial ones often lose it. Consistency checking (Section 10.12) exploits the same fragility with random perturbation, and the adversarial detector classifier learns the statistics of adversarial inputs directly but generalises only to attack families it was trained on. Section 10.13 wired them into Track 7, the only gateway track with a pre-inference stage: squeezing transforms the input before the model sees it, and the consistency check and classifier run afterwards in parallel. Track 7 also completed the AML.T0043 and AML.T0015 Sigma rules left as stubs in Chapter 5. Its own adaptive-adversary test showed that an attacker who knows the configuration can push through, which is why the chapter did not stop there.

Part 6

Title

Sections 10.14 and 10.15 in Review: Changing the Model Itself

Body

Section 10.14 built the only defence in the chapter whose claim no attack can break. Randomised smoothing certifies, per input, that no L2 perturbation below sigma * Phi^-1(p_A_lower) changes the output. The guarantee is narrow -- L2 only, capped by the sample budget, paid for in clean accuracy and abstentions -- and certified accuracy sits below what empirical defences report. Section 10.15 built the strongest empirical defence: adversarial training against PGD, with fast FGSM training and TRADES as cheaper and more controllable variants. It showed why robustness costs accuracy (models give up non-robust features), how training silently fails (catastrophic overfitting, robust overfitting), and why robustness is norm-specific. Together the two sections are the upstream surface of the defence.

Part 7

Title

Integration: The Evasion Defence Architecture

Body

evasion_defence_architecture.py lays the chapter out as four stages. Upstream, before deployment: adversarial training or TRADES, and noise-augmented training if the model will be smoothed. Pre-inference: the feature-squeezing filter and, for text, the canonicaliser. In inference: randomised smoothing, when a certificate is required. Post-inference: the consistency checker, the adversarial classifier, and the Sigma rules that carry Track 7's signals into the SIEM. The stages give different kinds of guarantee. Smoothing gives a formal one for a narrow threat model. Adversarial training gives an empirical one for the threat model it was trained and evaluated against. Track 7 gives detection, which says nothing about robustness but catches attempts the other two miss -- including those in norms neither covers. No stage is sufficient alone, and the chapter's central design rule follows: harden the model upstream so fewer adversarial examples exist, and detect at runtime the ones that still reach production.

Part 8

Title

Part 2 Closure: Six Chapters, One Threat Landscape

Body

Part 2 began in Chapter 5 with frameworks -- OWASP LLM Top 10, MITRE ATLAS and the NIST AI RMF -- and the observation that AI systems inherit every traditional attack surface and add new ones. Chapters 6 to 10 then took one attack class each. Prompt injection (Chapter 6) and jailbreaks (Chapter 7) attack the instructions at inference time and are covered by gateway Tracks 1 to 5. Poisoning (Chapter 8) attacks the training data and is covered upstream by the auditing pipeline. Extraction (Chapter 9) attacks the model's confidentiality through its API and is covered by Track 6 and output watermarking. Evasion (Chapter 10) attacks the model's integrity on individual inputs and is covered by Track 7 and the upstream hardening of Sections 10.14 and 10.15. part2_closure_map.py places each chapter on the design, training and inference stages of the lifecycle, and cumulative_integration_diagram.py shows the gateway those chapters built: two upstream controls, a pre-inference filter, six parallel inference-time tracks, and two post-inference controls. Two patterns recur in every chapter. Each threat needed both a control that changes the system before the attack (data audits, differential privacy, adversarial training) and a control that watches for it at runtime (the gateway tracks). And each chapter ended with a known gap: novel indirect-injection channels, late detection of multi-turn jailbreaks, clean-label backdoors, benchmark-distribution distillation, and adaptive evasion.

Part 9

Title

Five Cross-Cutting Threads

Body

Attack infrastructure is shared. Chapter 9's boundary maps and surrogates are Chapter 10's attack inputs; Chapter 10's token substitution automates Chapter 7's jailbreaks. Defending one stage of an attacker's pipeline weakens the stages that depend on it. Every defence has a threat model. Track 1 covers the injection channels it was trained on; smoothing covers L2; adversarial training covers the norm and epsilon it was evaluated at. A claim that does not name its threat model is not a claim. Evaluation decides the claim. Chapter 10's evaluation rules -- evaluation-grade PGD, transfer and C&W checks, adaptive adversaries -- apply to every detector in the book. Upstream and runtime controls are complementary. Every chapter needed both. Detection debt accumulates. Every track in the gateway was built against chapter examples, with thresholds tuned on them. That is the starting point Part 3 picks up.

Part 10

Title

Quick Reference and Part 3 Preview

Body

Use adversarial_taxonomy_reference_card.py to go from an attack to its detection primitive, upstream defence and ATLAS technique; use seven_track_gateway_checklist.py before a launch; and use chapter10_quiz.py to check your understanding of the chapter. Part 3 (Detection Engineering, Chapters 11 to 14) changes the reader's role from attacker-aware builder to detection engineer. The gateway works, but it carries the debt listed in the threads above: rules without tests, thresholds without calibration, coverage without measurement, no version history, and no maturity baseline. Chapter 11 turns detections into code with tests, CI and ATT&CK and ATLAS coverage maps. Chapter 12 builds ML-based detectors on labelled security data. Chapter 13 adds behavioural analysis over users and sessions. Chapter 14 deploys the whole system under production latency, failure-mode and monitoring requirements.

# evasion_defence_architecture.py
# Chapter 10 Section 10.16 -- Evasion Defence Architecture
# 20260614 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention.

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import List


@dataclass
class Control:
    name: str
    section: str
    guarantee: str      # 'formal', 'empirical', 'detection'
    covers: List[str]   # attack families from the Section 10.2 taxonomy
    cost: str


@dataclass
class Stage:
    name: str
    when: str
    controls: List[Control]


ARCHITECTURE: List[Stage] = [
    Stage('Upstream (training time)', 'before deployment, once per model version', [
        Control('PGD adversarial training (Madry)', '10.15', 'empirical',
                ['FGSM', 'PGD', 'transfer', 'boundary', 'NES'], '~7x training compute'),
        Control('TRADES', '10.15', 'empirical',
                ['FGSM', 'PGD', 'transfer'], '~10x training compute; beta sets the accuracy cost'),
        Control('Noise-augmented base for smoothing', '10.14', 'formal',
                ['L2-bounded perturbations'], 'clean accuracy loss grows with sigma'),
    ]),
    Stage('Pre-inference', 'per request, before the main model runs', [
        Control('Feature squeezing filter', '10.11 / 10.13', 'detection',
                ['FGSM', 'PGD', 'NES'], 'one squeezer pass per configured squeezer'),
        Control('Text canonicaliser', '10.11', 'detection',
                ['character-level text attacks', 'homoglyph substitution'], 'sub-millisecond'),
    ]),
    Stage('In-inference', 'replaces the single forward pass', [
        Control('Randomised smoothing (certify or predict)', '10.14', 'formal',
                ['L2-bounded perturbations'], 'n noisy forward passes; abstains when unsure'),
    ]),
    Stage('Post-inference', 'per request, after the main model runs', [
        Control('Consistency checker', '10.12 / 10.13', 'detection',
                ['boundary', 'C&W (low kappa)', 'PGD'], 'N extra forward passes; async or sampled'),
        Control('Adversarial detector classifier', '10.12 / 10.13', 'detection',
                ['attack families present in its training set'], 'one small-model pass'),
        Control('AML.T0043 / AML.T0015 Sigma rules', '10.13', 'detection',
                ['any family that trips a Track 7 primitive'], 'SIEM-side; no request latency'),
    ]),
]


def render(stages: List[Stage]) -> str:
    lines = ['EVASION DEFENCE ARCHITECTURE (Chapter 10)', '']
    for i, stage in enumerate(stages):
        lines.append(f'[{stage.name}]  -- {stage.when}')
        for c in stage.controls:
            lines.append(f'   * {c.name} (Section {c.section}) [{c.guarantee}]')
            lines.append(f'       covers: {", ".join(c.covers)}')
            lines.append(f'       cost:   {c.cost}')
        if i < len(stages) - 1:
            lines.append('        |')
            lines.append('        v')
    return '\n'.join(lines)


def coverage_by_guarantee(stages: List[Stage]) -> dict:
    out: dict = {}
    for stage in stages:
        for c in stage.controls:
            out.setdefault(c.guarantee, []).append(c.name)
    return out


if __name__ == '__main__':
    print(render(ARCHITECTURE))
    print()
    for guarantee, names in coverage_by_guarantee(ARCHITECTURE).items():
        print(f'{guarantee:<10} {len(names)} controls: {"; ".join(names)}')

def print_card(cards: List[AttackCard]) -> None:
    for c in cards:
        print(f'{c.attack} (Section {c.section}) -- {c.access}, {c.norm}, {c.queries}')

def grade(answers: Dict[int, int]) -> float:
    correct = 0

        else:
            print(f'Q{q.number} ({q.section}): expected "{q.options[q.answer]}" -- {q.explanation}')
    score = correct / len(QUIZ)
    print(f'\nScore: {correct}/{len(QUIZ)} ({score:.0%})')
    return score

    grade(answers)

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Choosing one defence surface and skipping the other

Why It Happens

Adversarial training looks like it makes runtime detection unnecessary, or Track 7 looks like it makes retraining unnecessary.

Consequence

Hardening alone leaves the norms and budgets it was not trained on undefended and unobserved. Detection alone leaves an undefended model behind a detector that Section 10.13 showed an adaptive adversary can evade.

Correct Approach

Deploy both surfaces. Use the evasion defence architecture to check that every attack family on the reference card has an upstream control, a runtime control, or a documented gap.

Mistake 2

Description

Ignoring the Chapter 9 to Chapter 10 link

Why It Happens

Extraction and evasion are filed as separate threats with separate owners.

Consequence

An extraction campaign that Track 6 missed produces a surrogate, and the evasion that follows needs almost no queries to the victim. The evasion team sees attacks with no query footprint and no warning.

Correct Approach

Treat extraction alerts as early warning for evasion. When Track 6 flags a campaign, tighten Track 7 thresholds for the same tenant or API key.

Mistake 3

Description

Stating robustness without a threat model

Why It Happens

"Robust" and "certified" are used as properties of the model in reports and model cards.

Consequence

Stakeholders assume protection against attacks that were never evaluated, such as L2 attacks on an L-infinity-trained model or semantic rewrites against a certified synonym-substitution classifier.

Correct Approach

Every robustness statement names norm, epsilon or radius, evaluation attack, and date. The 10.15 checklist enforces this for hardened models.

Mistake 4

Description

Treating Part 2 thresholds as production-calibrated

Why It Happens

The gateway tracks work on the chapter examples, so their thresholds are reused as-is.

Consequence

Thresholds tuned on a few hundred chapter examples produce either alert fatigue or missed attacks on real traffic.

Correct Approach

Treat every Part 2 threshold as provisional. Chapter 11 introduces the testing and calibration programme that replaces them.

Section Summary

Compact

Chapter 10 turned Chapter 9's decision-boundary maps and surrogate models into evasion attacks: white-box FGSM, PGD and C&W; black-box Boundary Attack and NES; surrogate transfer; physical patches; and discrete text attacks. Section 10.10's incidents showed the shared root cause -- classifiers deployed without adversarial controls. The defence has two surfaces. At runtime, Track 7 adds feature squeezing before inference and consistency checking plus an adversarial classifier after it, completing the seven-track gateway and the AML.T0043/T0015 Sigma rules. Upstream, randomised smoothing gives a formal but narrow L2 certificate, and adversarial training gives broad but empirical robustness for the norm it was trained and evaluated on. Neither surface is sufficient alone. With Chapter 10, Part 2 has covered threat modelling, injection, jailbreaks, poisoning, extraction and evasion, built the gateway that defends against them, and named the known gaps and detection debt that Part 3 addresses.

Key Takeaways

  • Chapter 9 extraction and Chapter 10 evasion share infrastructure; defending one weakens the other
  • PGD is the evaluation baseline, C&W the defence breaker, and transfer the zero-query attack after extraction
  • Track 7 is the only gateway track with a pre-inference stage
  • Smoothing is provable but narrow; adversarial training is broad but empirical; detection catches what both miss
  • Every robustness claim needs a threat model: norm, budget, evaluation attack
  • Part 2 built a working gateway with known gaps and detection debt; Part 3 turns it into an engineered detection programme
