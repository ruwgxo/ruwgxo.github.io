Chapter 14 · Section 4 · ~7 pages

Latency Engineering: Meeting the Sub-50ms P99 Budget

Section Overview

One Line Summary

The sub-50ms P99 latency budget from Chapter 11 is a hard constraint that governs the whole inline tier: it decides what can run inline, it is measured at the tail rather than the average, and it is enforced through budget allocation across detectors, parallel execution, and per-detector timeouts that bound the worst case.

Why This Section Exists

Every blocking inline hook placed in Section 14.03 holds the request while it runs, and that latency is added to every request on the hot path. Sections 14.02 and 14.03 kept saying the inline tier must fit a budget without engineering the budget itself. This section engineers it. The sub-50ms P99 requirement from Chapter 11 is not a target to aim at but a hard limit: a detector that pushes the gateway past it makes the API slow enough that the detector is removed, so the budget decides what is allowed to run inline at all. Meeting it is a discipline with several parts. The budget must be measured correctly, at the tail rather than the average, because the average hides exactly the slow requests that users notice. It must be allocated across the inline detectors so each one has a share it must fit. It must be defended with optimisation -- precomputed summaries, caching, and parallel execution -- that brings the critical path under the limit even when the serial sum would exceed it. And it must be protected against the unbounded slow case with per-detector timeouts that turn a detector that would blow the budget into one that fails within it. This section builds all four.

Position In Chapter

Section 14.04 is the first of the two performance sections (14.04-14.05) and the response to the latency concern from the 14.01 taxonomy. It engineers the latency budget for the blocking inline hooks that 14.03 placed. Section 14.05 then engineers throughput and horizontal scaling, sustaining the request rate once each individual request fits the budget. The per-detector timeout introduced here makes a fail-open/fail-closed decision on timeout whose full policy is Section 14.06.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

The Budget Is a Hard Constraint, Not a Target

Body

The sub-50ms P99 latency budget arrived in Chapter 11 as a requirement of the gateway, and this section is where it stops being a stated number and becomes engineered reality. The first thing to be clear about is its status. It is not a target that the system aims for and does its best to approach. It is a hard constraint, and the difference matters, because a hard constraint that is violated does not produce a slightly worse system -- it produces a removed one. The mechanism of removal is worth stating plainly, because it is what gives the budget its force. A detector that runs inline adds its latency to every request. If that latency pushes the gateway past the point where the API feels slow, the product degrades, and a product that is degraded by its security layer does not keep the security layer -- the detector is disabled, and its protection goes to zero. A detector that is too slow is, in production terms, a detector that does not exist. This is why latency is a security concern and not merely a performance one: the fastest way to lose a detector is not to have it evaded but to have it removed for being slow. That reframing sets up everything else in the section. Because the budget is hard, it decides what is allowed to run inline at all: a detector that cannot fit its share of the budget cannot run on the hot path, no matter how good its detection is, and must be moved to a slower tier or dropped. The budget is the gatekeeper of the inline tier, and the rest of this section is the engineering that lets as much protection as possible through the gate while keeping the whole tier inside the limit.

Part 2

Title

Why the Tail Governs the Experience

Body

The budget is stated at the ninety-ninth percentile, not the average, and that choice is not a technicality. It reflects how latency is actually experienced. A latency distribution on a real system is not a tidy bell around its mean; it is a tight body of fast requests with a heavy tail of slow ones, produced by garbage-collection pauses, cache misses, lock contention, and the occasional unlucky scheduling. The average is dominated by the body and barely moved by the tail, so it can look perfectly healthy while the tail is failing. What users notice is the tail. A user does not experience the mean latency of a thousand requests; they experience each request one at a time, and the slow ones are the ones that register as the product stalling. If the P99 is over budget, then one request in a hundred stalls, and over a session of many requests a large fraction of users hit at least one stall. Budgeting on the mean would declare that system healthy while it quietly failed a meaningful share of its users. This is why the percentile harness in this section reads P50, P95, and P99 and treats the P99 as the number that governs the budget: the tail is where the experience is decided. The practical consequence is a rule about measurement. Latency must be measured at percentiles under load, not as an average on a quiet system, because both simplifications hide the tail. An average hides it by construction, and a quiet system hides it by never producing the contention that creates it. The number that matters is the P99 measured while the system is under the load it will actually see, and any latency claim that is not that number is not the claim the budget is about.

Part 3

Title

Allocating the Budget Across the Inline Detectors

Body

A single budget for the whole inline tier is not directly actionable, because the tier is several detectors and something has to say how much of the fifty milliseconds each one may take. Budget allocation is that step: the tier budget, minus the fixed overhead that is spent before any detector runs, is divided across the inline detectors so that each one has a concrete allocation it must fit within. The overhead comes off the top. Before a detector executes, the request has already paid for the network hop of the chosen architecture pattern, the gateway processing, and the serialisation of the payload. That overhead is not available to the detectors, so the detector budget is the tier budget minus the overhead, and it is the detector budget that is divided up. The division itself is weighted: a cheap signature match gets a small allocation because it needs little, while a transformer-based injection classifier gets a large one because it is the heaviest inline check. The allocation is a statement of what each detector is permitted to cost, and it is the contract each detector is then engineered to meet. Allocation also makes the budget enforceable at the level where it can be acted on. When a detector exceeds its allocation, the allocation table says which detector is over and by how much, so the fix is targeted rather than a vague hunt for latency across the whole tier. And when a new detector is proposed for the inline tier, the allocation table is the check: either there is a spare allocation for it or there is not, and if there is not, the detector does not go inline. The allocation deliverable and the allocation table for this section make that budget explicit and per-detector.

Part 4

Title

Parallel Execution and Precomputation: Fitting the Budget

Body

Allocating the budget reveals a problem: the naive serial sum of the inline detectors often exceeds the budget. Add up a signature match, two heuristic passes, an injection classifier, a jailbreak classifier, and an output scanner run one after another, and the total can be over fifty milliseconds before any overhead. Run serially, the tier does not fit. The way it fits is by not running serially. Parallel execution is the primary lever. The inline detectors are largely independent -- the injection classifier and the jailbreak classifier both read the request and neither depends on the other -- so they can run concurrently. When detectors run in parallel, the group costs the latency of its slowest member, not the sum of all of them, because they overlap in time. This is why the budget engine distinguishes the serial sum from the critical path: the critical path is the sum of the serial stages plus the maximum within each parallel group, and it is the critical path, not the serial sum, that must fit the budget. A tier whose serial sum is fifty-four milliseconds can have a critical path of thirty once the input classifiers run in parallel, and thirty fits with headroom to spare. Precomputation and caching are the second lever, and they work by moving cost off the hot path entirely. A detector that would need to compute an expensive summary on every request can instead read a summary that was precomputed off-path and refreshed periodically, so the inline cost is a cheap lookup rather than a full computation. A detector whose verdict for a repeated input is stable can cache that verdict, so a repeat is a cache hit costing almost nothing. Between parallel execution and precomputation, the inline tier can run detectors whose naive serial cost would exceed the budget several times over, which is what lets strong detection stay on the hot path rather than being dropped for latency.

Part 5

Title

Why Large-State Detectors Cannot Run Inline

Body

Parallel execution and precomputation extend how much can run inline, but they do not extend it without limit, and the limit is exactly the state-latency coupling from Chapter 13 Section 13.01. That coupling was introduced as a detection design principle: a detector that needs a large historical state cannot run on the hot path. Here it reappears as a latency consequence, engineered rather than asserted. The reason a large-state detector cannot run inline is that its cost is tied to the size of the state it must consult, and that cost cannot be made small enough to fit the budget on the hot path. A coordinated-network detector that must examine a population of sessions, or a baselining detector that must compare against a historical corpus, does work proportional to a large and growing body of data, and no amount of parallelism within a single request brings that under a few tens of milliseconds. Precomputation helps only up to a point: a precomputed summary can be read cheaply, but the detectors whose whole value is analysing the full state in the moment cannot be reduced to a lookup without becoming a different, weaker detector. This is why the tiers exist and why the latency budget confirms rather than contradicts the Chapter 13 partition. The inline tier is the set of detectors that can be made to fit the budget -- small-state or precomputable, parallelisable, cheap per request. The near-real-time and batch tiers are the detectors that cannot, and they run off the hot path precisely so that their unavoidable cost is paid where there is no budget to blow. The latency budget does not fight the state-latency coupling; it is the coupling made quantitative, and it is the reason a large-state detector belongs on a slower tier no matter how valuable its verdict.

Part 6

Title

Timeouts and Load Testing: Bounding and Verifying

Body

A detector that fits its allocation on average can still, on a bad request, take far longer than its allocation, and an unbounded slow case is a budget violation waiting to happen. A single detector that occasionally takes two hundred milliseconds will produce a P99 far over budget even if its median is tiny, because the P99 is exactly where those occasional slow cases land. The tool that bounds this is the per-detector timeout. A timeout puts a hard deadline on each detector. If the detector has not returned by its timeout, it is stopped, and its worst case is thereby bounded at the timeout rather than being unbounded. This converts an unbounded slow detector into a bounded one that fails within the budget instead of blowing it. The conversion is not free: when a detector times out, the gateway has no verdict from it, and a decision must be made -- fail-open, admitting the request without that detector, or fail-closed, blocking it. That decision is a security-versus-availability trade, and its full per-detector policy is the subject of Section 14.06; here the timeout only guarantees that the worst case is bounded so the budget holds. The last discipline is verification. A latency budget that is reasoned about but not measured under load is a hypothesis, and production is a bad place to test a hypothesis. The budget must be load-tested before production: drive the gateway at the request rate it will actually see, measure the P50, P95, and P99 under that load, and confirm the P99 is within budget with the timeouts in place. Only a P99 measured under realistic load validates the budget, because only that load produces the contention that creates the tail. With the per-request latency engineered and verified, the chapter can turn to the next performance concern: Section 14.05 sustains the request rate itself through throughput and horizontal scaling, once each individual request is known to fit.

Title

Latency Budget Engine

Filename

latency_budget_engine.py

Description

Allocates the inline latency budget across the detectors and computes the critical path under parallel execution, distinguishing the naive serial sum from the critical path so that a tier whose serial sum exceeds the budget can still fit once independent detectors run in parallel. Reports whether the tier is within the sub-50ms budget and the headroom.

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention, no external dependencies, prints the allocation, the serial sum versus the critical path, and the budget verdict.

Code

# latency_budget_engine.py
# Section 14.04: Inline Latency Budget Allocation and P99 Measurement
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 14 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Dict


BUDGET_MS = 50.0  # Sub-50ms P99 hard constraint from Chapter 11


@dataclass
class InlineDetector:
    name: str
    allocation_ms: float
    execution: str  # 'serial' or 'parallel_group_a' etc.


@dataclass
class BudgetReport:
    total_allocation_ms: float
    critical_path_ms: float
    within_budget: bool
    headroom_ms: float


DETECTORS: List[InlineDetector] = [
    InlineDetector('Signature match', 3.0, 'parallel_group_a'),
    InlineDetector('Heuristic rules', 5.0, 'parallel_group_a'),
    InlineDetector('Injection classifier', 18.0, 'parallel_group_a'),
    InlineDetector('Jailbreak classifier', 16.0, 'parallel_group_a'),
    InlineDetector('Output PII scanner', 12.0, 'serial_post'),
]


def critical_path(detectors: List[InlineDetector]) -> float:
    '''The critical path is the sum of serial stages plus the max within each parallel group.

    Detectors in the same parallel group run concurrently, so the group costs the
    slowest member, not the sum. Serial stages add their full allocation.
    '''
    groups: Dict[str, List[float]] = {}
    serial_total = 0.0
    for d in detectors:
        if d.execution.startswith('parallel_'):
            groups.setdefault(d.execution, []).append(d.allocation_ms)
        else:
            serial_total += d.allocation_ms
    parallel_total = sum(max(v) for v in groups.values())
    return serial_total + parallel_total


def build_report(detectors: List[InlineDetector]) -> BudgetReport:
    total = sum(d.allocation_ms for d in detectors)
    cp = critical_path(detectors)
    return BudgetReport(total, cp, cp <= BUDGET_MS, BUDGET_MS - cp)


def print_report(detectors: List[InlineDetector]) -> None:
    print('=' * 78)
    print('INLINE LATENCY BUDGET ENGINE')
    print(f'Hard constraint: P99 <= {BUDGET_MS:.0f}ms (Chapter 11)')
    print('=' * 78)
    print()
    header = 'Detector'.ljust(24) + 'Alloc (ms)'.ljust(12) + 'Execution'.ljust(20)
    print(header)
    print('-' * 78)
    for d in detectors:
        print(f'{d.name:<24}{d.allocation_ms:<12.1f}{d.execution:<20}')
    print('-' * 78)
    r = build_report(detectors)
    print()
    print(f'Naive serial sum : {r.total_allocation_ms:.1f}ms')
    print(f'Critical path    : {r.critical_path_ms:.1f}ms (parallel groups cost their slowest member)')
    verdict = 'WITHIN BUDGET' if r.within_budget else 'OVER BUDGET'
    print(f'Verdict          : {verdict} (headroom {r.headroom_ms:.1f}ms)')
    print()
    if r.total_allocation_ms > BUDGET_MS and r.within_budget:
        print('Note: the serial sum exceeds the budget but parallel execution brings the')
        print('critical path under it. Parallelism is what makes the inline tier fit.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    print_report(DETECTORS)

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List


BUDGET_MS = 50.0
RESERVED_OVERHEAD_MS = 8.0  # gateway, network hop, serialisation -- not available to detectors


@dataclass
class Allocation:
    detector: str
    allocation_ms: float
    share_of_detector_budget: float
    rationale: str


def build_allocations(detector_budget_ms: float) -> List[Allocation]:
    # Detectors and their relative weights (heavier ML classifiers get more).
    raw = [
        ('Signature match', 1.0, 'Cheap exact and pattern match, near-constant time'),
        ('Heuristic rules', 1.5, 'Small rule set evaluated over the request'),
        ('Injection classifier', 6.0, 'Transformer-based input classifier, the heaviest inline check'),
        ('Jailbreak classifier', 5.0, 'Second input classifier, run in parallel with injection'),
        ('Output PII scanner', 3.0, 'Serial post-processing scan of the response'),
    ]
    weight_total = sum(w for _, w, _ in raw)
    allocations: List[Allocation] = []
    for name, w, reason in raw:
        share = w / weight_total
        allocations.append(Allocation(name, round(detector_budget_ms * share, 1), round(share * 100, 1), reason))
    return allocations


def print_table() -> None:
    detector_budget = BUDGET_MS - RESERVED_OVERHEAD_MS
    print('=' * 82)
    print('LATENCY BUDGET ALLOCATION TABLE')
    print(f'Total budget {BUDGET_MS:.0f}ms  -  reserved overhead {RESERVED_OVERHEAD_MS:.0f}ms '
          f'=  {detector_budget:.0f}ms for detectors')
    print('=' * 82)
    allocations = build_allocations(detector_budget)
    header = 'Detector'.ljust(24) + 'Alloc (ms)'.ljust(13) + 'Share'.ljust(10) + 'Rationale'
    print(header)
    print('-' * 82)
    for a in allocations:
        line = a.detector.ljust(24) + f'{a.allocation_ms:.1f}'.ljust(13) + f'{a.share_of_detector_budget:.1f}%'.ljust(10) + a.rationale
        print(line)
    print('-' * 82)
    allocated = sum(a.allocation_ms for a in allocations)
    print(f'Allocated to detectors: {allocated:.1f}ms of {detector_budget:.0f}ms available')
    print()
    print('The reserved overhead is spent before any detector runs -- the network hop,')
    print('gateway processing, and serialisation. Only what remains is the detector budget,')
    print('and every inline detector must fit its allocation.')

Percentile Latency Measurement Harness

Filename

percentile_latency_harness.py

Description

Measures P50, P95, and P99 from a simulated latency distribution with a tight body and a heavy tail, and contrasts the healthy-looking mean with the P99 at the tail to show why budgeting on the average hides the slow requests users notice. Reports pass or fail against the sub-50ms budget at the P99.

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention, standard-library random only, prints the percentile summary and the pass/fail verdict at the tail.

Code

# percentile_latency_harness.py
# Section 14.04: Percentile Latency Measurement Under Load
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 14 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import List


@dataclass
class LatencyStats:
    p50: float
    p95: float
    p99: float
    mean: float
    max_ms: float
    count: int


def percentile(sorted_samples: List[float], pct: float) -> float:
    '''Nearest-rank percentile on an already-sorted list.

    Percentiles are read at the tail, not the average, because the slowest
    requests are the ones users notice. P99 is the value at or below which
    99 per cent of requests complete.
    '''
    if not sorted_samples:
        return 0.0
    k = max(0, min(len(sorted_samples) - 1, int(round(pct / 100.0 * len(sorted_samples))) - 1))
    return sorted_samples[k]


def summarise(samples: List[float]) -> LatencyStats:
    s = sorted(samples)
    mean = sum(s) / len(s) if s else 0.0
    return LatencyStats(
        p50=percentile(s, 50),
        p95=percentile(s, 95),
        p99=percentile(s, 99),
        mean=round(mean, 2),
        max_ms=s[-1] if s else 0.0,
        count=len(s),
    )


def simulate_load(n: int, seed: int = 14) -> List[float]:
    '''Generate a realistic latency distribution: a tight body with a heavy tail.

    Most requests are fast, but a small fraction are much slower (garbage
    collection pauses, cache misses, contention). The heavy tail is exactly why
    the average hides the problem and the P99 exposes it.
    '''
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    samples: List[float] = []
    for _ in range(n):
        if rng.random() < 0.97:
            samples.append(rng.uniform(18.0, 34.0))   # fast body
        else:
            samples.append(rng.uniform(48.0, 95.0))   # heavy tail
    return samples


def print_stats(stats: LatencyStats, budget_ms: float = 50.0) -> None:
    print('=' * 78)
    print('PERCENTILE LATENCY MEASUREMENT HARNESS')
    print(f'Samples: {stats.count}  |  Budget: P99 <= {budget_ms:.0f}ms')
    print('=' * 78)
    print(f'  Mean : {stats.mean:.1f}ms   <- the average looks fine and hides the tail')
    print(f'  P50  : {stats.p50:.1f}ms')
    print(f'  P95  : {stats.p95:.1f}ms')
    print(f'  P99  : {stats.p99:.1f}ms   <- this is the number that governs the budget')
    print(f'  Max  : {stats.max_ms:.1f}ms')
    print()
    if stats.p99 <= budget_ms:
        print(f'PASS: P99 {stats.p99:.1f}ms is within the {budget_ms:.0f}ms budget')
    else:
        print(f'FAIL: P99 {stats.p99:.1f}ms exceeds the {budget_ms:.0f}ms budget despite a healthy mean')
    print()
    print('The mean is well under budget while the P99 is at the tail. Budgeting on the')
    print('mean would have declared this system healthy while 1 in 100 users saw a stall.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    samples = simulate_load(10000)
    print_stats(summarise(samples))

Code

# detector_timeout_config.py
# Section 14.04: Per-Detector Timeouts for Worst-Case Bounding
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 14 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import time
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Callable, List


@dataclass
class TimeoutConfig:
    detector: str
    allocation_ms: float
    timeout_ms: float
    on_timeout_fail_open: bool


@dataclass
class TimeoutResult:
    detector: str
    elapsed_ms: float
    timed_out: bool
    decision: str


def run_with_timeout(cfg: TimeoutConfig, detector_fn: Callable[[], bool]) -> TimeoutResult:
    '''Run a detector and bound its worst case at the timeout.

    A detector without a timeout is unbounded: one slow call can blow the whole
    latency budget. The timeout converts an unbounded slow detector into a bounded
    one that fails within the budget. On timeout a fail-open/fail-closed decision
    is made -- the full per-detector policy is Section 14.06; here the timeout only
    guarantees the worst case is bounded.
    '''
    start = time.perf_counter()
    budget_s = cfg.timeout_ms / 1000.0
    flagged = False
    timed_out = False
    # Cooperative timeout check: the detector reports progress and we stop at the bound.
    # In production this is enforced by a real deadline; here we measure and compare.
    flagged = detector_fn()
    elapsed_ms = (time.perf_counter() - start) * 1000.0
    if elapsed_ms > cfg.timeout_ms:
        timed_out = True
        if cfg.on_timeout_fail_open:
            decision = 'timed out -> fail-open (admit)'
        else:
            decision = 'timed out -> fail-closed (block)'
    else:
        decision = 'flagged -> block' if flagged else 'clean -> admit'
    return TimeoutResult(cfg.detector, round(elapsed_ms, 2), timed_out, decision)


def fast_detector() -> bool:
    time.sleep(0.002)
    return False


def slow_detector() -> bool:
    time.sleep(0.040)  # 40ms -- exceeds a 20ms timeout
    return False

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Budgeting and reporting latency on the average instead of the tail percentile

Why It Happens

The mean is the most familiar summary and the easiest to compute, and on a quiet test system it looks reassuringly low, so it is reported as the latency of the system without the tail ever being examined.

Consequence

The system is declared healthy on a mean that is well within budget while its P99 is over budget, so one request in a hundred stalls and a large fraction of users hit at least one stall that the average never revealed.

Correct Approach

Budget and measure at the tail: read the P99 under realistic load and treat it as the number the budget is stated at, using the mean only as a sanity check on the body, never as the budget.

Mistake 2

Description

Summing inline detector latencies serially and concluding the tier cannot fit the budget

Why It Happens

The serial sum is the obvious first calculation, and when it exceeds the budget it looks like a hard verdict that the tier is too heavy, without considering that independent detectors need not run one after another.

Consequence

Strong detectors are dropped from the inline tier or moved to a slower tier unnecessarily, because the serial sum was treated as the cost when the critical path under parallel execution would have fit the budget with headroom.

Correct Approach

Compute the critical path, not the serial sum: run independent detectors in parallel so a group costs its slowest member, and only the critical path -- serial stages plus the max of each parallel group -- has to fit the budget.

Mistake 3

Description

Running detectors inline without per-detector timeouts, leaving the worst case unbounded

Why It Happens

A detector that is fast in the median looks safe, and the occasional slow request is easy to overlook because it does not show up in the median or the mean, only in the tail.

Consequence

An occasional slow call on a bad request lands squarely in the P99 and pushes it over budget even though the median is tiny, and with no timeout the slow call adds its full unbounded duration to the request.

Correct Approach

Put a per-detector timeout on every inline detector so the worst case is bounded at the timeout, turning a budget-blowing slow call into a bounded failure, and make a deliberate fail-open or fail-closed decision on timeout.

Mistake 4

Description

Trying to force a large-state detector inline by optimisation because its verdict is valuable

Why It Happens

The detector produces a strong signal, so there is pressure to have it on the hot path where it can block, and it feels like enough parallelism or caching should eventually bring any detector under the budget.

Consequence

Effort is spent trying to shrink a cost that is fundamentally tied to a large and growing state, the detector either misses the budget anyway or is weakened into a different detector to fit, and the inline tier is destabilised by a check that never belonged there.

Correct Approach

Respect the state-latency coupling made quantitative by the budget: keep large-state detectors on the near-real-time or batch tier where their unavoidable cost is paid off the hot path, and use precomputed summaries inline only when a refreshed summary genuinely suffices.

Mistake 5

Description

Validating the latency budget by reasoning or on a quiet system instead of load-testing at percentiles

Why It Happens

Load testing is more work than reasoning about allocations, and a quick check on a quiet system shows low latency, so the budget is assumed to hold without ever being driven at production load.

Consequence

The tail that only appears under contention is never produced in testing, the budget looks satisfied, and the P99 blows past the limit the first time production load creates the garbage-collection pauses, cache misses, and contention that generate the tail.

Correct Approach

Load-test the budget before production: drive the gateway at the request rate it will actually see, measure P50, P95, and P99 under that load with timeouts in place, and confirm the P99 is within budget, because only realistic load produces the tail the budget is about.

Section Summary

Compact

The sub-50ms P99 latency budget from Chapter 11 is a hard constraint, not a target: a detector that pushes the gateway past it is removed for degrading the API, so a too-slow detector provides no protection and the budget decides what may run inline at all. The budget is stated at the tail because tail latency governs the experience -- the latency distribution is a tight body with a heavy tail, the average is dominated by the body and hides the tail, and users notice the slow requests one at a time, so the P99 measured under load is the number that matters. The tier budget, minus the fixed overhead spent before any detector runs, is allocated across the inline detectors so each has a share it must fit. The tier is kept within budget by optimisation: parallel execution makes an independent group cost its slowest member rather than the sum, so the critical path rather than the serial sum must fit, and precomputed summaries and caching move cost off the hot path. Large-state detectors still cannot fit, which is the Chapter 13 state-latency coupling made quantitative, so they run on the off-path tiers where there is no budget to blow. Per-detector timeouts bound the worst case, turning an unbounded slow detector into a bounded one that fails within the budget at the cost of a fail-open/fail-closed decision on timeout, and the whole budget is load-tested at percentiles before production because only realistic load produces the tail.

Key Takeaways

  • The sub-50ms P99 budget is a hard constraint, not a target: a detector removed for being slow provides zero protection, so the budget governs what can run inline at all
  • Tail latency governs the user experience, so the budget is read at the P99 measured under load, never at the average, which hides the heavy tail
  • The tier budget minus fixed overhead is allocated across the inline detectors, and each detector must fit its allocation
  • Parallel execution means a group costs its slowest member, so the critical path rather than the serial sum must fit the budget, and precomputation and caching move cost off the hot path
  • Large-state detectors cannot fit the inline budget -- the state-latency coupling made quantitative -- so they run on the off-path tiers where their cost is paid without a request budget
  • Per-detector timeouts bound the worst case at the cost of a fail-open/fail-closed decision on timeout, and the budget is load-tested at percentiles before production because only realistic load produces the tail
