Detection Primitives: Input Preprocessing and Feature Squeezing

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Feature squeezing detects adversarial examples through their sensitivity to input transformation: the model prediction changes significantly when a squeezer is applied to adversarial inputs but remains stable for natural inputs.

Why This Section Exists

Sections 10.3-10.9 built the attack catalogue. Section 10.10 grounded it in documented production incidents. Sections 10.11-10.12 build the Track 7 detection primitive library that the Section 10.13 gateway uses to defend against those attacks. Section 10.11 covers the input-side primitives: transformations applied to the input before or alongside model inference that reveal adversarial perturbations through prediction instability. Feature squeezing (Xu et al. 2018) is the foundational input-side primitive: three canonical squeezers (spatial smoothing, bit-depth reduction, JPEG compression), combined in an ensemble with disagreement as the detection signal. The latency budget calculation that determines how many squeezers fit within the 50ms P99 gateway budget is the critical engineering decision Section 10.13 inherits from this section.

Position In Chapter

Section 10.11 opens the Track 7 detection primitive library. It covers the pre-inference stage: transformations that must complete before the main model output is used. Section 10.12 covers the post-inference stage: consistency checking and adversarial classifier that operate on the model''s own output. Section 10.13 wires both stages into the production gateway.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

Detection by Transformation: The Asymmetry Between Natural and Adversarial Inputs

Body

The foundation of feature squeezing is a fundamental asymmetry between natural images and adversarial examples in how they respond to input transformation. A natural image of a cat remains recognisable as a cat after mild Gaussian blurring. The classifier that correctly identifies the original also correctly identifies the blurred version, because the semantically meaningful features (edges, textures, shapes) survive mild smoothing. The prediction may shift slightly in probability, but the argmax class remains the same. An adversarial example -- say, a cat image perturbed by FGSM to be classified as a toaster -- has been modified by a precisely calibrated perturbation. That perturbation exists at a specific frequency range (typically high-frequency fine-grained noise) and with a specific magnitude (exactly epsilon per pixel). Gaussian blurring changes the high-frequency components of the input, which are exactly the components that carry the adversarial signal. The perturbation precision is destroyed; the adversarial example, once blurred, may no longer fool the classifier and reverts to being classified as a cat. This asymmetry -- natural images are robust to mild transformation, adversarial examples are not -- is the detection signal. Feature squeezing (Xu et al. 2018) formalises it as a detection algorithm: apply a transformation ("squeezer") to the input, query the model on both the original and the squeezed version, and flag the input if the predictions disagree beyond a threshold. A natural input produces stable predictions; an adversarial input produces unstable predictions that shift significantly under the squeezer transformation. The asymmetry is not absolute: adversarial examples with very large perturbation budgets may survive mild squeezing, and natural images near the decision boundary may produce unstable predictions under squeezing (false positives). Threshold calibration and squeezer selection must account for both failure modes. But for the vast majority of adversarial examples in the practical epsilon range (4/255 to 8/255 for images), the asymmetry is strong enough to provide meaningful detection coverage.

Part 2

Title

The Three Canonical Squeezers: Spatial Smoothing, Bit-Depth, and JPEG

Body

Xu et al. 2018 identified three input transformation types that exploit different aspects of
the adversarial perturbation structure. Each squeezer targets a different adversarial attack
class and has different false positive and false negative characteristics.

Spatial smoothing applies a blurring filter to the input. Gaussian blur weights nearby pixels
by a Gaussian kernel centred at each pixel -- each output pixel is a weighted average of its
neighbourhood, with closer pixels weighted more. Median filtering replaces each pixel with the
median value of its neighbourhood, which is particularly effective against salt-and-pepper
noise (isolated pixels that differ sharply from their neighbours). Adversarial perturbations
from FGSM and PGD are distributed uniformly across all pixels with small per-pixel magnitude
-- exactly the pattern that Gaussian smoothing reduces. Spatial smoothing is most effective
against L-infinity adversarial examples (FGSM, PGD) and least effective against L0 sparse
adversarial examples (which modify few pixels significantly).

Bit-depth reduction rounds pixel values to fewer bits. An 8-bit image has 256 possible values
per channel; reducing to 5 bits gives 32 possible values. Adversarial perturbations rely on
precise per-pixel values: an epsilon=4/255 perturbation changes a pixel by approximately 0.016
on the normalised [0,1] scale. Rounding to 5 bits (step size ~0.032) destroys perturbations
smaller than the rounding step. This makes bit-depth reduction most effective against fine-
grained perturbations (small epsilon FGSM) and less effective against larger perturbations.

JPEG compression applies lossy block-based discrete cosine transform (DCT) quantisation.
The JPEG quality parameter controls the quantisation step size: low quality (high compression)
removes more high-frequency information, destroying fine-grained adversarial perturbations at
the cost of visible image quality degradation. Unlike spatial smoothing (which operates
uniformly) and bit-depth reduction (which operates per-pixel), JPEG operates in frequency
domain blocks and removes structured high-frequency patterns more aggressively. It is most
effective against perturbations with periodic or structured spatial patterns.

Part 3

Title

Squeezer Detection Logic: Threshold, Metric, and Calibration

Body

The squeezer detection score for a single squeezer S applied to input x is: score(x) = d(P(y | x), P(y | S(x))) where P(y | x) is the model''s probability vector on the original input, P(y | S(x)) is the probability vector on the squeezed input, and d is a distance metric. Two choices of d are common: L1 distance (sum of absolute differences across all classes) and L-infinity distance (maximum absolute difference across all classes). L1 is more sensitive to aggregate prediction shift; L-infinity is more sensitive to the maximum class probability change. A natural input produces score(x) near zero: the model''s prediction is approximately the same on the original and the squeezed version. An adversarial input produces a higher score: the squeezer destroys the adversarial perturbation, and the model''s prediction changes. The detection decision is: flag if score(x) > tau, where tau is a threshold calibrated to achieve the desired false positive rate on natural inputs. Threshold calibration requires a held-out set of natural inputs and a set of adversarial inputs generated at the specific epsilon value and attack method the deployment faces. The ROC curve (true positive rate vs false positive rate as tau varies) determines the operating point. For production deployments, a false positive rate of 1-5% is typically acceptable; the threshold is set to achieve this rate on the natural input held-out set. One calibration failure mode deserves explicit attention: natural inputs near decision boundaries produce high squeezer scores even without adversarial perturbation. A benign image that is borderline between two classes (classified as dog with 55% confidence and cat with 44%) may flip classification under mild squeezing. These natural hard examples generate false positives that are not adversarial. Calibration must be done on a dataset that includes hard natural examples, not just easily classified ones.

Part 4

Title

Squeezer Ensemble: Coverage, Disagreement, and Ensemble Logic

Body

No single squeezer covers all adversarial attack families. Spatial smoothing (Gaussian) catches FGSM and PGD effectively but is less effective against C&W minimum-norm examples (which may concentrate perturbation in spatial patterns that survive blurring). Bit-depth reduction catches fine-grained perturbations but misses large sparse perturbations (L0 attacks). JPEG compression catches periodic structured perturbations but may not catch sparse random ones. A squeezer ensemble runs multiple squeezers and flags an input if any squeezer produces a score above its threshold. The ensemble detection score is: max_i(score_i(x)) where score_i is the detection score for squeezer i. An input that passes all individual squeezers passes the ensemble; an input that fails any single squeezer is flagged. The ensemble increases coverage because different squeezers have complementary coverage: an adversarial example that survives Gaussian smoothing (perhaps because the perturbation is structured to avoid low-frequency components) may not survive bit-depth reduction (which destroys the fine per-pixel precision). The ensemble''s coverage is the union of individual squeezer coverages, not just the coverage of the best single squeezer. The ensemble also increases the cost for adaptive adversaries. An adversary optimising against a single squeezer crafts examples that survive that squeezer''s specific transformation. An adversary optimising against an ensemble must craft examples that simultaneously survive all squeezers -- a more constrained optimisation problem that typically requires larger perturbation or lower attack success rate. The adaptive attack cost scales with ensemble diversity, not just ensemble size: three diverse squeezers (spatial, bit-depth, frequency domain) are harder to jointly evade than three similar squeezers (three Gaussian blurs with different sigma values).

Part 5

Title

Adaptive Attacks Against Squeezing: The Limits of Single-Squeezer Defences

Body

The adaptive adversary argument (first made systematically in Carlini and Wagner 2017 and applied to squeezing defences in Sharma and Ding 2018) shows that any single squeezer can be defeated by an adversary who knows the squeezer and optimises through it. For a Gaussian squeezer S, the adaptive attack generates adversarial examples against the composed function S(model(x)) rather than model(x) alone. The C&W objective becomes: minimise ||delta||_2 + c * g(S(x + delta)) where g evaluates misclassification on the squeezed input. The resulting adversarial example misclassifies even after squeezing -- it is adversarial both before and after the squeezer is applied, so the squeezer score is near zero despite the example being adversarial. The Section 10.5 defence_evaluator_cw.py deliverable implements exactly this attack. The practical implication: feature squeezing alone cannot be the complete Track 7 detection layer. An adaptive adversary with knowledge of the squeezer configuration can craft examples that evade all squeezers while remaining adversarial. The squeezer ensemble provides meaningful resistance by increasing the number of constraints the adversary must satisfy simultaneously, but it does not provide formal guarantees. Section 10.12 adds two additional detection primitives -- consistency checking and the adversarial detector classifier -- that are harder to jointly evade with squeezing. Section 10.13 assembles all three into the three-primitive Track 7 ensemble. The honest framing for production: the squeezer ensemble provides high coverage against non-adaptive adversaries and moderate resistance against adaptive adversaries; formal guarantees require randomised smoothing (Section 10.14).

Part 6

Title

Text Preprocessing Analogues: Canonicalisation and Unicode Normalisation

Body

The image squeezer concept translates to text via preprocessing transformations that reduce the text''s feature space while preserving semantic content. Section 10.9 described character-level attacks (homoglyphs, zero-width characters) that exploit preprocessing gaps. Text canonicalisation squeezers close those gaps. Unicode canonicalisation applies NFC or NFKC normalisation, which collapses compatibility equivalents (full-width ASCII characters to their standard ASCII equivalents, ligatures to their component characters). NFKC is more aggressive than NFC and handles more encoding variants. Homoglyph normalisation goes further: it replaces visually similar cross-script characters (Cyrillic а → Latin a) using the Unicode confusables database. Zero-width character removal strips all invisible codepoints (U+200B, U+200C, U+200D, U+2060). The text squeezer detection logic follows the same pattern as image squeezing: apply the canonicalisation, run the classifier on both the original and the canonicalised version, flag if predictions disagree. An input containing homoglyphs or zero-width characters that was adversarially crafted to fool the text classifier on the non-normalised version will classify correctly after canonicalisation -- the disagreement is the detection signal. Synonym normalisation is a softer text squeezer: replace words with their most common synonym (using a standardised synonym dictionary) and check prediction stability. This catches TextFooler-style attacks where the adversary substituted less-common synonyms to evade the classifier. The limitation is that synonym normalisation may change the semantics of specialised text (technical documents, proper nouns) producing false positives.

Part 7

Title

Latency Budget: Squeezer Count, Parallelisation, and the 50ms P99 Constraint

Body

The critical engineering constraint for squeezer deployment is latency. Each squeezer requires one additional model forward pass (on the squeezed input), which is as expensive as the primary model inference. Running three squeezers sequentially triples inference latency. Within the 50ms P99 budget the gateway must respect, this may not be feasible. The correct architecture is parallel squeezer execution. The primary model inference and all squeezer model inferences run concurrently using async dispatch (torch.futures, asyncio with thread pool, or multi-process inference). The gateway waits for all parallel inferences to complete, compares predictions, and makes the detection decision. If all inferences complete in approximately the same time, the latency overhead is one model forward pass (the time for the slowest squeezer), not N model forward passes for N squeezers. The practical latency calculation: for a primary model with P99 inference latency of 20ms, and each squeezer adding 1ms of transformation time plus the same 20ms of model inference time, parallel execution completes in approximately 21ms total (20ms inference + 1ms transformation overhead). Sequential execution would take 60ms for two squeezers plus 20ms for the primary -- 80ms total, well over the 50ms budget. The squeezer_latency_benchmark.py deliverable measures per-squeezer latency under load and produces a recommendation for maximum ensemble size given the primary model''s inference latency. The Section 10.13 gateway uses this recommendation to configure the Track 7 pre-inference stage. For models where inference is very expensive (large transformers, multi-modal models), limiting the squeezer ensemble to one or two squeezers and relying more heavily on the post-inference consistency checker (Section 10.12) is the correct latency-aware architectural choice.

Feature Squeezer Library

Filename

feature_squeezer.py

Description

Gaussian blur, median filter, bit-depth reduction, and JPEG-approximate squeezers with squeezer detection score (L1 distance between original and squeezed prediction vectors) and threshold-based detection decision

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires: torch, torch.nn.functional. JPEG squeezer approximated via downsample/upsample (avoids PIL dependency; real production should use PIL JPEG encode/decode for accuracy). Import ToyLinear from fgsm_attack.py for demonstration.

Code

# feature_squeezer.py
# Section 10.11: Feature Squeezer Library
# 20260524 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention
# Reference: Xu et al. 2018 "Feature Squeezing: Detecting Adversarial Examples"

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch import Tensor
from typing import Callable, Tuple
from dataclasses import dataclass


# ---- Squeezer transformation functions ----

def gaussian_squeeze(x: Tensor, sigma: float = 1.0, kernel_size: int = 5) -> Tensor:
    '''Gaussian blur: reduces high-frequency adversarial noise.'''
    half = kernel_size // 2
    coords = torch.arange(-half, half + 1, dtype=torch.float32)
    k1d = torch.exp(-coords ** 2 / (2.0 * sigma ** 2))
    k1d = k1d / k1d.sum()
    k2d = k1d.unsqueeze(0) * k1d.unsqueeze(1)  # (k, k)
    k2d = k2d.unsqueeze(0).unsqueeze(0)         # (1, 1, k, k)
    n_ch = x.size(1)
    kernel = k2d.expand(n_ch, 1, kernel_size, kernel_size)
    return F.conv2d(x, kernel, padding=half, groups=n_ch).clamp(0.0, 1.0)


def median_squeeze(x: Tensor, window: int = 3) -> Tensor:
    '''Median filter: effective against salt-and-pepper and isolated pixel attacks.'''
    half = window // 2
    padded = F.pad(x, [half, half, half, half], mode='reflect')
    batch, channels, h, w = x.shape
    result = torch.zeros_like(x)
    for i in range(h):
        for j in range(w):
            patch = padded[:, :, i:i + window, j:j + window]
            result[:, :, i, j] = patch.reshape(batch, channels, -1).median(dim=2).values
    return result.clamp(0.0, 1.0)


def bit_depth_squeeze(x: Tensor, bits: int = 5) -> Tensor:
    '''Bit-depth reduction: destroys fine-grained perturbation precision.'''
    levels = (2 ** bits) - 1.0
    return (x * levels).round() / levels


def jpeg_squeeze(x: Tensor, scale: float = 0.5) -> Tensor:
    '''
    JPEG-approximate squeezer via downsample + upsample (bilinear).
    Approximates JPEG block quantisation without PIL dependency.
    For production accuracy: use PIL JPEG encode at target quality.
    '''
    h, w = x.size(-2), x.size(-1)
    downscaled = F.interpolate(x, scale_factor=scale, mode='bilinear',
                               align_corners=False)
    return F.interpolate(downscaled, size=(h, w), mode='bilinear',
                         align_corners=False).clamp(0.0, 1.0)


# ---- Detection logic ----

def squeezer_detection_score(
    model: nn.Module,
    x: Tensor,
    squeeze_fn: Callable[[Tensor], Tensor],
    metric: str = 'l1',
) -> Tensor:
    '''
    Per-sample detection score: distance between prediction vectors on
    original vs squeezed input. Higher score = more likely adversarial.
    metric: 'l1' (sum of abs differences) or 'linf' (max abs difference).
    '''
    with torch.no_grad():
        p_orig = F.softmax(model(x), dim=1)
        p_squeezed = F.softmax(model(squeeze_fn(x)), dim=1)
    diff = (p_orig - p_squeezed).abs()
    if metric == 'linf':
        return diff.max(dim=1).values
    return diff.sum(dim=1)  # L1 default


def detect_adversarial(
    model: nn.Module,
    x: Tensor,
    squeeze_fn: Callable[[Tensor], Tensor],
    threshold: float = 0.10,
    metric: str = 'l1',
) -> Tuple[Tensor, Tensor]:
    '''
    Returns (detection_decision, score_per_sample).
    detection_decision: bool tensor, True = adversarial flagged.
    '''
    scores = squeezer_detection_score(model, x, squeeze_fn, metric)
    return scores > threshold, scores


@dataclass
class SqueezerConfig:
    name: str
    fn: Callable
    threshold: float = 0.10
    metric: str = 'l1'


def build_default_squeezers() -> list:
    return [
        SqueezerConfig('gaussian_s1', lambda x: gaussian_squeeze(x, sigma=1.0)),
        SqueezerConfig('gaussian_s0.5', lambda x: gaussian_squeeze(x, sigma=0.5)),
        SqueezerConfig('bit_depth_5', lambda x: bit_depth_squeeze(x, bits=5)),
        SqueezerConfig('jpeg_approx', lambda x: jpeg_squeeze(x, scale=0.5)),
    ]

Squeezer Ensemble with Disagreement Scoring

Filename

squeezer_ensemble.py

Description

Multi-squeezer ensemble with maximum-disagreement and mean-disagreement scoring modes; threshold calibration from a held-out natural image set; ensemble detection rate comparison across attack families

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires: torch. Import from feature_squeezer.py and fgsm_attack.py.

Code

# squeezer_ensemble.py
# Section 10.11: Squeezer Ensemble with Disagreement Scoring
# 20260524 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch import Tensor
from typing import List, Tuple
from feature_squeezer import SqueezerConfig, squeezer_detection_score, build_default_squeezers


def ensemble_score(
    model,
    x: Tensor,
    squeezers: List[SqueezerConfig],
    mode: str = 'max',
) -> Tensor:
    '''
    Ensemble disagreement score.
    mode='max': flag if ANY squeezer shows high disagreement (union coverage).
    mode='mean': average disagreement across squeezers.
    '''
    scores = torch.stack([
        squeezer_detection_score(model, x, cfg.fn, cfg.metric)
        for cfg in squeezers
    ], dim=0)  # (n_squeezers, batch)

    if mode == 'max':
        return scores.max(dim=0).values
    return scores.mean(dim=0)


def calibrate_threshold(
    model,
    x_natural: Tensor,
    squeezers: List[SqueezerConfig],
    target_fpr: float = 0.05,
    mode: str = 'max',
) -> float:
    '''
    Set ensemble threshold to achieve target false positive rate on natural inputs.
    Returns threshold value.
    '''
    scores = ensemble_score(model, x_natural, squeezers, mode)
    # Find quantile such that (1 - target_fpr) of natural scores fall below threshold
    threshold = scores.quantile(1.0 - target_fpr).item()
    return threshold


def ensemble_detect(
    model,
    x: Tensor,
    squeezers: List[SqueezerConfig],
    threshold: float,
    mode: str = 'max',
) -> Tuple[Tensor, Tensor]:
    '''Returns (flagged: bool tensor, scores: float tensor).'''
    scores = ensemble_score(model, x, squeezers, mode)
    return scores > threshold, scores


def per_squeezer_breakdown(
    model,
    x_adv: Tensor,
    y_true: Tensor,
    squeezers: List[SqueezerConfig],
) -> None:
    '''Show which squeezers contribute to detection.'''
    print(f'{"Squeezer":<24} {"Mean score":<14} {"Detect @ thresh":<18}')
    print('-' * 58)
    for cfg in squeezers:
        scores = squeezer_detection_score(model, x_adv, cfg.fn, cfg.metric)
        mean_s = scores.mean().item()
        flagged = (scores > cfg.threshold).float().mean().item()
        print(f'{cfg.name:<24} {mean_s:<14.4f} {flagged:<18.3f}')


def demo() -> None:
    try:
        from fgsm_attack import ToyLinear, fgsm
        from pgd_attack import pgd_linf
    except ImportError:
        print('Run from directory containing fgsm_attack.py and pgd_attack.py')
        return

    torch.manual_seed(42)
    model = ToyLinear()
    model.eval()
    x_nat = torch.rand(64, 1, 28, 28)
    y = torch.randint(0, 10, (64,))
    eps = 8.0 / 255.0
    x_fgsm = fgsm(model, x_nat, y, epsilon=eps)
    x_pgd = pgd_linf(model, x_nat, y, eps, eps / 10.0, n_steps=20, n_restarts=1)

    squeezers = build_default_squeezers()

    # Calibrate threshold for 5% FPR on natural images
    threshold = calibrate_threshold(model, x_nat, squeezers, target_fpr=0.05)
    print(f'Calibrated ensemble threshold (5% FPR): {threshold:.4f}')

    for label, x_test in [('Natural', x_nat), ('FGSM', x_fgsm), ('PGD-20', x_pgd)]:

Squeezer Latency Benchmark

Filename

squeezer_latency_benchmark.py

Description

Measures per-squeezer transformation latency and model forward pass latency under load; determines maximum ensemble size within the 50ms P99 gateway budget; produces parallelisation recommendation

Implementation Notes

Code

# squeezer_latency_benchmark.py
# Section 10.11: Squeezer Latency Benchmark for Track 7 Budget
# 20260524 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import torch
import time
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import List, Callable
from feature_squeezer import SqueezerConfig, build_default_squeezers


N_WARMUP = 5
N_RUNS = 20
GATEWAY_BUDGET_MS = 50.0


@dataclass
class LatencyResult:
    name: str
    transform_ms: float
    model_ms: float
    total_ms: float


def measure_latency(fn: Callable, n_warmup: int = N_WARMUP, n_runs: int = N_RUNS) -> float:
    '''Measure mean latency of fn() in milliseconds.'''
    for _ in range(n_warmup):
        fn()
    times = []
    for _ in range(n_runs):
        start = time.perf_counter()
        fn()
        times.append((time.perf_counter() - start) * 1000.0)
    return sum(times) / len(times)


def benchmark_squeezers(
    model,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    squeezers: List[SqueezerConfig],
) -> List[LatencyResult]:
    results = []
    for cfg in squeezers:
        # Transform latency (no model)
        transform_ms = measure_latency(lambda: cfg.fn(x))

        # Model forward pass on squeezed input
        x_sq = cfg.fn(x)
        model_ms = measure_latency(lambda: model(x_sq))

        total_ms = transform_ms + model_ms
        results.append(LatencyResult(cfg.name, transform_ms, model_ms, total_ms))

    return results


def primary_model_latency(model, x: torch.Tensor) -> float:
    return measure_latency(lambda: model(x))


def sequential_ensemble_latency(results: List[LatencyResult]) -> float:
    return sum(r.total_ms for r in results)


def parallel_ensemble_latency(
    model,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    squeezers: List[SqueezerConfig],
    primary_ms: float,
) -> float:
    '''Simulate parallel execution: latency = max(primary, max squeezer).'''
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=len(squeezers) + 1) as pool:
        futures = [pool.submit(lambda cfg=cfg: model(cfg.fn(x))) for cfg in squeezers]
        start = time.perf_counter()
        for f in futures:
            f.result()
        parallel_ms = (time.perf_counter() - start) * 1000.0
    # Total = max(primary_ms, parallel squeezer time)
    return max(primary_ms, parallel_ms)


def print_benchmark_report(
    primary_ms: float,
    results: List[LatencyResult],
    budget_ms: float = GATEWAY_BUDGET_MS,
) -> None:
    print(f'Primary model inference: {primary_ms:.2f} ms')
    print(f'Gateway P99 budget:      {budget_ms:.1f} ms')
    print(f'Remaining for Track 7:   {budget_ms - primary_ms:.2f} ms')
    print()
    print(f'{"Squeezer":<22} {"transform_ms":<16} {"model_ms":<12} {"total_ms":<12} {"fits budget?"}')
    print('-' * 72)
    for r in results:
        fits = (primary_ms + r.total_ms) <= budget_ms
        print(f'{r.name:<22} {r.transform_ms:<16.2f} {r.model_ms:<12.2f} '
              f'{r.total_ms:<12.2f} {"YES (parallel)" if fits else "NO sequential"}')

    seq_total = primary_ms + sequential_ensemble_latency(results)
    print(f'\nSequential ensemble total: {seq_total:.2f} ms '
          f'({"within" if seq_total <= budget_ms else "EXCEEDS"} {budget_ms:.0f}ms budget)')

    # Max squeezers within budget (parallel: cost = max squeezer, not sum)
    max_single = max(r.total_ms for r in results)
    parallel_total = primary_ms + max_single
    print(f'Parallel ensemble total:   {parallel_total:.2f} ms '
          f'({"within" if parallel_total <= budget_ms else "EXCEEDS"} {budget_ms:.0f}ms budget)')
    print()
    print('Recommendation: run all squeezers in parallel with primary inference.')
    print('Latency overhead = transform time only (~1-3ms); model passes overlap.')

import unicodedata
from typing import Callable, List, Tuple


# Zero-width codepoints to strip
ZERO_WIDTH_CODEPOINTS = {0x200B, 0x200C, 0x200D, 0x2060, 0xFEFF, 0x00AD}

# Minimal confusable map: common cross-script lookalikes -> canonical ASCII
CONFUSABLE_MAP = {
    '\u0430': 'a',   # Cyrillic a -> Latin a
    '\u0435': 'e',   # Cyrillic ie -> Latin e
    '\u043e': 'o',   # Cyrillic o -> Latin o
    '\u0440': 'p',   # Cyrillic er -> Latin p
    '\u0441': 'c',   # Cyrillic es -> Latin c
    '\u0445': 'x',   # Cyrillic ha -> Latin x
    '\u0456': 'i',   # Cyrillic i -> Latin i
    '\u0455': 's',   # Cyrillic dze -> Latin s
    '\u0391': 'A',   # Greek alpha -> Latin A
    '\u0392': 'B',   # Greek beta -> Latin B
    '\u0395': 'E',   # Greek epsilon -> Latin E
    '\u0397': 'H',   # Greek eta -> Latin H
    '\u039a': 'K',   # Greek kappa -> Latin K
    '\u039c': 'M',   # Greek mu -> Latin M
    '\u039d': 'N',   # Greek nu -> Latin N
    '\u039f': 'O',   # Greek omicron -> Latin O
    '\u03a4': 'T',   # Greek tau -> Latin T
}


def remove_zero_width(text: str) -> str:
    '''Strip all zero-width and invisible Unicode codepoints.'''
    return ''.join(c for c in text if ord(c) not in ZERO_WIDTH_CODEPOINTS)


def normalise_unicode(text: str, form: str = 'NFKC') -> str:
    '''Apply Unicode normalisation (NFC, NFD, NFKC, or NFKD).'''
    return unicodedata.normalize(form, text)


def replace_confusables(text: str) -> str:
    '''Replace known cross-script confusable characters with their canonical ASCII equivalents.'''
    return ''.join(CONFUSABLE_MAP.get(c, c) for c in text)


def full_canonicalise(text: str) -> str:
    '''
    Full text canonicalisation pipeline:
    1. Unicode NFKC normalisation
    2. Zero-width character removal
    3. Confusable character replacement
    '''
    text = normalise_unicode(text, form='NFKC')
    text = remove_zero_width(text)
    text = replace_confusables(text)
    return text


def text_squeezer_score(
    model,
    text: str,
    canonicalise_fn: Callable[[str], str],
    max_len: int = 64,
) -> float:
    '''
    Text squeezer detection score: probability difference between
    original and canonicalised text classification.
    Returns L1 distance between prediction vectors.
    '''
    import torch
    import torch.nn.functional as F
    try:
        from token_substitution_attack import text_to_tensor
    except ImportError:
        return 0.0

    x_orig = text_to_tensor(text, max_len)
    x_canon = text_to_tensor(canonicalise_fn(text), max_len)
    with torch.no_grad():
        p_orig = F.softmax(model(x_orig), dim=1)
        p_canon = F.softmax(model(x_canon), dim=1)
    return (p_orig - p_canon).abs().sum().item()


def detect_text_adversarial(
    model,
    text: str,
    threshold: float = 0.05,
    max_len: int = 64,
) -> Tuple[bool, float, str]:
    '''
    Combined text squeezer detection:
    1. Canonicalise text (squeezer transformation)
    2. Compare predictions before/after
    3. Flag if score > threshold

    Returns (detected, score, canonical_text).
    '''
    canonical = full_canonicalise(text)
    score = text_squeezer_score(model, text, full_canonicalise, max_len)
    return score > threshold, score, canonical

Adaptive Adversary Test Against Squeezer

squeezer_adaptive_adversary_test.py

Tests squeezer effectiveness against C&W adversarial examples crafted with squeezer knowledge (adaptive attack) vs without squeezer knowledge (non-adaptive); quantifies the detection gap

Code

# squeezer_adaptive_adversary_test.py
# Section 10.11: Adaptive Adversary Test Against Feature Squeezer
# 20260524 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from feature_squeezer import (gaussian_squeeze, bit_depth_squeeze,
                              squeezer_detection_score, SqueezerConfig)
from squeezer_ensemble import ensemble_score, calibrate_threshold, build_default_squeezers


def non_adaptive_cw_l2(
    model: nn.Module,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    kappa: float = 0.0,
    c: float = 1.0,
    n_steps: int = 100,
    lr: float = 1e-2,
) -> torch.Tensor:
    '''Standard C&W L2: optimises against model(x_adv) only. Squeezer unaware.'''
    batch = x.size(0)
    x = x.detach()
    x_cl = x.clamp(1e-6, 1.0 - 1e-6)
    w = torch.atanh(2.0 * x_cl - 1.0).detach().requires_grad_(True)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([w], lr=lr)
    criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss(reduction='none')

    for _ in range(n_steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        x_adv = 0.5 * (torch.tanh(w) + 1.0)
        l2_sq = (x_adv - x).pow(2).view(batch, -1).sum(1)
        logits = model(x_adv)
        z_correct = logits.gather(1, y.view(-1, 1)).squeeze(1)
        mask = torch.ones_like(logits, dtype=torch.bool)
        mask.scatter_(1, y.view(-1, 1), False)
        best_other = logits[mask].view(batch, -1).max(1).values
        f_val = torch.clamp(z_correct - best_other, min=-kappa)
        (l2_sq + c * f_val).sum().backward()
        opt.step()

    with torch.no_grad():
        x_adv = 0.5 * (torch.tanh(w) + 1.0)
    return x_adv.detach()


def adaptive_cw_l2_vs_squeezer(
    model: nn.Module,
    squeeze_fn,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    kappa: float = 0.0,
    c: float = 1.0,
    n_steps: int = 100,
    lr: float = 5e-3,
) -> torch.Tensor:
    '''
    Adaptive C&W L2: optimises against model(squeezer(x_adv)).
    Adversarial example must misclassify AFTER squeezing.
    Squeezer-aware: squeezer is part of the forward pass.
    '''
    batch = x.size(0)
    x = x.detach()
    x_cl = x.clamp(1e-6, 1.0 - 1e-6)
    w = torch.atanh(2.0 * x_cl - 1.0).detach().requires_grad_(True)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([w], lr=lr)

    for _ in range(n_steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        x_adv = 0.5 * (torch.tanh(w) + 1.0)
        l2_sq = (x_adv - x).pow(2).view(batch, -1).sum(1)
        # KEY DIFFERENCE: evaluate misclassification on squeezed input
        x_squeezed = squeeze_fn(x_adv)
        logits = model(x_squeezed)
        z_correct = logits.gather(1, y.view(-1, 1)).squeeze(1)
        mask = torch.ones_like(logits, dtype=torch.bool)
        mask.scatter_(1, y.view(-1, 1), False)
        best_other = logits[mask].view(batch, -1).max(1).values
        f_val = torch.clamp(z_correct - best_other, min=-kappa)
        (l2_sq + c * f_val).sum().backward()
        opt.step()

    with torch.no_grad():
        x_adv = 0.5 * (torch.tanh(w) + 1.0)
    return x_adv.detach()


def evaluate_detection_gap(
    model: nn.Module,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    squeezers,
    threshold: float,
) -> None:
    squeeze_fn = lambda inp: gaussian_squeeze(inp, sigma=1.0)

    # Non-adaptive adversarial examples (no squeezer knowledge)
    x_nonadaptive = non_adaptive_cw_l2(model, x, y, kappa=0.0, c=1.0, n_steps=80)

    # Adaptive adversarial examples (knows the squeezer)
    x_adaptive = adaptive_cw_l2_vs_squeezer(model, squeeze_fn, x, y, kappa=0.0, c=1.0, n_steps=80)

    # Detection rates
    scores_na = ensemble_score(model, x_nonadaptive, squeezers)
    scores_ad = ensemble_score(model, x_adaptive, squeezers)
    scores_nat = ensemble_score(model, x, squeezers)

    detect_na = (scores_na > threshold).float().mean().item()
    detect_ad = (scores_ad > threshold).float().mean().item()
    fpr = (scores_nat > threshold).float().mean().item()

    # Attack success rates
    with torch.no_grad():
        succ_na = (model(x_nonadaptive).argmax(1) != y).float().mean().item()
        succ_ad = (model(x_adaptive).argmax(1) != y).float().mean().item()

    print('Adaptive vs Non-Adaptive Squeezer Evaluation')
    print(f'  Natural FPR:              {fpr:.3f}')
    print(f'  Non-adaptive detection:   {detect_na:.3f}  (attack success: {succ_na:.3f})')
    print(f'  Adaptive detection:       {detect_ad:.3f}  (attack success on squeezed: {succ_ad:.3f})')
    print(f'  Detection gap:            {detect_na - detect_ad:.3f}')
    print()
    print('Interpretation:')
    print('  Detection gap > 0 means the adaptive adversary evades squeezing detection.')
    print('  This motivates combining squeezing with consistency checking (Section 10.12).')

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Calibrating the squeezer detection threshold only on easy-to-classify natural inputs, producing a threshold that yields excessive false positives in production

Why It Happens

Calibration is done on a convenient sample of natural images that the model classifies with high confidence. Near the decision boundary, benign images produce high squeezer scores because mild smoothing is enough to flip their already-uncertain classification. This is not measured during calibration and the threshold is set too low.

Consequence

In production, borderline natural inputs (images that the model classifies with 55-60% confidence) regularly exceed the calibrated threshold and are flagged as adversarial. Alert volume overwhelms analysts; the squeezer is either recalibrated to a higher threshold (losing detection sensitivity) or disabled entirely. A well-tuned squeezer with proper calibration is replaced by an untuned squeezer or no squeezer.

Correct Approach

Calibrate the threshold on a dataset that includes hard natural examples near the decision boundary. If the production classifier sees ambiguous inputs (borderline images, unusual angles, low-quality captures), those must be represented in the calibration set. Accept that the threshold will be higher than what an "easy image" calibration would produce, and accept the resulting lower detection rate -- it is more honest than a falsely confident threshold.

Mistake 2

Description

Running squeezers sequentially and exceeding the 50ms P99 gateway latency budget without realising it

Why It Happens

The squeezer ensemble is implemented as a loop: for each squeezer, transform the input and run the model. Three squeezers means three model forward passes sequentially before the final detection decision is made. The latency impact is not measured until the system is deployed under production load.

Consequence

Gateway P99 latency triples or quadruples depending on the number of squeezers. User-facing requests time out; SLO violations accumulate; operations disables Track 7 to restore latency. The detection layer is removed not because it failed to detect adversarial examples but because it was not engineered for the latency budget.

Correct Approach

Always run squeezer inferences in parallel with the primary model inference using a thread pool or async dispatch. Measure parallel latency with squeezer_latency_benchmark.py before production deployment. The Section 10.13 gateway includes explicit parallel dispatch for Track 7 squeezer passes. The latency overhead of correct parallel execution is the squeezer transform time (~1-3ms), not the model inference time.

Mistake 3

Description

Treating squeezing as a complete defence against adversarial examples and removing the consistency checker and adversarial classifier from Track 7

Why It Happens

Feature squeezing shows high detection rates (70-90%) against FGSM and PGD in benchmarks. Engineers conclude that squeezing alone is sufficient and skip the complexity of implementing consistency checking and the adversarial classifier.

Consequence

Adaptive adversaries using C&W with squeezer knowledge (Section 10.5 defence_evaluator_cw.py) reduce squeezer detection rates to 20-35%. High-kappa C&W adversarial examples (kappa=20) further reduce detection rates. Physical adversarial patches are not effectively caught by per-pixel squeezing. The system is deployed believing it is defended when it is not against motivated adversaries who know squeezing is the defence.

Correct Approach

Squeezing is one of three Track 7 primitives. Section 10.12 consistency checking and adversarial classifier are required for complete Track 7 coverage. The three primitives cover complementary attack families and the ensemble is harder to jointly evade than any individual primitive. Never deploy Track 7 with only squeezing.

Mistake 4

Description

Applying image feature squeezers to text inputs without implementing text-specific preprocessing analogues

Why It Happens

Track 7 is configured generically: the squeezer ensemble runs on all inputs. For a multi-modal system that processes both images and text, the image squeezers (Gaussian blur, bit-depth reduction) are applied to text inputs, producing garbage transformations that have no meaningful relationship to text adversarial attacks.

Consequence

The squeezer scores for text inputs are either always near zero (if the transformation is ignored by the text tokeniser) or always very high (if the byte-level corruption produces out-of-vocabulary tokens that change predictions randomly). In either case, the squeezer provides no detection signal for text adversarial attacks. Homoglyph and zero- width character attacks evade Track 7 detection entirely.

Correct Approach

Deploy modality-specific squeezers: image squeezers (Gaussian, bit-depth, JPEG) for image inputs; text canonicalisers (Unicode normalisation, confusable replacement, zero-width removal) for text inputs. The text_canonicaliser.py deliverable implements the text-specific primitives. Section 10.13 routes inputs to the appropriate squeezer set based on input modality.

Mistake 5

Description

Reporting squeezer detection rates against non-adaptive adversarial examples as the production robustness guarantee

Why It Happens

Detection rate benchmarks are run against FGSM and PGD adversarial examples generated without knowledge of the squeezer. These show 70-90% detection rates. The production security report states "Track 7 detects 80% of adversarial examples" without specifying that this is against non-adaptive adversaries.

Consequence

Stakeholders believe the system is 80% protected. A motivated adversary who reads the squeezer configuration (either from published documentation or adaptive probing) reduces detection to 20-35% using adaptive C&W. The gap between the reported 80% and actual 20-35% is not disclosed in the security posture until an actual adversarial evasion incident is discovered.

Correct Approach

Always report two detection rate figures: non-adaptive (FGSM/PGD adversaries who do not know the squeezer configuration) and adaptive (C&W with squeezer knowledge, using squeezer_adaptive_adversary_test.py). The honest production statement is: "Track 7 detects 80% of non-adaptive adversarial examples and 35% of adaptive adversarial examples against squeezing alone; the three-primitive ensemble reduces the adaptive gap." This sets accurate expectations and motivates the full ensemble.

Section Summary

Compact

Feature squeezing detects adversarial examples through their sensitivity to input transformation: adversarial perturbations are precisely calibrated to the model and are fragile under mild transformation, while natural images are robust. Three canonical squeezers cover complementary attack families: Gaussian blur catches L-infinity attacks (FGSM, PGD), bit-depth reduction catches fine-grained perturbations, and JPEG compression catches structured frequency-domain perturbations. The squeezer detection score is the L1 distance between prediction vectors on original vs squeezed input; a threshold calibrated on natural inputs including hard borderline examples separates adversarial from benign. A squeezer ensemble provides union coverage but does not defend against adaptive adversaries who optimise through the squeezer. Squeezers must run in parallel with primary model inference to stay within the 50ms P99 Track 7 budget; sequential execution typically violates the latency constraint. Text-specific canonicalisation primitives (Unicode normalisation, confusable replacement, zero-width removal) implement the text analogue.

Key Takeaways

  • Detection signal: adversarial inputs change predictions under squeezing; natural inputs do not -- the asymmetry is the detection primitive
  • Three squeezers: Gaussian blur (L-inf attacks), bit-depth reduction (fine-grained), JPEG (structured frequency) -- each covers different attack families
  • Threshold calibration must include hard natural examples near the decision boundary; easy-image calibration over-fits and fails in production
  • Parallel squeezer execution is mandatory: sequential execution triples/quadruples latency and violates the 50ms P99 gateway budget
  • Single squeezer is insufficient: adaptive C&W reduces detection to 20-35%; the three-primitive Track 7 ensemble (squeezing + consistency + classifier) is required
  • Text analogues: Unicode NFKC normalisation + confusable replacement + zero-width removal; route text inputs to text canonicalisers, not image squeezers

Section 10.12: Detection Primitives -- Consistency Checking and Adversarial Classifier

Summary

Section 10.12 completes the Track 7 detection primitive library with two post-inference primitives. The consistency checker submits N randomly perturbed variants of the input and flags inputs whose classification is unstable across variants -- adversarial examples near the decision boundary are fragile and flip classification under small random perturbation, while natural inputs are stable. For text, the consistency checker uses back-translation and semantic paraphrase rather than random pixel noise. The adversarial detector classifier is a binary classifier trained to distinguish adversarial from natural examples using features extracted from the input (gradient magnitude distribution, embedding distance to training manifold, spectral properties). It generalises across attack families seen in training and degrades on novel attack families. Section 10.12 builds both primitives and combines them with the Section 10.11 squeezer ensemble into the five-component detection ensemble that Section 10.13 wires into the production Track 7 gateway.
