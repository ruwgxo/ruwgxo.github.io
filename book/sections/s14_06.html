Chapter 14 · Section 6 · ~7 pages

Failure Modes: Fail-Open versus Fail-Closed

Section Overview

One Line Summary

When a detector errors, times out, or is unavailable the gateway must do something deliberate: fail-open passes the request favouring availability, fail-closed blocks it favouring security, and the choice is made per detector and per tier -- classifying detectors as essential or droppable -- so a partial failure degrades gracefully to the essential detectors rather than failing one way for everything.

Why This Section Exists

Sections 14.04 and 14.05 made the gateway fast per request and sufficient in aggregate, and both assumed the detectors work. Detectors do not always work. A detector can raise an exception, exceed its timeout, or become unavailable because the service behind it is down, and when that happens the gateway has to decide what to do with the request it was inspecting. This is the failure concern named in the 14.01 taxonomy, and it is the one with the sharpest consequence of neglect: an unspecified failure mode is not the absence of a decision but a decision made by the bug, because whatever the code happens to do when the exception propagates becomes the policy. This section makes the failure behaviour deliberate. It defines the two possible policies -- fail-open, which admits the request when detection fails and favours availability, and fail-closed, which blocks it and favours security -- and establishes that each carries a real and opposite risk, so neither is safe in the abstract. It then makes the central argument of the section: the policy is chosen per detector and per tier, not once globally, because a critical security detector and a best-effort enrichment detector warrant opposite policies. It handles the timeout from 14.04 as one failure kind that resolves through the same policy, and it defines degraded-mode operation, classifying detectors as essential or droppable so that a partial failure sheds the droppable detectors and keeps serving through the essential ones. The result is a gateway whose behaviour under detector failure is designed rather than accidental.

Position In Chapter

Section 14.06 is the first of the two resilience sections (14.06-14.07) and the response to the failure-handling concern from the 14.01 taxonomy. It decides what a single failing detector does. Section 14.07 then supplies the mechanism that stops a detector from failing repeatedly into a cascade -- circuit breakers, bulkheads, health checks, and redundancy -- and turns the degraded-mode classification defined here into the graceful degradation it operates. This section sets the policy; 14.07 builds the machinery that enforces and contains it.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

An Unspecified Failure Mode Is Chosen by the Bug

Body

Every detector integrated into the request path can fail, and there are three ways it does. It can error -- raise an exception from a bug, a malformed input, or a missing model artifact. It can time out -- exceed the deadline that Section 14.04 put on it to bound its latency. Or it can be unavailable -- the service behind it is down, unreachable, or not yet started. In all three cases the gateway is left holding a request it was in the middle of inspecting, with no verdict from that detector, and it has to do something with the request anyway. The single most important idea in this section is that this something is always chosen, whether or not anyone chose it. If the failure mode is not specified, the request does not hang in limbo; it takes whatever path the code happens to take when the exception propagates. Maybe the exception bubbles up and the whole request is rejected, silently making the policy fail-closed. Maybe it is swallowed by a broad catch and the request proceeds as if the detector had passed it, silently making the policy fail-open. Either way a security-versus-availability decision of real consequence has been made, and it has been made by the accident of code structure rather than by an engineer weighing the trade. An unspecified failure mode is a failure mode chosen by the bug. That reframing is why this section exists as a design activity rather than an error-handling detail. The goal is not to prevent detectors from failing -- they will -- but to ensure that what happens when they do is a deliberate choice with a stated rationale. The failure-isolated integration boundary from Section 14.03 is where the choice is applied: the wrapper already catches the fault so it does not break the path, and this section supplies the policy the wrapper applies. The two policies it can apply are fail-open and fail-closed, and the rest of the section is about choosing between them.

Part 2

Title

Fail-Open and Fail-Closed: Two Policies, Two Risks

Body

The two policies are simple to state. Fail-open means that when a detector fails, the gateway passes the request -- it proceeds as though the failed detector had not objected. Fail-closed means that when a detector fails, the gateway blocks the request -- it refuses to proceed without the detector verdict. Fail-open favours availability: the request is served even though a detector could not run. Fail-closed favours security: the request is denied rather than served uninspected. What makes the choice hard is that each policy carries a real risk, and the risks are opposite. The fail-open risk is that the gateway admits the very attack the failed detector was there to catch. If the injection classifier fails open, an injection attempt that arrives during the failure sails through uninspected, and the protection the detector was supposed to provide is absent exactly when an attacker who has noticed the failure would strike. The fail-closed risk is the mirror image: the gateway denies service to legitimate traffic because a detector is having a bad moment. If the injection classifier fails closed, then while it is down every request -- the overwhelming majority of which are legitimate -- is rejected, and a detector fault has become a denial-of-service against the gateway own users. Neither risk is hypothetical and neither policy is safe in the abstract. Fail-open trades a security gap for continued service; fail-closed trades continued service for a security guarantee. The decision table for this section makes this symmetry explicit by recording, for each policy, the specific risk it takes, because the only honest way to choose is to name the risk you are accepting. The choice is therefore not between a safe option and a risky one but between two risks, and which risk is acceptable depends entirely on what the detector guards -- which is the argument of the next part.

Part 3

Title

The Policy Is Per Detector and Per Tier

Body

A single global failure policy is tempting because it is simple: decide once that the gateway fails open, or that it fails closed, and apply it everywhere. But a global policy is wrong for some detectors no matter which way it is set, because the detectors do not all guard the same thing or warrant the same trade. The central design principle of this section is that the fail-open versus fail-closed choice is made per detector and per tier. Consider two detectors at opposite ends. A prompt injection classifier is a critical security detector: its whole purpose is to block a class of attack, and admitting an uninspected request when it fails means admitting exactly the attack it guards against. For this detector the fail-open risk is severe and the fail-closed risk -- some denied legitimate traffic during a fault -- is the lesser evil, so it fails closed. Now consider an input PII audit tagger whose only job is to annotate requests for later audit. It blocks nothing in normal operation and its signal is best-effort. If it fails, denying service over a missing audit tag would be absurd -- the fail-closed risk is unacceptable and the fail-open risk is a mere audit gap -- so it fails open. A global policy set to fail-closed would turn the audit tagger into a denial-of-service risk; set to fail-open it would leave the injection classifier admitting attacks. Only a per-detector policy gets both right. The tier dimension reinforces the same point. The off-path near-real-time and batch tiers never block a request in the first place -- they consume the exchange asynchronously -- so their failure is naturally fail-open: there is no synchronous request to deny. The inline tier is where the hard fail-closed decisions live, because it is the only tier that can block. So the policy is a property of the detector and its tier together: what the detector guards sets the security stakes, and which tier it runs on sets whether blocking is even possible. The decision engine for this section encodes exactly this -- a policy attached to each detector, resolving every failure kind through that detector own choice.

Part 4

Title

Timeout as a Failure Mode

Body

Section 14.04 introduced the per-detector timeout as a latency-bounding mechanism: a deadline that stops a slow detector from blowing the budget. That section deliberately left one thing open -- what the gateway does when the timeout fires -- and this section closes it. A timeout is a failure mode, and it resolves through exactly the same per-detector policy as an error or an unavailability. The unification is worth being explicit about, because it keeps the design simple. From the gateway point of view, an errored detector, a timed-out detector, and an unavailable detector are the same situation: a detector that owes a verdict and has not produced one. The reason it has no verdict -- it crashed, it ran too long, it could not be reached -- does not change what the gateway must decide, only what it logs. So all three failure kinds map to the one policy the detector carries. A critical detector that times out fails closed, just as it would if it errored; a best-effort detector that times out fails open, just as it would if it were unavailable. The timeout-to-policy mapping for this section shows the same timeout mechanism producing opposite decisions on two detectors purely because their policies differ. This also completes the story the timeout began in 14.04. There, the timeout guaranteed the worst case was bounded so the latency budget held; the cost was that a bounded failure still needs a decision. Here, that decision is supplied by the failure policy, so the two sections together give the full account: 14.04 bounds how long a detector may take, and 14.06 says what happens when it takes too long. The timeout is not a separate kind of problem with its own handling -- it is folded into the single per-detector failure policy, which is what keeps the failure behaviour of the whole gateway coherent.

Part 5

Title

Degraded-Mode Operation: Essential and Droppable Detectors

Body

A per-detector policy handles the failure of one detector, but production failures are often partial and correlated: several detectors on a tier degrade at once under load, or a shared dependency wobbles. The question then is not just what each detector does on failure but what the gateway as a whole does when it can no longer run everything. The answer is degraded-mode operation, and it rests on classifying detectors as essential or droppable. An essential detector is one the gateway must keep running to provide its core security guarantee -- the injection classifier, the jailbreak classifier, the output PII scanner. These are the detectors the request path degrades toward, never drops: if they cannot run, fail-closed is the right response, because losing them silently would leave the gateway serving traffic it can no longer vouch for. A droppable detector is one whose loss weakens the picture but not the core guarantee -- the audit tagger, the near-real-time session scorer, the batch analytics. These are the detectors the gateway sheds first under stress, failing them open, because dropping them keeps the essential path serving rather than letting a best-effort detector take the whole gateway down. This classification is what turns failure handling from a per-detector decision into a whole-gateway strategy. Without it, a partial failure forces a single crude choice -- block everything or admit everything -- because there is no notion of which detectors matter more. With it, the gateway degrades gracefully: under stress it sheds the droppable detectors and keeps serving through the essential ones, so the security core stays intact while the enrichment falls away. The degraded-mode classification for this section is exactly this list of essential and droppable detectors, and it is the input that Section 14.07 graceful degradation will operate: this section decides which detectors are essential, and 14.07 builds the mechanism that sheds the rest under load.

Part 6

Title

From Policy to Mechanism

Body

With the failure policy in place, the gateway behaviour under detector failure is now designed rather than accidental. Every detector carries a deliberate fail-open or fail-closed policy chosen from what it guards and which tier it runs on; every failure kind -- error, timeout, unavailability -- resolves through that one policy; and the essential-versus-droppable classification lets a partial failure degrade to the security core rather than collapsing one way for everything. The security-versus-availability trade is made once, per detector, in advance, by an engineer weighing the risk, which is exactly what the 14.01 taxonomy demanded when it warned that an unspecified failure mode is chosen by the bug. What this section does not do is stop a detector from failing repeatedly. The policy says what to do on each failure, but if a detector is broken it will keep failing, and calling a broken detector on every request -- even to fail it open or closed by policy -- wastes work, adds latency, and can turn one detector fault into a load problem that spreads. Deciding the policy is necessary but not sufficient; the gateway also needs a mechanism that recognises a persistently failing detector and stops calling it until it recovers. That mechanism is the subject of Section 14.07. The circuit breaker cuts off a detector that has failed repeatedly, moving from closed to open to half-open as it tests recovery, so a broken detector is isolated rather than retried into a cascade. Bulkheads keep one detector failure from exhausting the resources the others need; health checks and readiness decide when an instance should receive traffic at all; and graceful degradation operates the essential-versus-droppable classification defined here to shed non-essential detectors under stress. This section decided what a failing detector does; the next builds the machinery that keeps a failing detector from taking the gateway down with it.

Fail-Open versus Fail-Closed Decision Engine

# fail_open_closed_policy.py
# Section 14.06: Fail-Open versus Fail-Closed Decision Engine
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 14 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass
from enum import Enum
from typing import List


class FailurePolicy(Enum):
    FAIL_OPEN = 'fail_open'      # admit the request when detection fails (availability)
    FAIL_CLOSED = 'fail_closed'  # block the request when detection fails (security)


class FailureKind(Enum):
    ERROR = 'error'              # the detector raised
    TIMEOUT = 'timeout'          # the detector exceeded its deadline
    UNAVAILABLE = 'unavailable'  # the detector could not be reached


@dataclass
class DetectorPolicy:
    name: str
    tier: str
    essential: bool          # essential detectors fail closed, droppable ones fail open
    policy: FailurePolicy
    rationale: str


@dataclass
class FailureDecision:
    detector: str
    kind: str
    policy: str
    request_admitted: bool
    detail: str


POLICIES: List[DetectorPolicy] = [
    DetectorPolicy('Injection classifier', 'inline', True, FailurePolicy.FAIL_CLOSED,
                   'Critical security detector: admitting an uninspected request risks the attack it guards against'),
    DetectorPolicy('Jailbreak classifier', 'inline', True, FailurePolicy.FAIL_CLOSED,
                   'Critical security detector: fail closed rather than admit a possibly malicious prompt'),
    DetectorPolicy('Output PII scanner', 'inline', True, FailurePolicy.FAIL_CLOSED,
                   'Leaking PII on failure is unacceptable, so withhold the response when the scan fails'),
    DetectorPolicy('Input PII audit tagger', 'inline', False, FailurePolicy.FAIL_OPEN,
                   'Best-effort audit signal: its failure must not deny service to a legitimate request'),
    DetectorPolicy('Session behaviour scorer', 'near_real_time', False, FailurePolicy.FAIL_OPEN,
                   'Off-path enrichment: it never blocks in the first place, so its failure fails open'),
]


def decide(policy: DetectorPolicy, kind: FailureKind) -> FailureDecision:
    '''Apply a detector policy to a specific failure and return the admit/block decision.

    The policy is a property of the detector, not of the failure kind: an error, a
    timeout, and an unavailability all resolve through the same fail-open or
    fail-closed choice. What differs by kind is only the detail logged.
    '''
    admitted = policy.policy is FailurePolicy.FAIL_OPEN
    verb = 'admitted' if admitted else 'blocked'
    detail = f'{policy.name} {kind.value}; policy {policy.policy.value} -> request {verb}'
    return FailureDecision(policy.name, kind.value, policy.policy.value, admitted, detail)


def print_policies(policies: List[DetectorPolicy]) -> None:
    print('=' * 84)
    print('FAIL-OPEN vs FAIL-CLOSED DECISION ENGINE')
    print('The policy is per detector and per tier, not a single global default')
    print('=' * 84)
    for p in policies:
        klass = 'essential' if p.essential else 'droppable'
        print()
        print(f'DETECTOR: {p.name} [{p.tier}, {klass}]')
        print(f'  Policy   : {p.policy.value}')
        print(f'  Rationale: {p.rationale}')


def demo_failures(policies: List[DetectorPolicy]) -> None:
    print()
    print('=' * 84)
    print('FAILURE DECISIONS (error / timeout / unavailable resolve through the same policy)')
    print('=' * 84)
    for p in policies:
        for kind in FailureKind:
            d = decide(p, kind)
            print(f'  {d.detail}')
        print()
    fail_open = sum(1 for p in policies if p.policy is FailurePolicy.FAIL_OPEN)
    fail_closed = len(policies) - fail_open
    print(f'Summary: {fail_closed} detectors fail closed (security), {fail_open} fail open (availability)')
    print('A single global policy would force one wrong trade on half of these detectors.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    print_policies(POLICIES)
    demo_failures(POLICIES)

# timeout_to_policy_mapping.py
# Section 14.06: Timeout-to-Failure-Policy Mapping
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 14 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass
from enum import Enum
from typing import List


class Policy(Enum):
    FAIL_OPEN = 'fail_open'
    FAIL_CLOSED = 'fail_closed'


@dataclass
class DetectorTimeout:
    name: str
    timeout_ms: float
    policy: Policy


@dataclass
class TimeoutOutcome:
    detector: str
    elapsed_ms: float
    timed_out: bool
    resolution: str


def resolve_timeout(cfg: DetectorTimeout, elapsed_ms: float) -> TimeoutOutcome:
    '''A timeout is one failure kind among error and unavailable, and it resolves
    through the same per-detector policy. Section 14.04 introduced the timeout as a
    latency-bounding mechanism; here the timeout is mapped to the fail-open or
    fail-closed decision that says what the gateway does when the deadline is hit.
    '''
    if elapsed_ms <= cfg.timeout_ms:
        return TimeoutOutcome(cfg.name, elapsed_ms, False,
                              'completed within deadline; normal verdict used')
    if cfg.policy is Policy.FAIL_OPEN:
        resolution = 'timed out -> fail-open -> request admitted without this detector'
    else:
        resolution = 'timed out -> fail-closed -> request blocked'
    return TimeoutOutcome(cfg.name, elapsed_ms, True, resolution)

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Leaving the failure mode unspecified and letting the code decide what happens when a detector fails

Why It Happens

In normal operation the detector never fails, so the failure path is never exercised and feels theoretical, and it is easy to assume the request will be handled sensibly without deciding what sensible means.

Consequence

When a detector eventually fails, the request takes whatever path the code happens to take -- silently failing open through a swallowed exception or failing closed through an uncaught one -- so a security-versus-availability decision of real consequence is made by the accident of code structure rather than by an engineer.

Correct Approach

Specify a deliberate fail-open or fail-closed policy for every detector and apply it at the isolated integration boundary, so that what happens on failure is a chosen policy with a stated rationale, never whatever the bug does.

Mistake 2

Description

Applying a single global failure policy to every detector

Why It Happens

One policy for the whole gateway is simple to state and reason about, and choosing once seems cleaner than maintaining a per-detector decision, so the gateway is set to fail open everywhere or fail closed everywhere.

Consequence

The global policy is wrong for some detectors whichever way it is set: fail-closed everywhere turns a best-effort audit tagger fault into a denial of service, while fail-open everywhere leaves the injection classifier admitting the attacks it exists to block.

Correct Approach

Choose the policy per detector and per tier from what the detector guards and whether its tier can block, so critical inline security detectors fail closed and best-effort or off-path detectors fail open.

Mistake 3

Description

Treating fail-open as the safe default because it keeps the service up

Why It Happens

Availability is visible and measured, and an outage is an obvious incident, so fail-open feels like the responsible choice because the gateway keeps serving and nothing appears to break.

Consequence

A critical security detector set to fail open silently admits the attacks it was meant to catch during every failure window, and an attacker who notices the detector is down has a clear opening that produces no visible outage to alert anyone.

Correct Approach

Recognise that fail-open takes a real security risk and is only the right default for best-effort and off-path detectors; fail the critical inline security detectors closed, accepting the availability cost as the lesser risk for what they guard.

Mistake 4

Description

Handling a timeout as a separate concern with its own ad hoc behaviour instead of through the failure policy

Why It Happens

The timeout was introduced in a different section as a latency mechanism, so it is easy to treat it as a performance detail and bolt on whatever behaviour is convenient when it fires, separately from the error-handling policy.

Consequence

The gateway ends up with inconsistent failure behaviour -- a detector that fails closed on an error but open on a timeout, or vice versa -- so its response to failure depends on the incidental reason for the failure rather than on a coherent policy.

Correct Approach

Treat the timeout as one failure kind alongside error and unavailability, resolving all three through the same per-detector policy, so a detector fails the same way regardless of why it failed to produce a verdict.

Mistake 5

Description

Having no essential-versus-droppable classification, so a partial failure forces a single crude gateway-wide choice

Why It Happens

Classifying every detector as essential or droppable is extra design work, and when all detectors are treated as equally important there is no obvious need for it until several fail at once.

Consequence

When a partial or correlated failure hits, the gateway has no notion of which detectors matter more, so it can only block everything or admit everything, losing either the service or the entire security core instead of degrading gracefully.

Correct Approach

Classify detectors as essential (fail-closed, never dropped) or droppable (fail-open, shed first), so a partial failure degrades to the essential security detectors and keeps serving through them while the droppable enrichment falls away.

Section Summary

Compact

When a detector errors, times out, or becomes unavailable, the gateway must do something deliberate, because an unspecified failure mode is not the absence of a decision but a decision made by the bug -- whatever the code does on the exception becomes the policy. The two policies are fail-open, which passes the request and favours availability, and fail-closed, which blocks it and favours security. Each carries a real and opposite risk: fail-open admits the attack the failed detector would have caught, and fail-closed denies service to legitimate traffic on a detector fault, so neither is safe in the abstract and the choice is between two risks. The policy is therefore made per detector and per tier, not once globally, because a critical inline security detector like the injection classifier warrants fail-closed while a best-effort audit tagger or an off-path scorer warrants fail-open, and a single global policy forces the wrong trade on half the detectors. The timeout from Section 14.04 is one failure kind that resolves through the same per-detector policy as an error or unavailability, so the same timeout mechanism produces opposite decisions on detectors whose policies differ. Degraded-mode operation classifies detectors as essential (fail-closed, never dropped) or droppable (fail-open, shed first), so a partial or correlated failure degrades gracefully to the essential security detectors rather than failing the whole gateway one way for everything. This decides what a failing detector does; the mechanism that stops a detector from failing repeatedly into a cascade is Section 14.07.

Key Takeaways

  • An unspecified failure mode is a failure mode chosen by the bug, so the gateway response to a detector error, timeout, or unavailability must be a deliberate policy
  • Fail-open passes the request favouring availability and risks admitting the attack; fail-closed blocks it favouring security and risks denying legitimate service -- neither is safe in the abstract
  • The fail-open versus fail-closed choice is made per detector and per tier, because a critical security detector and a best-effort enrichment detector warrant opposite policies
  • A single global failure policy forces the wrong trade on some detectors whichever way it is set
  • The timeout from Section 14.04 is one failure kind that resolves through the same per-detector policy as an error or unavailability
  • Degraded-mode operation classifies detectors as essential (fail-closed) or droppable (fail-open) so a partial failure degrades to the essential security path rather than failing one way for everything

Title

High Availability, Circuit Breakers, and Graceful Degradation

Summary

Section 14.06 decided what a failing detector does; Section 14.07 builds the mechanism that keeps a failing detector from taking the gateway down with it. It introduces the circuit breaker that cuts off a detector after repeated failures -- moving between closed, open, and half-open as it tests recovery -- so a broken detector is isolated rather than retried into a cascade. It adds bulkheads that keep one detector failure from exhausting shared resources, health checks and readiness that decide when an instance receives traffic, redundancy and failover for the tiers, and the graceful degradation that operates the essential-versus-droppable classification defined here. It is the second resilience section, turning failure policy into failure containment.
