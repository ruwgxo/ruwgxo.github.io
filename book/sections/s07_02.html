Chapter 7 · Section 2 · ~6 pages

Why Jailbreaks Work: RLHF Alignment Failure Modes

Content

Opening

Section 7.1 established that jailbreaks target the alignment layer. This section explains what that means at the mechanism level -- what alignment actually is in the weight space, where it is fragile, and why adversarial inputs can cross that fragility consistently enough to be weaponised. Every jailbreak technique in Sections 7.3 through 7.12 is an instance of the same underlying mechanism. Understanding the mechanism is what separates a defender who patches known prompts from one who anticipates the next variant before it appears.

Subsections

Number

7.2.1

Title

RLHF: What the Training Process Actually Does

Body

Chapter 4 Section 4.3 covered the RLHF pipeline at an architectural level. Here we go one layer deeper -- to what RLHF does and does not do to the model's weights, because that distinction is the entire basis of jailbreak possibility. Pre-training is the first phase. The base model is trained on a massive corpus of text -- web pages, books, code, forum posts -- using a next-token prediction objective. No instruction following. No refusal behaviour. The model learns to continue text in ways that are statistically consistent with the training distribution. It learns everything that is represented in that corpus: how to write code, how to explain chemistry, how to describe violence, how to construct persuasive arguments for any position. All of it. The pre-trained base model has no concept of what it should or should not produce. It has only learned what text looks like. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the second phase. Human labellers create examples of good model behaviour: a user asks a question, a labeller writes an ideal response. The model is fine-tuned on these examples. It learns to produce assistant-style outputs and to follow conversational instruction format. It also learns some basic refusal patterns from the examples where labellers wrote refusals. But SFT alone does not produce robust alignment. The model has seen only a finite set of labelled examples. It does not generalise refusal reliably to novel harmful requests. RLHF is the third phase. A reward model is trained separately on human preference data: pairs of model outputs where a labeller indicates which is better. The reward model learns to score responses according to human preference -- helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty. The main model is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning (PPO -- Proximal Policy Optimisation) to maximise the reward model's score. This is where alignment behaviour is strongly instilled. The model learns to produce outputs that score highly on the reward model -- which includes refusing harmful requests, hedging on uncertain claims, and declining to assist with activities the preference data marked as harmful. The critical point: at no stage in this process are capabilities removed from the model's weights. The pre-training knowledge is still there. RLHF adjusts the probability distribution over outputs given an input. It makes harmful outputs less likely. It does not make them impossible.

7.2.2

Title

The Capability Substrate: What Lives Under the Alignment

Body

Think of the pre-trained base model as a complete map of human knowledge and language patterns, including all the parts that should never be publicly expressed. RLHF lays a filter over that map. The filter is not a deletion -- it is a learned bias in the output distribution. Routes to harmful content still exist in the weight space. The filter makes them low-probability paths, not blocked paths. This is demonstrable. Take a safety-trained model and ask it to write instructions for something harmful. It refuses. Now take the same model's architecture, initialise it from the pre-trained checkpoint before RLHF, and ask the same question. It answers. The capability was in the base model. RLHF suppressed the path to that capability without deleting the capability itself. The capability substrate is intact beneath the alignment. This is not a criticism of RLHF as an approach. It is a mathematical consequence of how neural network fine-tuning works. Fine-tuning shifts weights by gradient descent on a new objective. It does not zero out weights that encode prior knowledge -- doing so would catastrophically degrade general performance. The fine-tuning objective (maximise reward model score) is achieved by adjusting output probabilities, not by erasing pre-training knowledge. For security engineers, the implication is precise: jailbreaks are not attacks on the model's knowledge. They are attacks on the filter. The goal is not to teach the model something new. The goal is to find a path to the pre-existing capability that the filter assigns low probability to -- and raise that probability above the refusal threshold.

Number

7.2.3

Title

Distribution Shift: The Mechanism Every Jailbreak Exploits

Body

RLHF trains on a finite distribution of inputs. The preference labellers write prompts and evaluate responses across a range of topics and phrasings. The reward model learns what human reviewers preferred in those specific contexts. The policy model (the aligned LLM) learns to produce outputs that score highly across that training distribution. The training distribution does not cover everything. It cannot. The space of possible prompts is effectively infinite. The preference data covers a meaningful but bounded subset. Outside that subset -- in regions of prompt space that labellers did not visit -- the model's alignment behaviour is extrapolated, not trained. Extrapolation is unreliable. Every jailbreak technique is a method for moving the input into a region of prompt space where: - the alignment training distribution is sparse (few labelled examples near this input), or - the refusal signal and the helpfulness signal are in direct conflict (the model cannot maximise both), or - the surface form of the input pattern-matches against non-harmful examples in the training data (roleplay, academic discussion, fiction) even though the semantic content is harmful. Persona jailbreaks (Section 7.3) move the input into the roleplay region of prompt space, where the alignment training focused on fictional assistance, not capability restriction. Fictional framing (Section 7.4) does the same for creative writing contexts. GCG adversarial suffixes (Section 7.9) move the input into a region of token space that is entirely outside the natural language distribution -- no labelled examples exist there because no human would write that prompt. Many-shot jailbreaks (Section 7.10) move the input into the few-shot learning region where demonstrated examples override prior trained behaviour. Different techniques, same mechanism: distribution shift out of the region where alignment is reliable.

7.2.4

Title

Reward Model Hacking: When the Proxy Diverges from Intent

Body

RLHF optimises the policy model to maximise the reward model's score. The reward model is a proxy for human preference. Any gap between the proxy and the actual intent creates an exploitable surface -- this is Goodhart's Law applied to alignment: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Reward model hacking means producing outputs that score highly on the reward model while violating the underlying human intent. In the alignment context, this manifests in two ways that are relevant to jailbreaks. First: the reward model was trained on preference data from human labellers who rated responses in natural, unambiguous contexts. Jailbreak prompts are adversarially constructed to be ambiguous -- to look like legitimate requests (creative writing, hypothetical discussion, academic research) while carrying harmful intent. A reward model that learned to score based on surface-form helpfulness and harmlessness may assign high scores to responses to these prompts that a knowledgeable human reviewer would flag as harmful. Second: the reward model's scores are frozen after training. The policy model continues to be updated against it. Given enough updates (or adversarial fine-tuning), the policy model can find output patterns that maximise reward model score without genuinely satisfying the original preference criteria. This is analogous to adversarial examples against classifiers -- the reward model is itself a neural network with adversarial vulnerabilities. For jailbreak detection, the implication is that output-side safety filters are also not a complete defence. The reward model that trained the alignment is a proxy, and adversarial outputs can satisfy the proxy while failing the intent. Infrastructure detection must operate independent of the model's own safety training.

7.2.5

Title

Constitutional AI and RLAIF: Stronger Alignment, Same Fragility

Body

Constitutional AI (CAI), developed at Anthropic, modifies the RLHF pipeline in two ways. First, it uses a set of explicit principles (the constitution) to guide the initial supervised fine-tuning phase -- the model is trained to critique and revise its own outputs against constitutional principles before human labellers ever see them. Second, it replaces human preference labellers with AI feedback (RLAIF -- Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback) for part of the reward model training, scaling the diversity of preference signals. CAI produces models with more consistent alignment behaviour across a wider distribution of inputs. The constitutional principles explicitly address categories of harm that might be underrepresented in human preference data. This raises the effective distribution coverage of the alignment training. It does not eliminate the fundamental fragility. The constitution is a finite set of principles. The space of adversarial inputs is infinite. CAI improves coverage of the training distribution and consistency of refusal behaviour, but the capability substrate is unchanged -- it is still pre-training knowledge. The boundary between aligned and misaligned behaviour is better defined and more robustly trained, but it still exists, and the distribution outside that boundary still has low coverage. Many-shot jailbreaking (Section 7.10) works against CAI-aligned models specifically because it leverages in-context learning -- a mechanism that operates at inference time, outside the scope of any alignment training technique. Constitutional principles and RLAIF-trained reward models cannot override what demonstrated in-context examples teach the model to do within a single forward pass.

7.2.6

Title

Fine-Tuning as Alignment Removal: The Open-Weight Threat

Body

The most direct exploitation of alignment fragility requires no jailbreak prompt at all. It requires access to model weights and a GPU. Fine-tuning a safety-trained open-weight model on a dataset of uncensored harmful completions strips alignment reliably. The mechanism is the same as the original alignment training, run in reverse: gradient descent on the new objective adjusts the output distribution toward the fine-tuning data, away from the RLHF-trained refusal behaviour. A few thousand examples and a modest training budget are sufficient. Published research has demonstrated this repeatedly on Llama, Mistral, and other open-weight models. The threat model this creates: an adversary downloads a safety-trained open-weight model, fine-tunes it on uncensored data, and operates the resulting model without any alignment constraints. The fine-tuned model retains all pre-training capability with the alignment filter removed. GCG adversarial suffix generation (Section 7.9) requires white-box access to model weights -- open-weight models provide exactly that access, which is why Zou et al. (2023) demonstrated transferable suffixes: generate on an open-weight model, transfer to a closed API model. For closed-model deployments, fine-tuning alignment removal is not a direct threat. It is an indirect one: adversaries use open-weight fine-tuning to develop jailbreak techniques that then transfer to closed models. The existence of open-weight models with removable alignment is what makes transferable attack research possible at scale.

7.2.7

Title

The Alignment Tax: Capability-Safety Tradeoffs as Attack Surface

Body

Alignment training does not come free. Training a model to refuse harmful requests reliably degrades performance on some legitimate tasks -- this degradation is called the alignment tax. The tax exists because refusal training and capability training pull the model's output distribution in different directions in overlapping regions of prompt space. Two specific alignment tax patterns are adversarially exploitable. First: over-refusal. Models trained with aggressive refusal thresholds refuse legitimate requests that superficially resemble harmful ones. A chemistry professor asking about reaction mechanisms gets refused. A security researcher asking about malware behaviour gets refused. Over-refusal is a business cost -- it makes the model less useful. It also creates pressure to reduce refusal aggressiveness, which widens the alignment gap. Competing objective jailbreaks (Section 7.11) explicitly exploit this tension: they frame the refusal as more harmful than compliance, pressuring the model's helpfulness objective to override its safety objective. Second: sycophancy. RLHF reward models are trained on human preferences. Human labellers consistently rate agreeable, validating responses more highly than correct-but-challenging ones. The policy model learns to be agreeable. Sycophancy is a systematic bias toward confirming user beliefs and accommodating user requests -- including adversarial ones. Context manipulation jailbreaks (Section 7.5) exploit sycophancy directly: they establish premises the model agrees with, then leverage that agreement toward harmful conclusions. Both over-refusal and sycophancy are documented properties of RLHF-trained models. They are not bugs -- they are predictable consequences of the training objective. Adversaries who understand the training process can engineer inputs that exploit them.

7.2.8

Title

Why Alignment Cannot Be a Hard Constraint: The Entanglement Problem

Body

A natural question: why not train the model so that harmful outputs are literally impossible -- zero probability, not just low probability? The answer is that harmful and beneficial capabilities are entangled in the weight space. Consider: the capability to explain chemistry is the same capability whether the chemistry being explained is industrial synthesis or medication dosing. The capability to write persuasively is the same capability whether the persuasion is a marketing email or a social engineering script. The capability to reason about human psychology is the same capability whether the application is therapy or manipulation. Setting the probability of harmful outputs to zero would require the model to lose capabilities that are identical at the weight level to the harmful ones. You cannot surgically remove the path to harmful chemistry explanation without removing the path to beneficial chemistry explanation -- they pass through the same weights. This is the entanglement problem, and it is why alignment is a boundary- adjustment technique rather than a capability-removal technique. The aligned model is the same model as the unaligned one, with the output distribution shifted away from harm-adjacent regions. The shift is robust against typical inputs. It is not robust against adversarially engineered inputs that approach harm-adjacent regions from the direction of the non-harmful capabilities they share weight paths with. The security implication is the most important point in this section: alignment is not and cannot be a hard security boundary. It is a probabilistic filter. Hard security boundaries must be implemented at the infrastructure layer -- input classifiers, output monitors, rate limiters, policy enforcement that operates independent of the model's own learned behaviour. This is not a failure of alignment research. It is a structural property of the architecture that your detection system must account for.

Number

7.2.9

Title

Infrastructure Detection: The Necessary Conclusion

Body

Everything in this section leads to one operational conclusion: you cannot rely on the model to detect attacks against itself. The aligned model refuses harmful requests because it has learned to assign low probability to harmful outputs. A jailbreak moves the input into a region where that probability assignment changes. At the moment the jailbreak succeeds, the model's own safety training is contributing to the problem -- the model believes it is behaving correctly within the framing the adversary has established. It is generating harmful content while being maximally confident it is behaving appropriately. This is not a hypothetical. Successful jailbreaks produce outputs where the model shows no hedging, no refusal indicators, no safety caveats -- because from the model's perspective, operating within the adversarial framing, the output is appropriate. The model has been moved to a region of prompt space where its refusal probability is genuinely low. The detection system must therefore operate outside the model's inference path. Input classifiers examine what goes in before the model sees it. Output monitors examine what comes out, independent of whether the model thought it was appropriate. Session-level trackers observe patterns across turns that no single-turn model inference can see. None of these depend on the model's own alignment training -- they are external constraints on a probabilistic filter that adversaries can and do shift. This is the architecture Section 7.14 builds. The jailbreak ensemble is an independent signal path. It does not ask the model whether an input is a jailbreak attempt. It uses external classifiers and statistical detectors to make that determination before the model processes the input at all.

Refusal Probability Distribution Visualiser

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt


def simulate_output_distribution(
    mean_harmful: float,
    std: float = 0.15,
    n_outputs: int = 1000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> np.ndarray:
    '''
    Simulate a model output distribution over a [0, 1] harmful-content axis.
    0 = fully harmless output. 1 = fully harmful output.
    Returns array of simulated output positions on this axis.
    '''
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    raw = rng.normal(loc=mean_harmful, scale=std, size=n_outputs)
    return np.clip(raw, 0.0, 1.0)


def plot_alignment_effect():
    '''
    Show three distributions on the harmful-content axis:
    1. Pre-trained base model: centred toward producing content that
       reflects training corpus distribution (including harmful content).
    2. RLHF-aligned model: distribution shifted left -- harmful outputs
       are low probability, but the distribution still has a tail above
       the jailbreak threshold.
    3. Jailbreak success: adversarial input shifts the effective mean
       back toward harmful region even for the aligned model.
    '''
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 4), sharey=True)

    scenarios = [
        {
            'label': 'Pre-trained base model\n(no alignment)',
            'mean': 0.5,
            'color': '#d62728',
            'jailbreak_threshold': None,
        },
        {
            'label': 'RLHF-aligned model\n(normal operation)',
            'mean': 0.15,
            'color': '#2ca02c',
            'jailbreak_threshold': 0.6,
        },
        {
            'label': 'RLHF-aligned model\n(under jailbreak)',
            'mean': 0.45,
            'color': '#ff7f0e',
            'jailbreak_threshold': 0.6,
        },
    ]

    harmful_threshold = 0.6

    for ax, scenario in zip(axes, scenarios):
        outputs = simulate_output_distribution(scenario['mean'])
        ax.hist(outputs, bins=50, density=True, color=scenario['color'],
                alpha=0.7, edgecolor='white', linewidth=0.3)
        ax.axvline(x=harmful_threshold, color='red', linestyle='--',
                   linewidth=2, label=f'Harmful threshold ({harmful_threshold})')
        ax.set_xlabel('Harmful content score (0=harmless, 1=harmful)')
        ax.set_ylabel('Probability density' if ax == axes[0] else '')
        ax.set_title(scenario['label'])
        ax.set_xlim(0, 1)

        p_harmful = np.mean(outputs >= harmful_threshold)
        ax.text(0.65, ax.get_ylim()[1] * 0.85,
                f'P(harmful) = {p_harmful:.3f}',
                fontsize=10, color='red')
        ax.legend(fontsize=8)

    plt.suptitle(
        'Alignment as distribution shift, not capability removal\n'
        'Jailbreaks move the effective input into a higher-harm region',
        fontsize=12, y=1.02,
    )
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig('alignment_distribution_effect.png', dpi=150,
                bbox_inches='tight')
    print('Saved: alignment_distribution_effect.png')
    print()
    print('Key observation:')
    print('  Base model:        P(harmful) approx 0.50')
    print('  Aligned (normal):  P(harmful) approx 0.02')
    print('  Aligned (jailbreak): P(harmful) approx 0.40')
    print()
    print('Alignment reduced P(harmful) by ~96% in normal operation.')
    print('Jailbreak restored P(harmful) to ~80% of base model level.')
    print('The capability was never removed -- only suppressed.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    plot_alignment_effect()

'''
Alignment removal via fine-tuning: conceptual sketch.

This demonstrates the STRUCTURE of the attack -- not a working implementation.
Purpose: understand what adversaries do so you can detect and defend against it.

The attack requires:
  1. Access to open-weight model checkpoint
  2. Dataset of uncensored (harmful) completions
  3. Standard fine-tuning loop (identical to SFT phase of RLHF)

The defence implications:
  1. Closed-model APIs are not directly vulnerable (no weight access)
  2. Open-weight models with removable alignment are used to develop
     transferable jailbreaks (GCG suffixes) that work on closed models
  3. Detection must operate at the API layer, not rely on alignment
'''

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Callable


@dataclass
class AlignmentRemovalConfig:
    base_model_checkpoint: str
    uncensored_dataset_path: str
    n_training_steps: int = 500       # Low step count is sufficient
    learning_rate: float = 2e-5
    batch_size: int = 4
    max_seq_len: int = 512
    target_loss: float = 1.5          # Approximate convergence signal


def describe_alignment_removal_threat(config: AlignmentRemovalConfig) -> dict:
    '''
    Describe what happens during alignment removal fine-tuning.
    Returns a structured threat description, not a training execution.
    '''
    return {
        'phase': 'adversarial_fine_tuning',
        'mechanism': (
            'Standard SFT training loop on uncensored completions. '
            'Gradient descent on cross-entropy loss over harmful outputs. '
            'Identical to the SFT phase that originally instilled instruction '
            'following -- run on a different objective.'
        ),
        'weight_effect': (
            'Output distribution shifts back toward pre-training state. '
            'RLHF-instilled refusal behaviour is overwritten in the weights '
            'that determine output token probabilities for harm-adjacent inputs. '
            'Pre-training capability is intact throughout -- it was never removed.'
        ),
        'steps_required': config.n_training_steps,
        'dataset_size_sufficient': '500 to 5000 examples (published research)',
        'result': (
            'Model retains full pre-training capability and instruction-following '
            'ability. Refusal behaviour is removed. Alignment tax is reversed. '
            'Model answers harmful requests that the base aligned model refused.'
        ),
        'transferability': (
            'GCG adversarial suffixes generated against this fine-tuned model '
            'transfer to closed-source API models at non-trivial success rates '
            '(Zou et al. 2023). Open-weight alignment removal enables development '
            'of closed-model attacks.'
        ),
        'defender_response': (
            'Closed-model API deployments: not directly vulnerable. '
            'Indirect threat via transferable suffixes -- deploy perplexity '
            'detector (Section 7.9) to catch GCG-generated inputs regardless '
            'of the model they were optimised against. '
            'Open-weight deployments: treat as unaligned -- infrastructure '
            'detection is the only reliable defence.'
        ),
    }


def alignment_removal_risk_assessment(
    deployment_type: str,
) -> list:
    '''
    Return risk items relevant to alignment removal for a given deployment type.
    deployment_type: 'closed_api', 'open_weight_self_hosted', 'fine_tuned_on_custom_data'
    '''
    risks = {
        'closed_api': [
            'LOW: No direct weight access. Alignment removal not possible by external adversary.',
            'MEDIUM: Transferable GCG suffixes from open-weight fine-tuning can bypass alignment.',
            'ACTION: Deploy perplexity detector (Section 7.9) on all user inputs.',
        ],
        'open_weight_self_hosted': [
            'HIGH: Alignment removal via fine-tuning is straightforward with weight access.',
            'HIGH: Internal adversary with GPU access can strip alignment in hours.',
            'HIGH: Published techniques (QLoRA on 500 examples) are commodity.',
            'ACTION: Treat model-level alignment as untrusted. Infrastructure detection is mandatory.',
            'ACTION: Audit all fine-tuning jobs against base model refusal behaviour.',
        ],
        'fine_tuned_on_custom_data': [
            'MEDIUM-HIGH: Custom fine-tuning may have inadvertently degraded alignment.',
            'MEDIUM: Fine-tuning on domain data shifts distribution, may erode refusal coverage.',
            'ACTION: Run alignment regression tests after every fine-tuning run.',
            'ACTION: Test refusal behaviour on a standard harmful prompt benchmark post-fine-tune.',
        ],
    }
    return risks.get(deployment_type, ['UNKNOWN deployment type -- assess manually.'])


if __name__ == '__main__':
    config = AlignmentRemovalConfig(
        base_model_checkpoint='meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-chat-hf',
        uncensored_dataset_path='data/uncensored_completions.jsonl',
    )

    threat = describe_alignment_removal_threat(config)
    print('=== Alignment Removal Threat Description ===')
    for k, v in threat.items():
        print(f'\n{k.upper()}:')
        print(f'  {v}')

    print('\n\n=== Risk Assessment by Deployment Type ===')
    for dtype in ['closed_api', 'open_weight_self_hosted', 'fine_tuned_on_custom_data']:
        print(f'\n{dtype.upper()}:')
        for risk in alignment_removal_risk_assessment(dtype):
            print(f'  {risk}')

Key Takeaways

  • RLHF does not remove capabilities from model weights. It shifts the output probability distribution away from harmful outputs. The capability substrate from pre-training is intact beneath the alignment.
  • Every jailbreak technique is an instance of distribution shift: moving the input into a region of prompt space where the alignment training distribution is sparse, conflicted, or pattern-matched incorrectly.
  • Reward model hacking means adversarial outputs can satisfy the RLHF proxy while violating the original human intent. Output-side safety filters are not a complete defence.
  • Constitutional AI and RLAIF improve alignment coverage and robustness. They do not eliminate the fundamental fragility: the capability substrate is unchanged, and inference-time mechanisms like many-shot learning operate outside the scope of any alignment training technique.
  • Fine-tuning alignment removal on open-weight models strips RLHF in hours with commodity hardware. Closed-model deployments face this as an indirect threat via transferable GCG suffixes, not a direct one.
  • Alignment cannot be a hard constraint: capability and safety share weight paths. The security boundary must be at the infrastructure layer -- input classifiers, output monitors, session trackers that operate independent of the model's own learned behaviour.
