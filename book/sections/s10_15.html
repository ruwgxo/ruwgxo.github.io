Chapter 10 · Section 15 · ~6 pages

Adversarial Training: Upstream Empirical Robustness

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Adversarial training makes the model itself harder to fool by training on its own worst-case inputs -- the strongest empirical defence against the Chapter 10 attacks, paid for in training compute, clean accuracy, and a robustness claim that is only as good as the evaluation behind it.

Why This Section Exists

Track 7 detects adversarial examples after the model is deployed. Section 10.14 certifies a narrow L2 threat model at a real accuracy and inference cost. Neither changes how many adversarial examples exist for the model in the first place. Adversarial training does: it moves the decision boundary away from the data so that small perturbations no longer cross it. It is the defence that survived the 2017-2018 wave of broken defences, and every later method in this section is a refinement of it. It also comes with the most ways to fool yourself. Single-step training can collapse while its own metrics look healthy, the final epoch is often not the most robust one, and a model hardened for one norm can be weaker in another. This section builds the training loops and the evaluator and checklist that keep the robustness claim honest.

Position In Chapter

Section 10.4 built PGD and the PGDConfig training and evaluation presets; this section wraps them in the min-max training loop. Section 10.14 gave the provable but narrow guarantee; this section gives the broad but unprovable one. Section 10.16 combines both with Track 7.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

The Min-Max Objective

Body

Section 10.4 introduced the robust optimisation objective of Madry, Makelov, Schmidt, Tsipras and Vladu (2018): min_theta E_(x,y)~D [ max_{||delta|| <= epsilon} L(f_theta(x + delta), y) ] Adversarial training is the direct implementation. For each minibatch, solve the inner maximisation approximately with PGD to get the worst-case perturbed inputs, then take an ordinary gradient step on the loss at those inputs. The attacker moves first; the model learns from the attacker's best move. Two implementation details matter more than they look. First, the inner attack runs against the current weights in evaluation mode. In training mode, batch normalisation updates its running statistics on every PGD step, so the statistics end up describing adversarial batches rather than the data. adversarial_trainer.py switches to eval for the attack and back to train for the update. Second, the attack configuration for training and for evaluation must differ. Training uses PGDConfig.madry_training (k = 7, alpha = 2 * epsilon / 3, one restart) because the inner loop runs on every batch. Evaluation uses PGDConfig.evaluation_grade (k = 40, five restarts) because evaluation is where the claim is made. On CIFAR-10 at epsilon = 8/255 this recipe gives roughly 87% clean accuracy and 45% PGD robust accuracy, against about 95% clean and near-zero robust accuracy for standard training. That gap -- eight points of clean accuracy for forty points of robustness -- is the trade the rest of this section manages.

Part 2

Title

Why Robustness Costs Accuracy: Robust and Non-Robust Features

Body

Tsipras et al. 2019 showed that the accuracy cost is not an artefact of training difficulty. It can be built into the data. The deliverables in this section use their construction: one feature that agrees with the label 90% of the time and is far from the boundary, plus fifty features that are each only weakly correlated with the label but together predict it almost perfectly. A standard model learns the fifty weak features, because they give near-perfect accuracy. Each weak feature is only slightly shifted by the label, so an L-infinity perturbation a little larger than that shift erases all fifty at once. In the adversarial_trainer.py demo the standard model scores 99.9% clean and 7.9% under PGD. The adversarially trained model learns that the weak features cannot be trusted, falls back to the robust feature, and scores 89.8% clean and 89.8% under PGD. Its clean accuracy is capped by the robust feature's 90% agreement rate. That cap is the accuracy-robustness trade-off in its simplest form. Ilyas et al. 2019 argued that real image datasets contain many such non-robust features: genuinely predictive patterns that humans do not perceive and that small perturbations can flip. On that view adversarial examples are not bugs in the model -- they are the model using every feature it was given. Adversarial training removes the non-robust features from what the model may use, and with them some accuracy.

Part 3

Title

Single-Step Training: FGSM, Free and Fast

Body

PGD training costs about k + 1 forward-backward passes per batch, so k = 7 makes training roughly eight times slower. The obvious shortcut is the single-step attack. Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy (2015) trained on FGSM examples mixed 50/50 with clean examples. It improved robustness to FGSM, but Kurakin et al. 2017 and Tramer et al. 2018 found that single-step training from the clean input mostly teaches the model to make its own gradient misleading near the data -- gradient masking. Such models resist FGSM, fall to PGD, and are often more vulnerable to transfer attacks from an undefended surrogate than to white-box attacks on themselves. Two methods made cheap adversarial training work. Free adversarial training (Shafahi et al. 2019) replays each minibatch several times and reuses the single backward pass of each replay both to update the weights and to update the perturbation, reaching PGD-like robustness at close to standard training cost. Fast adversarial training (Wong, Rice and Kolter 2020) showed that plain FGSM works if the step starts from a random point in the epsilon ball and uses a step of about 1.25 * epsilon. Fast training has a characteristic failure: catastrophic overfitting. Within a single epoch, robust accuracy against PGD can drop to near zero while accuracy against FGSM stays high. The model has learned to defeat the single-step linear approximation rather than to be robust. The defence is cheap: evaluate PGD on a small held-out batch every epoch and roll back on a sharp drop. fgsm_adversarial_trainer.py implements that guard. Random initialisation plus the guard is what makes single-step training usable; without the guard, the training metrics will not tell you it has failed.

Part 4

Title

TRADES: Separating Accuracy from Robustness

Body

Madry training replaces the clean loss with the adversarial loss, so accuracy and robustness are traded implicitly. TRADES (Zhang et al. 2019) makes the trade explicit: L = CE(f(x), y) + beta * max_{||x' - x|| <= epsilon} KL(f(x) || f(x')) The first term is ordinary classification accuracy. The second term penalises how much the prediction can change inside the epsilon ball -- the smoothness of the model around each input. The inner maximisation does not use the label at all: it pushes the prediction away from the model's own clean prediction. beta sets the balance. beta = 0 is standard training; larger beta buys robustness with clean accuracy. trades_trainer.py sweeps beta on the synthetic task. beta = 0 reproduces the standard model (100% clean, about 10% robust), beta = 0.1 buys a little robustness, and beta = 1 already reaches the robust-feature solution. On CIFAR-10 the paper used beta = 6, and TRADES won the NeurIPS 2018 Adversarial Vision Challenge. The production lesson is that beta is a business decision -- how much clean accuracy the deployment owner will give up -- and should be set and signed off as one, not copied from a paper.

Part 5

Title

Robust Overfitting and Model Selection

Body

Rice, Wong and Kolter (2020) found that adversarially trained networks overfit in a way standard networks largely do not: robust test accuracy peaks partway through training and then declines, often by several points, while robust training accuracy keeps rising. Training to the final epoch and shipping the result is therefore a mistake. Simple early stopping on held-out robust accuracy recovered most of the gains attributed to later algorithmic improvements. adversarial_trainer.py evaluates robust accuracy on a validation set every epoch with the evaluation-grade PGD config, keeps the best checkpoint, and stops after a patience window. The cost is one evaluation-grade PGD pass over the validation set per epoch -- small next to the training cost, and the only way to know which checkpoint is the robust one.

Part 6

Title

Evaluating a Hardened Model Honestly

Body

Adversarial training changes the model's gradients, so every evaluation trap from Sections 10.4 and 10.5 applies with extra force. adversarial_training_evaluator.py runs five checks on the same test set. Clean accuracy measures the cost. FGSM and evaluation-grade PGD measure robustness; the FGSM to PGD gap is the Section 10.4 gradient-masking diagnostic. Transfer accuracy uses PGD examples crafted on a separately trained standard model: if transfer attacks succeed more often than white-box PGD on the hardened model, its gradients are masked and the white-box number cannot be trusted. The C&W column measures the median L2 norm needed to flip a correctly classified input. The C&W column carries the section's most important warning. On the synthetic task the L-infinity-hardened model needs a smaller L2 perturbation to flip than the standard model does. It relies on one robust feature, and an L2 perturbation concentrated on that one feature is cheap. Robustness is norm-specific: training against L-infinity at one epsilon buys robustness against L-infinity at that epsilon and does not certify anything else. The evaluation must cover every norm the threat model names. For publication-grade results, AutoAttack (Croce and Hein 2020) -- an ensemble of parameter-free attacks -- and the RobustBench leaderboard are the current standard; they exist because many published defences were later found to be over-reported by weaker evaluations.

Part 7

Title

Adversarial Training for Text and LLM Classifiers

Body

For image models the inner maximisation is a continuous optimisation. For text it is the discrete search of Section 10.9, which is far more expensive: running TextFooler or HotFlip inside every training batch multiplies cost by the number of queries each attack needs. Two practical patterns exist. Augmentation generates adversarial text offline with the Section 10.9 attacks and adds it to the training set; it is cheap but only covers the attacks used. Embedding space training (FreeLB, Zhu et al. 2020) applies PGD to the continuous token embeddings instead of the tokens; it improves robustness to small perturbations but the embedding-space perturbation does not correspond to any real text an attacker can type. For LLM safety the same min-max idea has been applied against optimised jailbreak suffixes: HarmBench (Mazeika et al. 2024) trained a model against GCG-style attacks (Section 7.9) during fine-tuning and reported large gains against that attack. The lesson from images carries over: robustness transfers poorly to attack families the training did not include. For LLM classifiers adversarial training is a hardening step, not a guarantee, and Track 7 together with the Chapter 7 jailbreak tracks stay in front of it.

Part 8

Title

Positioning: The Two-Surface Defence

Body

Adversarial training is the upstream control. It reduces the fraction of inputs for which an adversarial example exists within the defended budget. Track 7 is the runtime control. It catches adversarial examples that survive into production, including those outside the defended norm or epsilon. Chapter 8 used the same pattern for poisoning and Chapter 9 for extraction: differential privacy training upstream, monitoring and output controls at runtime. The two layers also make each other better. An adversarially trained model has smoother decision boundaries, so Track 7's consistency checker sees fewer natural inputs near the boundary and its false positive rate falls. The checklist in this section ends with one operational step that is easy to miss: adversarial training moves the decision boundary, so every threshold set on the old model -- in the model's own decision layer and in Track 7 -- must be recalibrated on the hardened one.

PGD Adversarial Trainer

# adversarial_trainer.py
# Section 10.15: Adversarial Training -- PGD adversarial training (Madry et al. 2018)
# 20260608 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import copy
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Tuple

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch import Tensor

from pgd_attack import pgd_linf
from pgd_training_data_generator import PGDConfig


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Synthetic task with one robust feature and many non-robust features
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(n: int, n_weak: int = 50, p_robust: float = 0.9,
                                  eta: float = 0.064, noise: float = 0.15,
                                  seed: int = 0) -> Tuple[Tensor, Tensor]:
    '''
    Binary task in [0, 1] after Tsipras et al. 2019.
    Feature 0 (robust): 0.5 +/- 0.25, agrees with the label with probability p_robust.
    Features 1..n_weak (non-robust): 0.5 +/- eta plus Gaussian noise. Each is weak,
    but together they predict the label almost perfectly -- and an L-inf perturbation
    of a little more than eta erases all of them at once.
    '''
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    y = torch.randint(0, 2, (n,), generator=g)
    sign = (2 * y - 1).float().unsqueeze(1)
    agree = (torch.rand(n, 1, generator=g) < p_robust).float() * 2 - 1
    robust = 0.5 + 0.25 * sign * agree
    weak = 0.5 + eta * sign + noise * torch.randn(n, n_weak, generator=g)
    return torch.cat([robust, weak], dim=1).clamp(0.0, 1.0), y


class ToyMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_features: int = 51, n_classes: int = 2, hidden: int = 64):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(n_features, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, n_classes),
        )

    def forward(self, x: Tensor) -> Tensor:
        return self.net(x.view(x.size(0), -1))


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Training
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

@dataclass
class AdvTrainConfig:
    train_pgd: PGDConfig             # inner maximisation: PGDConfig.madry_training(eps)
    eval_pgd: PGDConfig              # model selection: PGDConfig.evaluation_grade(eps)
    epochs: int = 30
    lr: float = 1e-2
    batch_size: int = 128
    clean_weight: float = 0.0        # 0.0 = pure Madry; 0.5 = Goodfellow-style 50/50 mix
    patience: int = 8                # epochs without robust-accuracy improvement before stopping


@dataclass
class EpochLog:
    epoch: int
    train_loss: float
    val_clean_accuracy: float
    val_robust_accuracy: float


@dataclass
class TrainingResult:
    model: nn.Module
    best_epoch: int
    logs: List[EpochLog] = field(default_factory=list)


def clean_accuracy(model: nn.Module, x: Tensor, y: Tensor) -> float:
    model.eval()
    with torch.no_grad():
        return (model(x).argmax(1) == y).float().mean().item()


def robust_accuracy(model: nn.Module, x: Tensor, y: Tensor, cfg: PGDConfig) -> float:
    model.eval()
    x_adv = pgd_linf(model, x, y, epsilon=cfg.epsilon, alpha=cfg.alpha, n_steps=cfg.n_steps,
                     n_restarts=cfg.n_restarts, clip_min=cfg.clip_min, clip_max=cfg.clip_max)
    return clean_accuracy(model, x_adv, y)


def train_epoch(model: nn.Module, opt: torch.optim.Optimizer, x: Tensor, y: Tensor,
                cfg: AdvTrainConfig) -> float:
    loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
    perm = torch.randperm(x.size(0))
    total, batches = 0.0, 0
    for i in range(0, x.size(0), cfg.batch_size):
        idx = perm[i:i + cfg.batch_size]
        xb, yb = x[idx], y[idx]

        # Inner maximisation. Attack the CURRENT weights in eval mode so batch-norm
        # statistics are not updated by adversarial batches mid-attack.
        model.eval()
        x_adv = pgd_linf(model, xb, yb, epsilon=cfg.train_pgd.epsilon, alpha=cfg.train_pgd.alpha,
                         n_steps=cfg.train_pgd.n_steps, n_restarts=cfg.train_pgd.n_restarts,
                         clip_min=cfg.train_pgd.clip_min, clip_max=cfg.train_pgd.clip_max)

        # Outer minimisation.
        model.train()
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = (1 - cfg.clean_weight) * loss_fn(model(x_adv), yb)
        if cfg.clean_weight > 0:
            loss = loss + cfg.clean_weight * loss_fn(model(xb), yb)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
        total += loss.item()
        batches += 1
    return total / batches


def adversarial_train(model: nn.Module, x_train: Tensor, y_train: Tensor,
                      x_val: Tensor, y_val: Tensor, cfg: AdvTrainConfig) -> TrainingResult:
    '''
    Madry min-max training with model selection on held-out ROBUST accuracy.
    Robust overfitting (Rice et al. 2020) means the last epoch is often not the
    most robust one: keep the best checkpoint, not the final weights.
    '''
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=cfg.lr)
    best_state, best_robust, best_epoch = copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict()), -1.0, 0
    logs: List[EpochLog] = []
    for epoch in range(1, cfg.epochs + 1):
        loss = train_epoch(model, opt, x_train, y_train, cfg)
        log = EpochLog(epoch, loss, clean_accuracy(model, x_val, y_val),
                       robust_accuracy(model, x_val, y_val, cfg.eval_pgd))
        logs.append(log)
        if log.val_robust_accuracy > best_robust:
            best_robust, best_epoch = log.val_robust_accuracy, epoch
            best_state = copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict())
        elif epoch - best_epoch >= cfg.patience:
            break
    model.load_state_dict(best_state)
    model.eval()
    return TrainingResult(model, best_epoch, logs)


def standard_train(model: nn.Module, x: Tensor, y: Tensor, epochs: int = 30,
                   lr: float = 1e-2, batch_size: int = 128) -> nn.Module:
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
    model.train()
    for _ in range(epochs):
        perm = torch.randperm(x.size(0))
        for i in range(0, x.size(0), batch_size):
            idx = perm[i:i + batch_size]
            opt.zero_grad()
            loss_fn(model(x[idx]), y[idx]).backward()
            opt.step()
    model.eval()
    return model


def demo() -> None:
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    eps = 0.1
    x_train, y_train = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(4000, seed=0)
    x_val, y_val = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(500, seed=1)
    x_test, y_test = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(1000, seed=2)
    eval_cfg = PGDConfig.evaluation_grade(eps)

    standard = standard_train(ToyMLP(), x_train, y_train)
    cfg = AdvTrainConfig(train_pgd=PGDConfig.madry_training(eps), eval_pgd=eval_cfg)
    result = adversarial_train(ToyMLP(), x_train, y_train, x_val, y_val, cfg)

    print(f'PGD adversarial training demo -- L-inf eps={eps}, robust feature agrees 90% of the time')
    print(f'{"model":<22}{"clean acc":<12}{"PGD-40x5 acc":<14}')
    for name, model in (('standard', standard), (f'Madry (epoch {result.best_epoch})', result.model)):
        print(f'{name:<22}{clean_accuracy(model, x_test, y_test):<12.3f}'
              f'{robust_accuracy(model, x_test, y_test, eval_cfg):<14.3f}')
    print('\nThe standard model reads the non-robust features and is near-perfect until attacked.')
    print('The adversarially trained model gives up that accuracy for the robust feature alone.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    demo()

# fgsm_adversarial_trainer.py
# Section 10.15: Adversarial Training -- fast FGSM training with a catastrophic-overfitting guard
# 20260608 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import copy
import time
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch import Tensor

from adversarial_trainer import (
    ToyMLP, clean_accuracy, make_robust_nonrobust_dataset, robust_accuracy,
)
from pgd_training_data_generator import PGDConfig


@dataclass
class FastATConfig:
    epsilon: float
    alpha_ratio: float = 1.25        # Wong et al. 2020: step of 1.25 * epsilon from a random start
    random_init: bool = True         # False reproduces zero-init FGSM training (Goodfellow et al. 2015)
    epochs: int = 30
    lr: float = 1e-2
    batch_size: int = 128
    collapse_drop: float = 0.20      # PGD-accuracy drop that signals catastrophic overfitting
    clip_min: float = 0.0
    clip_max: float = 1.0


@dataclass
class FastATResult:
    model: nn.Module
    gradient_passes: int             # forward-backward passes spent on the inner maximisation
    seconds: float
    collapsed_at_epoch: int = 0      # 0 means no catastrophic overfitting detected
    pgd_history: List[float] = field(default_factory=list)


def fgsm_example(model: nn.Module, x: Tensor, y: Tensor, cfg: FastATConfig) -> Tensor:
    if cfg.random_init:
        delta = torch.empty_like(x).uniform_(-cfg.epsilon, cfg.epsilon)
    else:
        delta = torch.zeros_like(x)
    delta = (torch.clamp(x + delta, cfg.clip_min, cfg.clip_max) - x).requires_grad_(True)
    loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(model(x + delta), y)
    grad, = torch.autograd.grad(loss, delta)
    with torch.no_grad():
        delta = delta + cfg.alpha_ratio * cfg.epsilon * grad.sign()
        delta = torch.clamp(delta, -cfg.epsilon, cfg.epsilon)
        return torch.clamp(x + delta, cfg.clip_min, cfg.clip_max)


def fast_adversarial_train(model: nn.Module, x: Tensor, y: Tensor, x_val: Tensor, y_val: Tensor,
                           cfg: FastATConfig) -> FastATResult:
    '''
    One gradient step per example instead of PGD's seven. Cheap, but it can
    collapse: robust accuracy against PGD falls to near zero within an epoch while
    accuracy against FGSM stays high, because the model learns to make the
    single-step linear approximation useless rather than becoming robust.
    Guard: evaluate PGD on a held-out batch every epoch and roll back on a sharp drop.
    '''
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=cfg.lr)
    loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
    guard = PGDConfig(epsilon=cfg.epsilon, alpha=cfg.epsilon / 4, n_steps=10,
                      clip_min=cfg.clip_min, clip_max=cfg.clip_max)
    best_state, best_pgd = copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict()), -1.0
    result = FastATResult(model, 0, 0.0)
    start = time.perf_counter()
    for epoch in range(1, cfg.epochs + 1):
        perm = torch.randperm(x.size(0))
        for i in range(0, x.size(0), cfg.batch_size):
            idx = perm[i:i + cfg.batch_size]
            model.eval()
            x_adv = fgsm_example(model, x[idx], y[idx], cfg)
            result.gradient_passes += 1
            model.train()
            opt.zero_grad()
            loss_fn(model(x_adv), y[idx]).backward()
            opt.step()
        pgd_acc = robust_accuracy(model, x_val, y_val, guard)
        result.pgd_history.append(pgd_acc)
        if pgd_acc > best_pgd:
            best_pgd, best_state = pgd_acc, copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict())
        elif best_pgd - pgd_acc > cfg.collapse_drop:
            result.collapsed_at_epoch = epoch
            break
    result.seconds = time.perf_counter() - start
    model.load_state_dict(best_state)
    model.eval()
    return result


def demo() -> None:

    eps = 0.1
    x_train, y_train = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(4000, seed=0)
    x_val, y_val = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(500, seed=1)
    x_test, y_test = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(1000, seed=2)
    eval_cfg = PGDConfig.evaluation_grade(eps)

    print(f'{"variant":<29}{"clean":<8}{"PGD-40x5":<10}{"inner passes":<14}{"seconds":<9}{"collapse":<9}')

TRADES Trainer

Filename

trades_trainer.py

Description

TRADES objective with label-free KL inner maximisation and a beta sweep showing the clean-robust trade-off

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires: torch. The inner attack starts from x plus small Gaussian noise because the KL gradient is exactly zero at x' = x.

Code

# trades_trainer.py
# Section 10.15: Adversarial Training -- TRADES (Zhang et al. 2019)
# 20260608 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch import Tensor

from adversarial_trainer import (
    ToyMLP, clean_accuracy, make_robust_nonrobust_dataset, robust_accuracy,
)
from pgd_training_data_generator import PGDConfig


@dataclass
class TradesConfig:
    epsilon: float
    beta: float = 6.0                # weight on the robustness term; 6.0 is the paper's CIFAR-10 setting
    step_size: float = 0.0           # 0.0 -> epsilon / 4
    n_steps: int = 10
    epochs: int = 30
    lr: float = 1e-2
    batch_size: int = 128
    clip_min: float = 0.0
    clip_max: float = 1.0


def trades_adversary(model: nn.Module, x: Tensor, cfg: TradesConfig) -> Tensor:
    '''
    Inner maximisation of TRADES: find x' in the epsilon ball that maximises
    KL(f(x) || f(x')). Note there is no label here -- the adversary pushes the
    prediction AWAY FROM THE MODEL'S OWN clean prediction, not away from y.
    '''
    step = cfg.step_size or cfg.epsilon / 4
    with torch.no_grad():
        p_clean = F.softmax(model(x), dim=1)
    x_adv = (x + 0.001 * torch.randn_like(x)).clamp(cfg.clip_min, cfg.clip_max)
    for _ in range(cfg.n_steps):
        x_adv = x_adv.detach().requires_grad_(True)
        kl = F.kl_div(F.log_softmax(model(x_adv), dim=1), p_clean, reduction='sum')
        grad, = torch.autograd.grad(kl, x_adv)
        with torch.no_grad():
            x_adv = x_adv + step * grad.sign()
            x_adv = torch.min(torch.max(x_adv, x - cfg.epsilon), x + cfg.epsilon)
            x_adv = x_adv.clamp(cfg.clip_min, cfg.clip_max)
    return x_adv.detach()


def trades_loss(model: nn.Module, x: Tensor, x_adv: Tensor, y: Tensor, beta: float) -> Tensor:
    '''CE(f(x), y) + beta * KL(f(x) || f(x_adv)): accuracy term plus boundary-smoothness term.'''
    logits_clean = model(x)
    natural = F.cross_entropy(logits_clean, y)
    robust = F.kl_div(F.log_softmax(model(x_adv), dim=1), F.softmax(logits_clean, dim=1),
                      reduction='batchmean')
    return natural + beta * robust


def trades_train(model: nn.Module, x: Tensor, y: Tensor, cfg: TradesConfig) -> nn.Module:
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=cfg.lr)
    for _ in range(cfg.epochs):
        perm = torch.randperm(x.size(0))
        for i in range(0, x.size(0), cfg.batch_size):
            idx = perm[i:i + cfg.batch_size]
            model.eval()
            x_adv = trades_adversary(model, x[idx], cfg)
            model.train()
            opt.zero_grad()
            trades_loss(model, x[idx], x_adv, y[idx], cfg.beta).backward()
            opt.step()
    model.eval()
    return model


def demo() -> None:
    eps = 0.1
    x_train, y_train = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(4000, seed=0)
    x_test, y_test = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(1000, seed=2)
    eval_cfg = PGDConfig.evaluation_grade(eps)

    print(f'TRADES beta sweep -- L-inf eps={eps}')
    print(f'{"beta":<7}{"clean":<9}{"PGD-40x5":<10}')
    for beta in (0.0, 0.1, 1.0, 6.0):
        torch.manual_seed(0)
        model = trades_train(ToyMLP(), x_train, y_train, TradesConfig(epsilon=eps, beta=beta, epochs=15))
        print(f'{beta:<7}{clean_accuracy(model, x_test, y_test):<9.3f}'
              f'{robust_accuracy(model, x_test, y_test, eval_cfg):<10.3f}')
    print('\nbeta=0 is standard training. Raising beta trades clean accuracy for robustness;')
    print('beta is the dial the deployment owner sets, not a constant to copy from a paper.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    demo()

# adversarial_training_evaluator.py
# Section 10.15: Adversarial Training -- evaluate a hardened model against every Chapter 10 attack
# 20260608 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from dataclasses import dataclass

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch import Tensor

from adversarial_trainer import (
    AdvTrainConfig, ToyMLP, adversarial_train, clean_accuracy,
    make_robust_nonrobust_dataset, standard_train,
)
from cw_attack import cw_l2
from fgsm_attack import fgsm
from pgd_attack import pgd_linf
from pgd_training_data_generator import PGDConfig


@dataclass
class HardeningReport:
    name: str
    clean: float
    fgsm: float
    pgd: float                       # PGD-40, 5 restarts: the headline number
    transfer: float                  # PGD examples crafted on a separate standard surrogate
    cw_median_l2: float              # median L2 norm C&W needs to flip a correct prediction (higher = harder)
    masking_gap: float               # fgsm - pgd accuracy; large gap means obfuscated gradients


def evaluate(name: str, model: nn.Module, surrogate: nn.Module, x: Tensor, y: Tensor,
             epsilon: float) -> HardeningReport:
    model.eval()
    cfg = PGDConfig.evaluation_grade(epsilon)
    x_fgsm = fgsm(model, x, y, epsilon=epsilon)
    x_pgd = pgd_linf(model, x, y, epsilon=cfg.epsilon, alpha=cfg.alpha,
                     n_steps=cfg.n_steps, n_restarts=cfg.n_restarts)
    x_transfer = pgd_linf(surrogate, x, y, epsilon=cfg.epsilon, alpha=cfg.alpha,
                          n_steps=cfg.n_steps, n_restarts=cfg.n_restarts)
    x_cw = cw_l2(model, x, y, n_binary_search=5, n_steps=100)
    with torch.no_grad():
        # Only inputs the model got right to begin with: a misclassified input needs no perturbation.
        flipped = (model(x).argmax(1) == y) & (model(x_cw).argmax(1) != y)
        norms = (x_cw - x).view(x.size(0), -1).norm(dim=1)
        cw_l2_median = norms[flipped].median().item() if flipped.any() else float('inf')
    fgsm_acc, pgd_acc = clean_accuracy(model, x_fgsm, y), clean_accuracy(model, x_pgd, y)
    return HardeningReport(
        name=name,
        clean=clean_accuracy(model, x, y),
        fgsm=fgsm_acc,
        pgd=pgd_acc,
        transfer=clean_accuracy(model, x_transfer, y),
        cw_median_l2=cw_l2_median,
        masking_gap=fgsm_acc - pgd_acc,
    )


def print_reports(*reports: HardeningReport) -> None:
    print(f'{"model":<12}{"clean":<8}{"FGSM":<8}{"PGD":<8}{"transfer":<10}{"C&W med L2":<12}{"FGSM-PGD gap":<12}')
    for r in reports:
        flag = '  <- check for gradient masking' if r.masking_gap > 0.15 else ''
        print(f'{r.name:<12}{r.clean:<8.3f}{r.fgsm:<8.3f}{r.pgd:<8.3f}{r.transfer:<10.3f}'
              f'{r.cw_median_l2:<12.3f}{r.masking_gap:<12.3f}{flag}')


def demo() -> None:
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    eps = 0.1
    x_train, y_train = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(4000, seed=0)
    x_val, y_val = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(500, seed=1)
    x_test, y_test = make_robust_nonrobust_dataset(300, seed=2)

    surrogate = standard_train(ToyMLP(), x_train, y_train)        # attacker's own model
    standard = standard_train(ToyMLP(), x_train, y_train)         # victim, undefended
    cfg = AdvTrainConfig(train_pgd=PGDConfig.madry_training(eps), eval_pgd=PGDConfig.evaluation_grade(eps))
    hardened = adversarial_train(ToyMLP(), x_train, y_train, x_val, y_val, cfg).model

    print(f'Hardening report -- L-inf eps={eps}, {x_test.size(0)} test inputs')
    print_reports(
        evaluate('standard', standard, surrogate, x_test, y_test, eps),
        evaluate('madry', hardened, surrogate, x_test, y_test, eps),
    )
    print('\nPGD is the headline. Transfer must not beat PGD (if it does, white-box gradients are')
    print('masked). C&W measures a different norm: L-inf training buys L-inf robustness, and a')
    print('model that leans on one robust feature can be cheaper to flip in L2, not dearer.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    demo()

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Callable, List, Set


@dataclass
class HardeningEvidence:
    '''What the training run and adversarial_training_evaluator.py produced.'''
    eval_pgd_steps: int
    eval_pgd_restarts: int
    clean_accuracy_baseline: float       # undefended model
    clean_accuracy_hardened: float
    accuracy_drop_budget: float          # signed off by the model owner, e.g. 0.10
    fgsm_accuracy: float
    pgd_accuracy: float
    transfer_accuracy: float
    selected_on_robust_validation: bool  # checkpoint chosen by held-out robust accuracy
    threshold_recalibrated: bool         # operating threshold re-fit after hardening
    threat_norms: Set[str] = field(default_factory=set)      # norms in the threat model
    evaluated_norms: Set[str] = field(default_factory=set)   # norms the evaluator actually ran


@dataclass
class ChecklistItem:
    item_number: int
    gate: str
    remediation: str
    check: Callable[[HardeningEvidence], bool]


CHECKLIST: List[ChecklistItem] = [
    ChecklistItem(1, 'Robustness measured with evaluation-grade PGD (k >= 40, R >= 5)',
                  'Re-run with PGDConfig.evaluation_grade(eps); k=7 training PGD overstates robustness',
                  lambda e: e.eval_pgd_steps >= 40 and e.eval_pgd_restarts >= 5),
    ChecklistItem(2, 'No gradient masking: FGSM-PGD gap <= 0.15 and transfer accuracy >= PGD accuracy',
                  'Obfuscated gradients suspected; evaluate with C&W and BPDA (Section 10.5) before trusting PGD',
                  lambda e: e.fgsm_accuracy - e.pgd_accuracy <= 0.15 and e.transfer_accuracy >= e.pgd_accuracy),
    ChecklistItem(3, 'Clean accuracy drop within the signed-off budget',
                  'Lower beta (TRADES) or epsilon, or escalate the budget to the model owner',
                  lambda e: e.clean_accuracy_baseline - e.clean_accuracy_hardened <= e.accuracy_drop_budget),
    ChecklistItem(4, 'Checkpoint selected on held-out robust accuracy, not the final epoch',
                  'Robust overfitting: retrain with per-epoch robust validation and keep the best checkpoint',
                  lambda e: e.selected_on_robust_validation),
    ChecklistItem(5, 'Operating threshold recalibrated on the hardened model',
                  'Adversarial training moves the decision boundary; re-fit the threshold to the FPR target',
                  lambda e: e.threshold_recalibrated),
    ChecklistItem(6, 'Every norm in the threat model has been evaluated',
                  'Robustness is norm-specific; run the evaluator for each missing norm',
                  lambda e: e.threat_norms <= e.evaluated_norms),
]


def run_checklist(evidence: HardeningEvidence) -> bool:
    print('Adversarial Training Deployment Checklist')
    print('=' * 60)
    failed = 0
    for item in CHECKLIST:
        ok = item.check(evidence)
        failed += not ok
        print(f'[{"OK" if ok else "FAIL"}] Item {item.item_number}: {item.gate}')
        if not ok:
            print(f'       -> {item.remediation}')
    print()
    print(f'PASS {len(CHECKLIST) - failed}/{len(CHECKLIST)}')
    if failed:
        print('ACTION REQUIRED: do not ship the hardened model until every item passes.')
    return failed == 0

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Reporting robustness measured with the training attack

Why It Happens

The training loop already computes PGD accuracy on every batch with k = 7, so that number is logged and reported.

Consequence

k = 7 with one restart is a weak attacker. The reported robustness is inflated, sometimes by many points, and the model is shipped with a claim that evaluation-grade PGD would have refuted.

Correct Approach

Keep training and evaluation configs separate: PGDConfig.madry_training for the inner loop, PGDConfig.evaluation_grade (k = 40, five restarts) for every reported number. Checklist item 1 enforces this.

Mistake 2

Description

Shipping the final epoch

Why It Happens

Standard training habits: train for the scheduled epochs and save the last weights.

Consequence

Robust overfitting means robust test accuracy has often peaked and declined by the final epoch. The shipped model is less robust than a checkpoint the run already produced.

Correct Approach

Evaluate robust accuracy on held-out data every epoch and keep the best checkpoint, as adversarial_train does. Record the selected epoch in the model card.

Mistake 3

Description

Trusting fast FGSM training without a PGD guard

Why It Happens

Fast training reports FGSM accuracy, which stays high, and the run looks healthy.

Consequence

Catastrophic overfitting can drop PGD robustness to near zero within one epoch while FGSM accuracy stays high. Without a PGD check the collapsed model is the one that ships.

Correct Approach

Evaluate a short PGD attack on a held-out batch every epoch and roll back on a sharp drop, as fast_adversarial_train does. Always use random initialisation.

Mistake 4

Description

Generating adversarial examples in training mode

Why It Happens

The attack runs inside the training loop, so the model is already in train mode.

Consequence

Batch normalisation updates its running statistics on every PGD step, so after training the statistics describe adversarial batches. Dropout randomises the gradient the attack follows, producing weaker training examples. Clean and robust accuracy both suffer, and the cause is hard to see.

Correct Approach

Switch to eval mode to generate the attack and back to train mode for the weight update, as train_epoch does.

Mistake 5

Description

Claiming robustness beyond the norm and epsilon that were trained and evaluated

Why It Happens

"Adversarially trained" reads as a property of the model rather than of a specific threat model.

Consequence

An L-infinity-trained model can be weaker than the standard model under L2, as the evaluator demo shows. Attackers choose the norm; a robustness claim that does not name it invites the one that was not tested.

Correct Approach

Record norm and epsilon with every robustness number, evaluate every norm in the threat model, and let checklist item 6 block deployment until coverage is complete.

Section Summary

Compact

Adversarial training implements the Madry et al. min-max objective: PGD solves the inner maximisation on every batch and the weight update minimises the loss at those worst-case inputs. It is the strongest empirical defence against the Chapter 10 attacks and costs about eight times standard training at k = 7, plus clean accuracy -- a cost the robust/non-robust feature view explains: the model gives up predictive features that small perturbations can flip. Fast FGSM training with random initialisation matches PGD training at a fraction of the cost but can collapse catastrophically, so it needs a per-epoch PGD guard. TRADES makes the accuracy-robustness trade explicit through beta. Robust overfitting means checkpoints must be selected on held-out robust accuracy. A hardened model must be evaluated with evaluation-grade PGD, transfer and C&W, and across every norm in the threat model, because robustness is norm-specific. Adversarial training is the upstream surface of the Chapter 10 defence; Track 7 remains the runtime surface.

Key Takeaways

  • Train with k = 7 PGD, evaluate with k = 40 and five restarts; a robustness claim is only as strong as the evaluation
  • Generate the inner attack in eval mode and update weights in train mode
  • Select the checkpoint on held-out robust accuracy; the final epoch is often not the most robust
  • Fast FGSM training is viable only with random initialisation and a PGD guard against catastrophic overfitting
  • beta in TRADES is a business decision about clean accuracy, not a constant from a paper
  • Robustness is norm-specific: evaluate every norm in the threat model and recalibrate all thresholds after hardening
