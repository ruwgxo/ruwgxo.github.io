Transferability: Surrogate-Based Cross-Model Adversarial Examples

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Adversarial examples crafted against a surrogate fool the victim at 20-80% rates; the Chapter 9 distillation surrogate is the transfer vehicle, reducing victim query cost to near-zero after the extraction campaign.

Why This Section Exists

Section 10.7 is the mechanistic bridge that closes the Chapter 9 to Chapter 10 kill chain. Chapter 9 produced a surrogate model via distillation. Chapter 10 Sections 10.3-10.6 built adversarial attacks. Section 10.7 connects them: adversarial examples crafted against the surrogate with white-box gradient access transfer to the victim at rates determined by architecture and training distribution similarity. This is the cheapest and most practical black-box adversarial attack in production environments: the query cost to the victim is amortised across the Chapter 9 extraction campaign, leaving near-zero per-example cost.

Position In Chapter

Section 10.7 closes the attack sequence (Sections 10.3-10.7) and completes the extraction-to- evasion kill chain introduced in Section 10.1. Every subsequent defence section (10.11-10.15) must be evaluated against transfer attacks, not just direct white-box attacks, because transfer is the dominant black-box adversarial threat in production.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

Why Transferability Exists: Shared Decision Boundary Geometry

Body

Adversarial transferability was noted by Szegedy et al. in 2013 and systematised by Papernot et al. in 2016. The core observation is that adversarial examples crafted against one model often fool a completely different model trained on the same data, even when the two models have different architectures, different training hyperparameters, and were never exposed to each other''s weights. The explanation comes from the nature of generalisation. When many different models are trained on the same input-output distribution, they converge to similar input-output mappings in the regions of the input space where training data is dense. The decision boundaries of these models, while not identical, are geometrically aligned in high-data regions. An adversarial perturbation that crosses the source model''s boundary in a particular direction is likely to cross nearby boundaries of other models trained on the same distribution, because the boundary geometry is similar. The alignment is strongest in the direction of the gradient: the gradient of model A''s loss with respect to the input points in roughly the same direction as the gradient of model B''s loss, because both gradients point away from the decision boundary in the input space. FGSM''s gradient sign step exploits this alignment directly: sign(nabla_x J_A) is correlated with sign(nabla_x J_B), and a step along one is likely also a step along the other. The alignment degrades when models differ in architecture family, training objective, or training data. A ResNet and a VGG trained on ImageNet share more boundary geometry than a ResNet trained on ImageNet and a transformer fine-tuned on a domain-specific dataset. Transfer rate measurement (deliverable 1) quantifies this degradation across architecture pairs -- the output is the operational intelligence the adversary uses to evaluate whether their surrogate is a good enough transfer vehicle.

Part 2

Title

The Chapter 9 to Chapter 10 Kill Chain

Body

Transfer attacks depend on a prerequisite: the adversary must have a surrogate model that approximates the victim''s decision boundary. Section 9.4 established exactly this: the distillation pipeline queries the victim API, labels a surrogate training set with the victim''s outputs, and trains a surrogate that matches the victim''s behaviour. The surrogate is the Chapter 9 extraction product. Section 10.7 is its use as a Chapter 10 attack vehicle. The two-phase kill chain proceeds as follows. Phase 1 (Chapter 9): the adversary runs a distillation extraction campaign against the victim API. This requires 10,000-100,000 victim API queries and produces a surrogate model with white-box access. Phase 2 (Chapter 10): the adversary generates adversarial examples against the surrogate using any white-box method (FGSM, PGD, C&W). This requires no victim API queries -- only forward-backward passes against the local surrogate. The adversary then submits the resulting adversarial examples to the victim API -- typically one per target input, producing a near-zero per-example victim query cost. The total victim query cost for the transfer attack is: Phase 1 queries + Phase 2 queries. Phase 2 victim queries is zero. Phase 1 queries is the extraction budget, amortised across however many adversarial examples the adversary generates. If the adversary needs 1,000 adversarial examples, the per-example victim query cost is 10-100 queries (extraction budget divided by number of adversarial examples). This is orders of magnitude cheaper than running NES (1,000-10,000 queries per example) or Boundary Attack (10,000-100,000 per example) directly against the victim. The defence implication follows directly: blocking Chapter 9 extraction (Track 6 rate limiting, output watermarking, adaptive rate limits) reduces the adversary''s transfer attack capacity. Without a good surrogate, transfer rates fall from 40-80% to 10-30%. Defending against extraction is therefore partial defence against transfer attacks.

Part 3

Title

Transfer Rate Measurement and Influencing Factors

Body

Transfer rate is defined as the fraction of adversarial examples that fool the source model AND also fool the victim model. It is not the fraction of natural inputs for which an adversarial example can be transferred -- that requires first having a successful adversarial example against the source. The measurement is: generate N adversarial examples against the source, submit all N to the victim, count how many both fool the source and fool the victim. Four factors determine transfer rate in decreasing order of impact. Architecture family match is the strongest predictor. Same-family models (ResNet-50 to ResNet-101, VGG-16 to VGG-19) achieve 50-80% transfer. Cross-family models (ResNet to VGG, CNN to transformer) achieve 20-40%. The shared inductive biases within an architecture family produce more aligned gradient directions, even when model capacity differs. Training distribution match is the second factor. Models trained on exactly the same dataset (different random seeds or data orderings) transfer at higher rates than models trained on related but different datasets. The victim''s decision boundary is most similar to surrogates trained on the same distribution because the shared data determines what decision boundary is optimal. Attack method strength is the third factor. C&W adversarial examples with kappa>0 transfer at higher rates than FGSM examples at matched perturbation budget. The C&W kappa parameter forces a large margin between the adversarial class and all others -- this margin survives the slight geometric misalignment between source and victim boundaries. FGSM''s single-step examples sit right at the source boundary (minimal margin) and often fall back to the correct side of the victim''s slightly different boundary. Perturbation budget (epsilon) is the fourth factor. Larger epsilon generally produces higher transfer rates because larger perturbations are more likely to exceed the victim''s boundary even when the source and victim boundaries are not perfectly aligned. But larger epsilon is also more detectable by Track 7 feature squeezing -- this is the adversary''s trade-off.

Part 4

Title

Ensemble Transfer Attacks: Averaging Gradients to Reduce Boundary Overfitting

Body

Single-surrogate transfer attacks overfit to the source model''s specific boundary geometry. The adversarial example is optimised to be adversarial against one specific model -- and while the perturbation direction is likely aligned with the victim''s boundary, it is calibrated to the source''s exact gradient, not a generic perturbation direction. Ensemble attacks reduce this overfitting by averaging gradients across multiple surrogate models. The ensemble gradient is: g_ensemble = (1/k) * sum_{i=1}^{k} nabla_x J(f_i, x, y). The sign of g_ensemble produces a perturbation direction that is adversarial on average across all k surrogates. When k diverse surrogates are included (different architectures, different training runs), the averaged gradient direction smooths out source-specific artefacts and produces a perturbation that is more universally aligned with the general boundary geometry shared across models. Liu et al. (2017) demonstrated that ensemble adversarial examples achieve 15-30 percentage point higher transfer rates than single-surrogate examples at matched epsilon and attack method. The improvement is largest when the surrogates are diverse: an ensemble of 5 models from different architecture families outperforms an ensemble of 5 models from the same family. The attack cost scales linearly with ensemble size: k surrogates require k forward-backward passes per adversarial example generation step. For PGD with k=40 steps and an ensemble of 5 surrogates, this is 200 forward-backward passes per adversarial example. Against 5 locally available surrogate models, this is still effectively zero victim API queries.

Part 5

Title

Input Diversity and Translation-Invariant Gradient

Body

Two attack augmentation techniques from the 2019 literature further improve transfer rates by reducing gradient overfitting to the source architecture. Translation-invariant attacks (Dong et al. 2019) convolve the gradient with a Gaussian kernel before taking the sign step. The update becomes: x_{t+1} = clip(x_t + alpha * sign(W*g_t), 0, 1) where W is a convolution kernel and g_t is the gradient. Convolution smooths spatial high- frequency gradient components that are specific to the source architecture''s convolutional filters. The resulting perturbation is more spatially smooth and less tied to specific receptive field patterns of the source model -- making it more transferable across architectures with different convolutional structures. Input diversity attacks (Xie et al. 2019) apply random resizing and padding to the input before the forward pass. In each gradient computation step, the input x is randomly resized to a size between d_min and d_max and then padded back to d_max. The gradient is computed on this randomly transformed input. Averaging over random transformations reduces the gradient''s sensitivity to input-level features that are model-specific -- the resulting adversarial perturbation is robust to the types of input transformations that differ between the source architecture and the victim architecture. Both techniques are applied during the gradient computation phase (inside the PGD loop), not as post-processing of the adversarial example. The resulting adversarial examples look similar to standard PGD examples but achieve transfer rates that are 10-20 percentage points higher at matched epsilon.

Part 6

Title

Defence Implications: Robustness Must Account for Transfer Adversaries

Body

The standard adversarial robustness evaluation (PGD with k=40 and R=5, Section 10.4) measures how many examples an adversary with the model''s own gradients can cause to be misclassified. This is the ideal case for the defender: the adversary has maximum information. Transfer attacks represent a weaker adversary (they have gradient access to a surrogate, not the victim), but they are the realistic threat model for most production deployments where gradient access to the victim is not available. A model that achieves 45% PGD robust accuracy at epsilon=8/255 (standard CIFAR-10 benchmark) may have lower transfer-attack robust accuracy against a well-matched surrogate. The transfer adversary does not optimise against the victim''s exact gradient -- they optimise against the surrogate''s gradient. If the surrogate approximates the victim closely, the transfer attack approaches white-box attack strength. If the surrogate is a poor approximation, transfer rates drop significantly. The quality of the surrogate produced in Chapter 9 extraction directly determines the strength of the Chapter 10 transfer attack. For Track 7 calibration: transfer adversarial examples have different properties from direct adversarial examples. C&W kappa>0 transfer examples are the hardest to detect (large margin, stable under perturbation). PGD transfer examples from a well-matched surrogate behave similarly to direct PGD examples against the victim -- they are near the victim''s boundary and are somewhat detectable by consistency checking. Ensemble transfer examples may be more robust to feature squeezing because the ensemble gradient direction is smoother and survives transformation better than single-model gradients.

Part 7

Title

Detection: Can the Victim Log Detect Transfer Examples?

Body

Transfer adversarial examples arrive at the victim as a single submission with no campaign footprint. Track 6 sees nothing anomalous -- the query pattern for one adversarial example submission is identical to the query pattern for one natural query. Track 7 is the only detection layer. Track 7''s effectiveness against transfer examples depends on the attack method used on the surrogate. FGSM-based transfer examples are the most detectable: they sit near the surrogate''s boundary (and thus near the victim''s similar boundary), are fragile under random perturbation, and are caught by the consistency checker at rates comparable to direct FGSM attacks. PGD-based transfer examples are harder: they are closer to the victim''s boundary but still slightly offset because the surrogate boundary is not exactly the victim boundary. The consistency checker catches some; feature squeezing catches some. C&W kappa>0 transfer examples are the hardest to detect: they have a large margin and are stable under random perturbation. Ensemble transfer examples with input diversity augmentation may survive squeezing better than single-model transfer examples because the smooth ensemble gradient produces perturbations that are less sensitive to input transformation. The detection gap for transfer attacks motivates the adversarial detector classifier (Section 10.12): it is trained on adversarial examples from multiple attack families and can learn distributional properties of adversarial examples that are independent of whether the attack was direct or transferred. If the training set for the adversarial classifier includes transfer examples from diverse surrogates, it can achieve meaningful coverage of the transfer attack family even when other Track 7 primitives are less effective.

Transferability Analyser

Filename

transferability_analyser.py

Description

Measures transfer rate from source to victim across epsilon values and attack methods; the operational tool for quantifying surrogate quality for adversarial transfer

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires: torch, matplotlib. Import ToyLinear from fgsm_attack.py; pgd_linf from pgd_attack.py. ToyMLP defined inline as a second architecture. Note: random-weight models produce low transfer rates; production models with trained weights show the 20-80% ranges documented in the reference tables.

Code

# transferability_analyser.py
# Section 10.7: Transfer Rate Measurement Across Model Architecture Pairs
# 20260523 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from typing import List, Tuple, Dict
from fgsm_attack import fgsm, ToyLinear, attack_success_rate
from pgd_attack import pgd_linf


class ToyMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_features: int = 28 * 28, n_classes: int = 10, hidden: int = 128):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(n_features, hidden),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, n_classes),
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x.view(x.size(0), -1))


def transfer_rate(
    source: nn.Module,
    victim: nn.Module,
    x_adv: torch.Tensor,
    y_true: torch.Tensor,
) -> Tuple[float, float]:
    '''
    Measure transfer rate: among examples adversarial on source, how many are also
    adversarial on victim?
    Returns (source_success_rate, transfer_rate).
    '''
    with torch.no_grad():
        src_preds = source(x_adv).argmax(1)
        vic_preds = victim(x_adv).argmax(1)
    src_adv = (src_preds != y_true).float()
    vic_adv = (vic_preds != y_true).float()
    src_rate = src_adv.mean().item()
    if src_adv.sum() > 0:
        xfer = (vic_adv * src_adv).sum() / src_adv.sum()
        return src_rate, xfer.item()
    return src_rate, 0.0


def sweep_epsilon_transfer(
    source: nn.Module,
    victim: nn.Module,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    epsilons: List[float],
    attack: str = 'fgsm',
    pgd_steps: int = 20,
    pgd_restarts: int = 2,
) -> Dict[str, List[float]]:
    src_rates, xfer_rates = [], []
    for eps in epsilons:
        if attack == 'fgsm':
            x_adv = fgsm(source, x, y, epsilon=eps)
        else:
            alpha = eps / 5.0
            x_adv = pgd_linf(source, x, y, eps, alpha, pgd_steps, pgd_restarts)
        sr, xr = transfer_rate(source, victim, x_adv, y)
        src_rates.append(sr)
        xfer_rates.append(xr)
    return {'source': src_rates, 'transfer': xfer_rates}


def compare_attack_methods(
    source: nn.Module,
    victim: nn.Module,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    epsilon: float = 8.0 / 255.0,
) -> None:
    print(f'\nTransfer rate comparison (eps={epsilon * 255:.1f}/255)')
    print(f'{"Attack":<28} {"Source success":<18} {"Transfer rate"}')
    print('-' * 60)

    # FGSM
    x_fgsm = fgsm(source, x, y, epsilon=epsilon)
    sr, xr = transfer_rate(source, victim, x_fgsm, y)
    print(f'{"FGSM (k=1)":<28} {sr:<18.3f} {xr:.3f}')

    # PGD
    x_pgd = pgd_linf(source, x, y, epsilon, epsilon / 5.0, 20, 2)
    sr, xr = transfer_rate(source, victim, x_pgd, y)
    print(f'{"PGD-20 R=2":<28} {sr:<18.3f} {xr:.3f}')

    # Import C&W if available
    try:
        from cw_attack import cw_l2
        x_cw0 = cw_l2(source, x, y, kappa=0.0, n_binary_search=3, n_steps=80)
        sr, xr = transfer_rate(source, victim, x_cw0, y)
        print(f'{"C&W kappa=0":<28} {sr:<18.3f} {xr:.3f}')

        x_cw20 = cw_l2(source, x, y, kappa=20.0, n_binary_search=3, n_steps=80)
        sr, xr = transfer_rate(source, victim, x_cw20, y)
        print(f'{"C&W kappa=20":<28} {sr:<18.3f} {xr:.3f}')
    except ImportError:
        print('C&W not available (cw_attack.py not in path)')


def plot_transfer_sweep(
    epsilons: List[float],
    fgsm_result: Dict,
    pgd_result: Dict,
    save_path: str = 'transfer_sweep.png',
) -> None:
    eps_255 = [e * 255 for e in epsilons]
    fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5))

    ax1.plot(eps_255, fgsm_result['source'], color='#e74c3c', marker='o', markersize=4,
             linewidth=2, label='FGSM source success')
    ax1.plot(eps_255, fgsm_result['transfer'], color='#e74c3c', marker='s', markersize=4,
             linewidth=2, linestyle='--', label='FGSM transfer rate')
    ax1.plot(eps_255, pgd_result['source'], color='#2980b9', marker='o', markersize=4,
             linewidth=2, label='PGD source success')
    ax1.plot(eps_255, pgd_result['transfer'], color='#2980b9', marker='s', markersize=4,
             linewidth=2, linestyle='--', label='PGD transfer rate')
    ax1.set_xlabel('Epsilon (pixel units)', fontsize=11)
    ax1.set_ylabel('Rate', fontsize=11)
    ax1.set_title('Transfer Rate vs Epsilon\n(FGSM and PGD source attacks)', fontsize=11)
    ax1.legend(fontsize=8)
    ax1.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    ax1.set_ylim(0, 1.05)

    gap_fgsm = [s - t for s, t in zip(fgsm_result['source'], fgsm_result['transfer'])]
    gap_pgd = [s - t for s, t in zip(pgd_result['source'], pgd_result['transfer'])]
    ax2.fill_between(eps_255, gap_fgsm, alpha=0.4, color='#e74c3c', label='FGSM transfer gap')
    ax2.fill_between(eps_255, gap_pgd, alpha=0.4, color='#2980b9', label='PGD transfer gap')
    ax2.set_xlabel('Epsilon (pixel units)', fontsize=11)
    ax2.set_ylabel('Source success - Transfer rate', fontsize=11)
    ax2.set_title('Transfer Gap\n(gap = source success minus victim transfer)', fontsize=11)
    ax2.legend(fontsize=8)
    ax2.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig(save_path, dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    print(f'Saved: {save_path}')
    plt.show()

# surrogate_pipeline.py
# Section 10.7: End-to-End Surrogate Pipeline (Ch9 -> Ch10 Kill Chain)
# 20260523 | AI Security Mastery by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Optional
from fgsm_attack import fgsm, ToyLinear, attack_success_rate
from pgd_attack import pgd_linf


@dataclass
class SurrogatePipelineResult:
    ch9_victim_queries: int          # Queries spent on extraction (Ch9 phase)
    ch10_victim_queries: int         # Queries spent on transfer (Ch10 phase, should be ~0)
    total_victim_queries: int
    n_adversarial_examples: int
    per_example_victim_cost: float   # total / n_adversarial_examples
    source_success_rate: float
    transfer_rate: float
    victim_success_rate: float       # Transfer rate * source success


def simulate_ch9_extraction_cost(
    n_examples: int,
    extraction_queries: int = 50_000,
) -> int:
    '''
    Return the Ch9 victim query cost for the extraction phase.
    extraction_queries: total queries spent building the surrogate.
    This cost is amortised across all transfer examples generated.
    '''
    return extraction_queries


def run_surrogate_pipeline(
    victim: nn.Module,
    surrogate: nn.Module,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    epsilon: float = 8.0 / 255.0,
    attack: str = 'pgd',
    pgd_steps: int = 20,
    pgd_restarts: int = 2,
    ch9_extraction_queries: int = 50_000,
) -> SurrogatePipelineResult:
    '''
    Phase 1 (Ch9): adversary has surrogate from extraction (ch9_extraction_queries spent).
    Phase 2 (Ch10): generate adversarial examples against surrogate (0 victim queries).
                    Submit to victim (1 query per example for final check -- not adversarial generation).
    '''
    alpha = epsilon / 5.0
    if attack == 'fgsm':
        x_adv = fgsm(surrogate, x, y, epsilon=epsilon)
    else:
        x_adv = pgd_linf(surrogate, x, y, epsilon, alpha, pgd_steps, pgd_restarts)

    # Phase 2 victim queries: 0 for adversarial generation; count submissions only
    ch10_victim_queries = 0  # adversarial examples generated locally against surrogate

    with torch.no_grad():
        src_preds = surrogate(x_adv).argmax(1)
        vic_preds = victim(x_adv).argmax(1)

    src_adv = (src_preds != y).float()
    vic_adv = (vic_preds != y).float()

    src_rate = src_adv.mean().item()
    if src_adv.sum() > 0:
        xfer_rate = (vic_adv * src_adv).sum().item() / src_adv.sum().item()
    else:
        xfer_rate = 0.0

    total_victim_q = ch9_extraction_queries + ch10_victim_queries
    per_example_cost = total_victim_q / max(x.size(0), 1)

    return SurrogatePipelineResult(
        ch9_victim_queries=ch9_extraction_queries,
        ch10_victim_queries=ch10_victim_queries,
        total_victim_queries=total_victim_q,

# ensemble_attack.py
# Section 10.7: Ensemble Transfer Attack via Gradient Averaging

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from typing import List
from fgsm_attack import ToyLinear, attack_success_rate


class ToyMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_features=28*28, n_classes=10, hidden=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(n_features, hidden), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, n_classes),
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x.view(x.size(0), -1))


def ensemble_gradient(
    models: List[nn.Module],
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    criterion: nn.Module = None,
) -> torch.Tensor:
    '''
    Average gradient of loss w.r.t. x across all models in ensemble.
    Each model gets a fresh clone of x with independent gradient tracking.
    Returns averaged gradient tensor (same shape as x).
    '''
    if criterion is None:
        criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
    grad_sum = torch.zeros_like(x)
    for model in models:
        x_req = x.clone().detach().requires_grad_(True)
        loss = criterion(model(x_req), y)
        model.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        grad_sum = grad_sum + x_req.grad.data
    return grad_sum / len(models)


def ensemble_fgsm(
    models: List[nn.Module],
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    epsilon: float,
) -> torch.Tensor:
    '''FGSM using averaged gradient across ensemble.'''
    g_avg = ensemble_gradient(models, x, y)
    x_adv = x + epsilon * g_avg.sign()
    return torch.clamp(x_adv, 0.0, 1.0)


def ensemble_pgd(
    models: List[nn.Module],
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    epsilon: float,
    alpha: float,
    n_steps: int,
    n_restarts: int = 1,
) -> torch.Tensor:
    '''PGD using averaged gradient across ensemble; L-infinity projection.'''
    criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss(reduction='none')
    best_loss = torch.full((x.size(0),), -float('inf'), device=x.device)
    best_x_adv = x.clone()

    for _ in range(n_restarts):
        delta = torch.zeros_like(x).uniform_(-epsilon, epsilon)
        x_adv = torch.clamp(x + delta, 0.0, 1.0).detach()

        for step in range(n_steps):
            # Compute averaged gradient
            grad_avg = torch.zeros_like(x)
            for model in models:
                x_req = x_adv.detach().requires_grad_(True)
                loss_per = criterion(model(x_req), y)
                loss_per.sum().backward()
                grad_avg = grad_avg + x_req.grad.data
            grad_avg = grad_avg / len(models)

            with torch.no_grad():
                x_step = x_adv + alpha * grad_avg.sign()
                delta = torch.clamp(x_step - x, -epsilon, epsilon)
                x_adv = torch.clamp(x + delta, 0.0, 1.0)

        # Multi-restart tracking
        with torch.no_grad():
            # Average loss across ensemble for restart tracking
            avg_loss = torch.zeros(x.size(0), device=x.device)
            for model in models:
                avg_loss = avg_loss + criterion(model(x_adv), y)
            avg_loss = avg_loss / len(models)
            improved = avg_loss > best_loss
            best_loss[improved] = avg_loss[improved]
            best_x_adv[improved] = x_adv[improved]

    return best_x_adv.detach()

        with torch.no_grad():
            src = (model(x_adv).argmax(1) != y).float().mean().item()

        print(f'{"FGSM single surrogate " + str(i+1):<40} {src:<18.3f} {vic:.3f}')

    # Ensemble attack (all models)

        ens_vic = (victim(x_ens).argmax(1) != y).float().mean().item()
    print(f'{"FGSM ensemble (" + str(len(models)) + " surrogates)":<40} {ens_src:<18.3f} {ens_vic:.3f}')

def build_transfer_matrix(
    models: list,
    x: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    epsilon: float = 8.0 / 255.0,
    pgd_steps: int = 20,
    pgd_restarts: int = 2,
) -> np.ndarray:
    n = len(models)
    matrix = np.zeros((n, n))
    alpha = epsilon / 5.0
    for i, source in enumerate(models):
        x_adv = pgd_linf(source, x, y, epsilon, alpha, pgd_steps, pgd_restarts)
        with torch.no_grad():
            src_adv = (source(x_adv).argmax(1) != y).float()
            for j, victim in enumerate(models):
                vic_adv = (victim(x_adv).argmax(1) != y).float()
                if src_adv.sum() > 0:
                    matrix[i, j] = (vic_adv * src_adv).sum().item() / src_adv.sum().item()
                else:
                    matrix[i, j] = 0.0
    return matrix

def bit_depth_squeeze(x: torch.Tensor, n_bits: int = 4) -> torch.Tensor:
    steps = 2 ** n_bits - 1
    return (x * steps).round() / steps


def squeezer_detection_rate(
    model: nn.Module,
    x_adv: torch.Tensor,
    threshold: float = 0.20,
) -> float:
    with torch.no_grad():
        pred_orig = model(x_adv).argmax(1)
        pred_squeezed = model(bit_depth_squeeze(x_adv)).argmax(1)
    return (pred_orig != pred_squeezed).float().mean().item()


def consistency_detection_rate(
    model: nn.Module,
    x_adv: torch.Tensor,
    n_variants: int = 20,
    noise_std: float = 0.02,
) -> float:
    with torch.no_grad():
        pred_adv = model(x_adv).argmax(1)
        fired = torch.zeros(x_adv.size(0), dtype=torch.bool, device=x_adv.device)
        for _ in range(n_variants):
            noisy = torch.clamp(x_adv + torch.randn_like(x_adv) * noise_std, 0.0, 1.0)
            pred_noisy = model(noisy).argmax(1)
            fired = fired | (pred_noisy != pred_adv)
    return fired.float().mean().item()


def analyse_detection(
    model: nn.Module,
    x_adv: torch.Tensor,
    y: torch.Tensor,
    label: str,
) -> Dict[str, float]:
    asr = attack_success_rate(model, x_adv, y)
    sq = squeezer_detection_rate(model, x_adv)
    cc = consistency_detection_rate(model, x_adv)
    ensemble = 1.0 - (1.0 - sq) * (1.0 - cc)  # union approximation
    print(f'  {label:<38} ASR={asr:.3f}  Squeezer={sq:.3f}  CC={cc:.3f}  Ensemble={ensemble:.3f}')
    return {'label': label, 'asr': asr, 'squeezer': sq, 'cc': cc, 'ensemble': ensemble}

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Using FGSM as the surrogate attack for transfer and expecting strong transfer rates

Why It Happens

FGSM is the simplest and fastest attack. Engineers building a transfer attack pipeline use FGSM against the surrogate because it is already implemented and fast. They observe poor transfer rates and conclude the surrogate is a poor approximation of the victim.

Consequence

FGSM adversarial examples sit right at the source model''s decision boundary with minimal margin. When the source and victim boundaries are not perfectly aligned (which they never are), FGSM examples frequently fall back on the correct side of the victim''s boundary. Transfer rates for FGSM are typically 15-35%, even for well-matched surrogates. This understates the transfer threat: the same surrogate used with C&W kappa=20 or ensemble PGD would achieve 50-70% transfer.

Correct Approach

Use C&W with kappa >= 20 for maximum transfer rate: the confidence margin forces examples far enough from the source boundary to also cross the victim''s slightly offset boundary. Use ensemble PGD across multiple surrogates as the practical high-transfer configuration when C&W is too expensive. Transfer rate benchmarking should always include kappa>0 C&W as the upper bound.

Mistake 2

Description

Treating transfer rate as symmetric: assuming the A-to-B transfer rate equals the B-to-A transfer rate

Why It Happens

Transfer rate symmetry seems intuitive: if A and B have similar boundaries, then adversarial examples should transfer equally in both directions. Engineers measure A-to-B transfer, find it moderate, and assume B-to-A is similar without measuring.

Consequence

Transfer rates are asymmetric when the models have different capacities or boundary smoothness. A smaller model (linear classifier) trained on the same data as a larger model (deep MLP) will show higher transfer from large-to-small than small-to-large, because the large model''s more complex boundary geometry produces more robust adversarial examples that transfer even to the simpler model. An adversary who uses the wrong direction in their surrogate-to-victim transfer may significantly over- or under-estimate their transfer success.

Correct Approach

Always measure both directions in the transfer rate heatmap. The transfer_rate_heatmap.py deliverable computes the full N x N matrix. In a production threat model, the relevant direction is surrogate -> victim (adversary uses surrogate to attack victim). Verify that the surrogate has been selected to maximise transfer in that direction, not the reverse.

Mistake 3

Description

Concluding that PGD adversarial training makes a model transfer-attack-robust

Why It Happens

PGD adversarial training (Section 10.15) produces models that are robust to direct PGD attacks. Engineers report that their adversarially trained model has 45% PGD robust accuracy and conclude it is adversarially robust, including against transfer attacks.

Consequence

PGD adversarial training is measured against the model''s own gradients. Transfer attacks use surrogate gradients that are correlated but not identical. An adversarially trained model can have lower transfer-attack robust accuracy than a standardly trained model if the adversarial training changed the boundary geometry in a way that makes it more susceptible to surrogate- gradient perturbations. This is not always the case but is not precluded by PGD robustness claims.

Correct Approach

Evaluate adversarially trained models against transfer attacks using diverse surrogates (multiple architectures, different training data samples). Use the surrogate_pipeline.py and transfer_rate_heatmap.py deliverables. Report both direct PGD robustness and transfer robustness as separate metrics. They measure different adversary access tiers.

Mistake 4

Description

Building an ensemble attack from k copies of the same architecture (different random seeds) and expecting the same transfer improvement as a diverse ensemble

Why It Happens

k models with the same architecture trained with different random seeds are easy to generate and seem diverse. Engineers build an ensemble of 5 ToyLinear models with 5 different random seeds and compare to a single ToyLinear.

Consequence

Same-architecture models share inductive biases. Their gradient directions are highly correlated even with different random seeds. The ensemble gradient from 5 ToyLinear models is barely different from a single ToyLinear''s gradient -- the ensemble averaging does not reduce architecture-specific gradient artefacts because all models have the same architecture- specific features. Transfer rate improvement over single-source is marginal (1-5 percentage points) rather than the 15-30 percentage points reported for diverse architecture ensembles.

Correct Approach

Build diverse ensembles: include models from different architecture families (linear, MLP, CNN, transformer if available) and ideally trained on data samples from the same distribution. The ensemble_attack.py deliverable uses ToyLinear + ToyMLP to demonstrate this. In production, a diverse ensemble of 3-5 architectures achieves substantially higher transfer rates than 5 instances of the same architecture.

Mistake 5

Description

Assuming Track 7 detection rates measured against direct adversarial examples apply equally to transfer adversarial examples

Why It Happens

Track 7 calibration benchmarks use adversarial examples generated directly against the deployed model (white-box). Transfer examples from a surrogate are assumed to have the same detectability properties since they look similar and have the same epsilon constraint.

Consequence

Transfer adversarial examples have subtly different distributional properties from direct adversarial examples. They are generated to be adversarial against the surrogate, not the victim -- their relationship to the victim''s decision boundary is less precise. This can make them either easier to detect (if they are farther from the victim''s boundary) or harder to detect (if the ensemble gradient produces smoother perturbations that survive squeezing better). Calibrating Track 7 only against direct examples leaves the transfer case uncalibrated.

Correct Approach

Include transfer adversarial examples in the Track 7 calibration dataset. Use transfer_detection_signal_analyser.py to compare direct vs transfer detection rates and identify which primitive has the largest detection gap. Typically the consistency checker gap is largest for high-kappa C&W transfer examples -- these should receive additional coverage from the adversarial classifier in Section 10.12.

Section Summary

Compact

Transfer attacks close the Chapter 9 to Chapter 10 kill chain. The Ch9 distillation surrogate is the transfer vehicle: adversarial examples crafted white-box against the surrogate fool the victim at 20-80% rates determined by architecture match, training distribution match, attack method strength, and perturbation budget. Ensemble attacks improve transfer by averaging gradients across diverse surrogates (15-30 percentage point improvement). Per-example victim query cost is near-zero: the Ch9 extraction budget is amortised across all transfer examples generated. C&W kappa>0 produces the most transferable adversarial examples and is hardest to detect by consistency checking; adversarial classifier is the primary Track 7 primitive for high-kappa transfer examples. Track 6 has no signal for transfer attacks; Track 7 is the only detection layer.

Key Takeaways

  • Transfer works because models trained on the same distribution learn aligned boundary geometry; gradient directions are correlated across models
  • Two-phase kill chain: Ch9 extraction (10k-100k victim queries, amortised) + Ch10 transfer (0 additional victim queries per example)
  • C&W kappa>0 and ensemble PGD produce the highest transfer rates (50-70%); FGSM single-step produces the lowest (15-35%)
  • Transfer rate is asymmetric; always measure both directions; report the surrogate-to-victim direction for production threat models
  • Track 6 has no signal for transfer examples (no query campaign footprint); Track 7 adversarial classifier is the primary detection primitive
  • PGD adversarial training does not guarantee transfer robustness; evaluate adversarially trained models against diverse surrogate attacks

Section 10.8: Physical Adversarial Examples -- Patches, Stop Signs, and 3D Objects

Summary

Section 10.8 extends the adversarial example threat beyond the API to physical sensors. Universal adversarial patches (Brown et al. 2017) are printable regions that cause misclassification when placed in any scene, regardless of background image content. Stop sign attacks (Eykholt et al. 2018) demonstrated that physical stickers cause autonomous vehicle detectors to misclassify real-world signs. Expectation over transformation (EOT) optimises patches to be adversarial across a distribution of viewing angles, lighting conditions, and distances rather than at a single digital position. Physical adversarial attacks expand the threat surface to systems accessed through cameras or sensors -- with no API access required. Section 10.8 covers the patch generator, the EOT training procedure, and a patch localisation detector as the Track 7 primitive for physical adversarial attacks.
