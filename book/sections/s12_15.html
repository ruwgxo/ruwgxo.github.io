Four-Detector Ensemble: Combining Anomaly, Classification, Sequence, and Semantic Signals

Section Overview

One Line Summary

The four-detector ensemble is the Chapter 12 production deliverable: it combines anomaly, supervised, sequence, and semantic detection into a single decision whose coverage and precision exceed any individual detector through complementarity.

Why This Section Exists

Chapter 12 built four input-side detectors, each covering a different part of the attack surface with a different error profile. The Isolation Forest catches unknown attack variants with zero labels but with low precision. The supervised classifiers catch known attack families with high precision but miss novel attacks. The GRU catches multi-turn escalation that single-message detectors cannot see. The embedding detector catches paraphrase variants that evade keyword-based detection. Deployed individually, each has a coverage gap that the others fill. The ensemble combines them into a single detection decision. The justification is coverage complementarity: where one detector errs, another often catches the attack, so the combined system covers the full attack taxonomy from Chapters 6 through 10 better than any individual detector. The supervised classifier's miss on a novel attack is caught by the anomaly detector; the anomaly detector's false positive on an unusual but benign request is corrected by the supervised classifier's confident benign score; the single-message detectors' blindness to multi-turn escalation is covered by the GRU. The combination is not automatic. Two detectors that always agree add nothing to each other -- the ensemble of two identical detectors is just one detector. The value of the ensemble depends on the detectors making independent errors, which is measured by the confidence correlation. If the four detectors are highly correlated, the ensemble barely improves on the best single detector; if they are independent, the ensemble's errors are the intersection of the individual errors, which is much smaller. Section 12.15 measures the complementarity and correlation, builds the combination (weighted voting and a stacking meta-learner), and routes the ensemble output through a three-tier alert system aligned with the Chapter 11 false positive management. The combination requires the calibrated probabilities from Section 12.10 -- uncalibrated scores from different detectors are not on a common scale and cannot be combined coherently -- and runs on the async scoring architecture from Section 12.12 so the four detectors execute in parallel within the latency budget.

Position In Chapter

Section 12.15 is the first of the integration sections. It consumes all four input-side detectors (Sections 12.03, 12.05, 12.06, 12.07), the calibration from Section 12.10, and the async scoring from Section 12.12, and produces the ensemble decision that the Section 12.16 benchmarking evaluates and the Section 12.17 production gateway acts on. The PII scanner (Section 12.09) is not in this ensemble -- it is an output-side detector that runs separately on the model response.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

Why Ensemble Detection Outperforms Any Single Detector

Body

The case for the ensemble rests on two properties: coverage complementarity and error independence. Coverage complementarity means the detectors cover different parts of the attack surface. Error independence means that where one detector errs, the others often do not. Together, these properties make the ensemble cover more attacks with fewer false positives than any individual detector. Coverage complementarity arises from the different detection mechanisms. The anomaly detector (Isolation Forest) flags inputs that deviate from the learned benign distribution; it catches novel attacks that no labelled example resembles, because novelty is exactly what it detects. The supervised classifiers (Random Forest, XGBoost) flag inputs matching learned attack patterns; they catch known attack families with high precision but are blind to attacks unlike anything in their training data. The GRU flags multi-turn escalation patterns; it catches attacks that build across conversation turns, which single-message detectors cannot see. The embedding detector flags inputs semantically similar to known attacks; it catches paraphrase variants that change the wording but preserve the meaning, which keyword-based detection misses. Each mechanism has a coverage gap that another mechanism fills. The supervised classifier's gap (novel attacks) is the anomaly detector's strength. The single-message detectors' gap (multi-turn escalation) is the GRU's strength. The keyword detectors' gap (paraphrase variants) is the embedding detector's strength. The union of the four coverages is broader than any individual. Error independence is what converts the complementarity into improved precision. If the four detectors made the same errors -- flagged the same benign requests as attacks -- combining them would not reduce the false positives. But because the detectors use different mechanisms, they tend to make different errors: the anomaly detector's false positive on an unusual benign request is not shared by the supervised classifier, which confidently scores it benign. Combining the detectors so that a confident benign signal can override an isolated false positive reduces the ensemble's false positive rate below any individual detector's.

Part 2

Title

Coverage Complementarity Analysis

Body

The coverage complementarity is not assumed; it is measured. The coverage complementarity analysis builds a matrix mapping each detector to the attack families it covers, using the MITRE ATLAS technique taxonomy as the attack family reference. For each ATLAS technique relevant to the gateway -- prompt injection (AML.T0051), jailbreak, data exfiltration, and the others from the Chapter 6 through 10 taxonomy -- the matrix records which detectors reliably catch attacks of that technique. The matrix reveals the coverage structure. Some techniques are covered by multiple detectors (a direct prompt injection is caught by the supervised classifier, the embedding detector, and often the anomaly detector), providing redundancy. Some techniques are covered by only one detector (multi-turn escalation is caught primarily by the GRU), making that detector the sole coverage for that technique -- a single point of failure that the matrix surfaces. Some techniques may be covered by none of the four, revealing a coverage gap that needs a new detector or a different control. The single-coverage techniques are the most important finding. If only the GRU covers multi-turn escalation, then a regression in the GRU leaves multi-turn attacks undetected, and the ensemble's apparent defence-in-depth is illusory for that technique. The coverage analysis identifies these single points of failure so they can be addressed -- by adding a second detector for the technique, by monitoring the sole detector more closely, or by accepting the risk explicitly. The coverage_complementarity_analyser.py in deliverable_2 builds the matrix from a labelled evaluation set tagged with ATLAS techniques. For each technique, it measures each detector's recall on that technique's examples, producing the coverage matrix that shows where the ensemble is redundant, where it is single-covered, and where it has gaps.

Part 3

Title

Confidence Correlation: Are the Errors Independent?

Body

The ensemble's value depends on the detectors making independent errors. Two detectors that always agree -- that produce correlated confidence scores -- add nothing to each other: the ensemble of two perfectly correlated detectors is just one detector with extra compute cost. The confidence correlation measures how independent the detectors actually are. The confidence correlation matrix computes the pairwise correlation between the detectors' calibrated scores across an evaluation set. A correlation near 1 between two detectors means they score the same inputs similarly -- they agree on what is suspicious -- so they share the same blind spots and the ensemble of the two adds little. A correlation near 0 means they score independently -- they catch different things -- so the ensemble of the two covers more than either alone. The ideal ensemble has detectors with high individual accuracy and low pairwise correlation: each detector is good, and they are good at different things. The Chapter 12 detectors are designed for this -- the anomaly, supervised, sequence, and embedding mechanisms are fundamentally different, so their errors are largely independent. The correlation matrix verifies this design assumption holds on the actual data: if the supervised classifier and the embedding detector turn out to be highly correlated (perhaps because they both rely on similar surface features), the matrix surfaces it, and the ensemble weighting can account for the redundancy. The correlation also informs the combination strategy. Highly correlated detectors should not both receive full weight in a voting scheme, because their agreement is not independent confirmation -- it is the same signal counted twice. The confidence_correlation_matrix.py in deliverable_3 computes the matrix and identifies the detector pairs whose correlation is high enough to warrant down-weighting one of them in the ensemble.

Part 4

Title

Combination Strategies: Weighted Voting and Stacking

Body

Two combination strategies turn the four detector scores into a single decision. Weighted voting assigns each detector a weight and combines the calibrated scores as a weighted average; the ensemble flags an attack when the weighted average exceeds a threshold. The weights are set proportional to each detector's reliability: a detector with higher precision on a threat class gets more weight for that class. Weighted voting is simple, interpretable (the contribution of each detector is the weight times its score), and robust, but the weights are set heuristically or by a coarse optimisation. Stacking learns the combination instead of setting it heuristically. A meta-learner -- typically a logistic regression -- is trained on the four detectors' calibrated scores as features, with the ground-truth attack labels as the target. The meta-learner learns the optimal combination: which detectors to trust for which inputs, how to weight their agreement, and how to handle the cases where they disagree. The stacking meta-learner can learn that the GRU is most reliable for multi-turn jailbreaks and the embedding detector is most reliable for injection variants, assigning input-dependent effective weights that a fixed weighted vote cannot. The stacking meta-learner is trained on a held-out set -- not the data the individual detectors were trained on -- so that the meta-learner sees the detectors' scores on data they did not memorise, reflecting their true generalisation behaviour. This is the same temporal-split discipline applied one level up: the detectors train on the training split, the meta-learner trains on the validation split, and the test split evaluates the full ensemble. Stacking generally outperforms weighted voting when there is enough labelled data to train the meta-learner reliably, because it learns the combination from data rather than assuming a linear weighting. Weighted voting is the better choice when labelled data is scarce or when interpretability is paramount. The detector_ensemble.py in deliverable_1 implements both, defaulting to stacking when the validation set is large enough and falling back to weighted voting otherwise.

Part 5

Title

Three-Tier Alert Routing

Body

The ensemble produces a combined attack probability, but the action taken depends on more than a single threshold. The three-tier alert routing -- block, flag, monitor -- aligns the ensemble output with the Chapter 11 false positive management and the analyst capacity. The block tier is for high-confidence detections where the ensemble is strongly confident the input is an attack. The condition is typically strong detector agreement: when all four detectors (or a strong majority weighted by reliability) agree the input is an attack with high calibrated confidence, the gateway blocks the request. Blocking is the most consequential action, so it is reserved for the detections where the false positive risk is lowest -- the strong-agreement cases. The flag tier is for medium-confidence detections where the ensemble has a partial signal. The condition is a majority signal or a single high-confidence detector without full agreement. Flagged requests are not blocked -- they proceed -- but they are routed to the analyst review queue. This is where the analyst capacity from Section 12.10 is consumed, and the flag threshold is calibrated to the capacity so the flagged volume matches what the analysts can review. The monitor tier is for weak signals where a single detector flags the input without corroboration. Monitored requests are not blocked and not routed to the analyst queue -- they are logged for later analysis and for the uncertainty sampling in Section 12.14. The monitor tier captures the weak signals that are not actionable individually but are valuable in aggregate for understanding the attack distribution and for the active learning loop. The three tiers align the ensemble with the analyst capacity and the false positive tolerance. The block tier acts automatically on the highest-confidence detections; the flag tier fills the analyst queue with the medium-confidence detections worth human review; the monitor tier captures the weak signals without consuming analyst time. The detector_ensemble.py implements the routing with configurable tier thresholds.

Part 6

Title

Ensemble Latency and Explainability

Body

The ensemble runs the four detectors on the async parallel architecture from Section 12.12, so the ensemble latency is the maximum of the four detector latencies (around 15ms) plus the combination step (under 1ms for the weighted vote or the meta-learner inference). The ensemble_latency_profiler.py in deliverable_5 measures the full ensemble against the 50ms P99 budget, confirming that adding the combination to the parallel detectors does not breach the budget. The combination is cheap relative to the detector inference, so the ensemble latency is essentially the parallel detector latency. Explainability is the property that the ensemble decision can be attributed to its contributing detectors. When the ensemble blocks a request, the explanation should state which detectors flagged it and with what confidence: 'blocked because the supervised classifier (0.94) and the embedding detector (0.89) both flagged this as a prompt injection.' This attribution is essential for three reasons. Debugging: when the ensemble makes a wrong decision, the attribution shows which detector drove it, so the fix targets the right detector. Tuning: the attribution reveals which detectors contribute most to the decisions, informing the weight adjustments. Audit: when an ensemble decision is challenged, the attribution provides the explanation of why the decision was made. The ensemble_explainer.py in deliverable_4 produces the attribution. For a weighted vote, the attribution is the weighted contribution of each detector to the combined score. For a stacking meta-learner, the attribution uses the meta-learner's coefficients (for a logistic regression) or feature importances to show each detector's contribution to the final decision. The explainer also displays the per-detector calibrated confidence so the analyst sees not just that the ensemble flagged the request but which detectors drove the decision and how confident each was. Explainability connects to the broader detection-as-code discipline. An ensemble that produces opaque decisions is hard to operate; an ensemble whose every decision can be traced to its contributing detectors is debuggable, tunable, and defensible. The attribution is not an optional feature -- it is what makes the ensemble a maintainable production system rather than a black box.

Detector Ensemble

Filename

detector_ensemble.py

Description

Four-detector ensemble with weighted voting, a stacking meta-learner, and three-tier alert routing. Combines the calibrated scores of the Isolation Forest, XGBoost, GRU, and embedding detector into a single decision routed to block, flag, or monitor.

Implementation Notes

Code

# detector_ensemble.py
# Section 12.15: Four-Detector Ensemble with Three-Tier Routing
# 20260606 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 12 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from enum import Enum
from typing import Dict, List, Optional

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression


class AlertTier(Enum):
    BLOCK = 'block'
    FLAG = 'flag'
    MONITOR = 'monitor'
    PASS = 'pass'


DETECTOR_NAMES = ['isolation_forest', 'xgboost', 'gru', 'embedding']


@dataclass
class EnsembleResult:
    tier: AlertTier
    combined_score: float
    detector_scores: Dict[str, float]
    n_agreeing: int
    strategy: str
    contributions: Dict[str, float] = field(default_factory=dict)


class DetectorEnsemble:
    '''
    Combines four calibrated detector scores via weighted voting or stacking.
    Routes the decision to block, flag, or monitor.

    Requires CALIBRATED scores (Section 12.10): uncalibrated scores from
    different detectors are not on a common scale and cannot be combined.
    '''

    def __init__(
        self,
        weights: Optional[Dict[str, float]] = None,
        block_threshold: float = 0.8,
        flag_threshold: float = 0.5,
        monitor_threshold: float = 0.3,
        agreement_confidence: float = 0.7,
    ) -> None:
        self.weights = weights or {n: 1.0 for n in DETECTOR_NAMES}
        self.block_threshold = block_threshold
        self.flag_threshold = flag_threshold
        self.monitor_threshold = monitor_threshold
        self.agreement_confidence = agreement_confidence
        self._meta_learner: Optional[LogisticRegression] = None
        self.strategy = 'weighted_voting'

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Stacking meta-learner training
    # ------------------------------------------------------------------

    def fit_meta_learner(
        self,
        detector_scores: np.ndarray,   # shape (n_samples, 4)
        labels: np.ndarray,
        min_samples: int = 500,
    ) -> bool:
        '''
        Train the stacking meta-learner on detector scores from the VALIDATION split.
        Returns True if stacking is enabled, False if falling back to weighted voting.
        '''
        if len(labels) < min_samples:
            print(f'Only {len(labels)} samples (< {min_samples}); using weighted voting.')
            self.strategy = 'weighted_voting'
            return False
        self._meta_learner = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)
        self._meta_learner.fit(detector_scores, labels)
        self.strategy = 'stacking'
        print('Stacking meta-learner trained.')
        return True

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Combination
    # ------------------------------------------------------------------

    def _weighted_vote(self, scores: Dict[str, float]) -> tuple:
        total_w = sum(self.weights.get(n, 1.0) for n in scores)
        combined = sum(self.weights.get(n, 1.0) * s for n, s in scores.items()) / max(total_w, 1e-9)
        contributions = {
            n: (self.weights.get(n, 1.0) * s) / max(total_w, 1e-9)
            for n, s in scores.items()
        }
        return combined, contributions

    def _stacked(self, scores: Dict[str, float]) -> tuple:
        x = np.array([[scores.get(n, 0.0) for n in DETECTOR_NAMES]])
        combined = float(self._meta_learner.predict_proba(x)[0, 1])
        # Contribution = coefficient * score (logistic regression attribution)
        coefs = self._meta_learner.coef_[0]
        contributions = {
            n: float(coefs[i] * scores.get(n, 0.0))
            for i, n in enumerate(DETECTOR_NAMES)
        }
        return combined, contributions

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Routing
    # ------------------------------------------------------------------

    def _route(self, combined: float, scores: Dict[str, float]) -> tuple:
        n_agreeing = sum(1 for s in scores.values() if s >= self.agreement_confidence)

        # Block: strong agreement and high combined score
        if combined >= self.block_threshold and n_agreeing >= 3:
            return AlertTier.BLOCK, n_agreeing
        # Block also on very high combined even without full agreement
        if combined >= self.block_threshold:
            return AlertTier.FLAG, n_agreeing
        # Flag: majority signal
        if combined >= self.flag_threshold:
            return AlertTier.FLAG, n_agreeing
        # Monitor: weak single-detector signal
        if combined >= self.monitor_threshold or n_agreeing >= 1:
            return AlertTier.MONITOR, n_agreeing
        return AlertTier.PASS, n_agreeing

    def evaluate(self, detector_scores: Dict[str, float]) -> EnsembleResult:
        if self.strategy == 'stacking' and self._meta_learner is not None:
            combined, contributions = self._stacked(detector_scores)
        else:
            combined, contributions = self._weighted_vote(detector_scores)

        tier, n_agreeing = self._route(combined, detector_scores)
        return EnsembleResult(
            tier=tier,
            combined_score=combined,
            detector_scores=detector_scores,
            n_agreeing=n_agreeing,
            strategy=self.strategy,
            contributions=contributions,
        )


if __name__ == '__main__':
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    ensemble = DetectorEnsemble()

    cases = {
        'strong attack (all agree)': {'isolation_forest': 0.85, 'xgboost': 0.92, 'gru': 0.78, 'embedding': 0.88},
        'partial signal': {'isolation_forest': 0.3, 'xgboost': 0.72, 'gru': 0.4, 'embedding': 0.55},
        'single weak signal': {'isolation_forest': 0.65, 'xgboost': 0.2, 'gru': 0.15, 'embedding': 0.25},
        'clearly benign': {'isolation_forest': 0.1, 'xgboost': 0.05, 'gru': 0.08, 'embedding': 0.12},
    }
    for label, scores in cases.items():
        result = ensemble.evaluate(scores)
        print(f'\n{label}:')
        print(f'  combined={result.combined_score:.3f}  tier={result.tier.value.upper()}  '
              f'agreeing={result.n_agreeing}/4')

    # Train a stacking meta-learner
    print('\n--- Training stacking meta-learner ---')
    n = 1000
    X = rng.random((n, 4))
    y = (X.mean(axis=1) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, n) > 0.5).astype(int)
    ensemble.fit_meta_learner(X, y)
    result = ensemble.evaluate({'isolation_forest': 0.85, 'xgboost': 0.92, 'gru': 0.78, 'embedding': 0.88})
    print(f'Stacked decision: combined={result.combined_score:.3f}  tier={result.tier.value.upper()}')

Coverage Complementarity Analyser

Filename

coverage_complementarity_analyser.py

Description

Per-attack-family coverage matrix showing which detectors cover which MITRE ATLAS techniques. Measures each detector recall per technique, identifies single-coverage techniques (single points of failure) and coverage gaps.

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires: numpy. Input is a labelled evaluation set tagged with ATLAS techniques and per-detector scores.

Code

# coverage_complementarity_analyser.py
# Section 12.15: Coverage Complementarity Matrix
# 20260606 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 12 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Dict, List, Optional, Tuple

import numpy as np


@dataclass
class TechniqueCoverage:
    technique: str
    n_examples: int
    per_detector_recall: Dict[str, float]
    covering_detectors: List[str]    # recall >= coverage_threshold
    is_single_coverage: bool
    is_gap: bool


@dataclass
class CoverageMatrix:
    techniques: List[TechniqueCoverage]
    single_coverage: List[str]
    gaps: List[str]
    well_covered: List[str]


class CoverageComplementarityAnalyser:
    '''
    Builds the coverage matrix: which detectors cover which ATLAS techniques.
    Surfaces single-coverage techniques (SPOFs) and coverage gaps.
    '''

    def __init__(
        self,
        detector_names: List[str],
        coverage_threshold: float = 0.7,
        decision_threshold: float = 0.5,
    ) -> None:
        self.detector_names = detector_names
        self.coverage_threshold = coverage_threshold
        self.decision_threshold = decision_threshold

    def analyse(
        self,
        techniques: List[str],          # per-example ATLAS technique label
        detector_scores: Dict[str, np.ndarray],   # detector -> per-example scores
        is_attack: np.ndarray,          # per-example attack label (1/0)
    ) -> CoverageMatrix:
        unique_techniques = sorted(set(t for t, a in zip(techniques, is_attack) if a == 1))
        tech_arr = np.array(techniques)

        coverages = []
        for tech in unique_techniques:
            mask = (tech_arr == tech) & (is_attack == 1)
            n = int(mask.sum())
            if n == 0:
                continue
            per_detector = {}
            covering = []
            for det in self.detector_names:
                preds = (detector_scores[det][mask] >= self.decision_threshold)
                recall = float(preds.mean())
                per_detector[det] = recall
                if recall >= self.coverage_threshold:
                    covering.append(det)
            coverages.append(TechniqueCoverage(
                technique=tech,
                n_examples=n,
                per_detector_recall=per_detector,
                covering_detectors=covering,
                is_single_coverage=(len(covering) == 1),
                is_gap=(len(covering) == 0),
            ))

        return CoverageMatrix(
            techniques=coverages,
            single_coverage=[c.technique for c in coverages if c.is_single_coverage],
            gaps=[c.technique for c in coverages if c.is_gap],
            well_covered=[c.technique for c in coverages if len(c.covering_detectors) >= 2],
        )


def print_coverage_matrix(matrix: CoverageMatrix, detector_names: List[str]) -> None:
    print('Coverage Complementarity Matrix')
    print('=' * 80)
    header = '{:<28}'.format('ATLAS Technique') + ''.join(f'{d[:10]:>12}' for d in detector_names)
    print(header)
    print('-' * 80)
    for c in matrix.techniques:
        row = '{:<28}'.format(c.technique[:28])
        for d in detector_names:
            r = c.per_detector_recall.get(d, 0.0)
            mark = '*' if r >= 0.7 else ' '
            row += f'{r:>11.2f}{mark}'
        print(row)
    print('-' * 80)
    print(f'  Well covered (2+ detectors): {len(matrix.well_covered)}')
    if matrix.single_coverage:
        print(f'  SINGLE COVERAGE (SPOF): {matrix.single_coverage}')
    if matrix.gaps:
        print(f'  COVERAGE GAPS: {matrix.gaps}')

    ).tolist()
    is_attack = np.array([0 if t == 'benign' else 1 for t in techniques])

    scores = {}
    for det in detector_names:
        s = rng.beta(2, 5, n)
        for i, t in enumerate(techniques):
            if det == 'xgboost' and t in ('prompt_injection', 'jailbreak'):
                s[i] = rng.beta(8, 2)
            elif det == 'gru' and t == 'multiturn_escalation':
                s[i] = rng.beta(8, 2)
            elif det == 'embedding' and t == 'paraphrase_variant':
                s[i] = rng.beta(8, 2)
            elif det == 'isolation_forest' and t != 'benign':
                s[i] = rng.beta(4, 4)
        scores[det] = s

    analyser = CoverageComplementarityAnalyser(detector_names)
    matrix = analyser.analyse(techniques, scores, is_attack)
    print_coverage_matrix(matrix, detector_names)

Confidence Correlation Matrix

Filename

confidence_correlation_matrix.py

Description

Measures the pairwise correlation between detector outputs to quantify ensemble independence. Identifies highly correlated detector pairs that warrant down-weighting because their agreement is not independent confirmation.

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires: numpy. Optionally produces a heatmap with matplotlib.

Code

# confidence_correlation_matrix.py
# Section 12.15: Detector Confidence Correlation
# 20260606 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 12 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Dict, List, Optional, Tuple

import numpy as np


@dataclass
class CorrelationReport:
    detector_names: List[str]
    matrix: np.ndarray
    high_correlation_pairs: List[Tuple[str, str, float]]
    mean_off_diagonal: float


class ConfidenceCorrelationMatrix:
    '''
    Computes pairwise correlation between detector calibrated scores.
    High correlation means the detectors share blind spots; the ensemble
    of correlated detectors adds little over the best single detector.
    '''

    def __init__(self, high_correlation_threshold: float = 0.7) -> None:
        self.high_correlation_threshold = high_correlation_threshold

    def compute(
        self,
        detector_scores: Dict[str, np.ndarray],
    ) -> CorrelationReport:
        names = list(detector_scores.keys())
        score_matrix = np.column_stack([detector_scores[n] for n in names])
        corr = np.corrcoef(score_matrix, rowvar=False)

        high_pairs = []
        for i in range(len(names)):
            for j in range(i + 1, len(names)):
                if corr[i, j] >= self.high_correlation_threshold:
                    high_pairs.append((names[i], names[j], float(corr[i, j])))

        # Mean off-diagonal correlation (overall ensemble independence)
        n = len(names)
        off_diag = corr[~np.eye(n, dtype=bool)]
        mean_off = float(off_diag.mean())

        return CorrelationReport(
            detector_names=names,
            matrix=corr,
            high_correlation_pairs=high_pairs,
            mean_off_diagonal=mean_off,
        )

    def suggest_downweights(self, report: CorrelationReport) -> Dict[str, float]:
        '''Suggest down-weighting one detector in each highly correlated pair.'''
        weights = {n: 1.0 for n in report.detector_names}
        for a, b, corr in report.high_correlation_pairs:
            # Down-weight the second detector in the correlated pair
            weights[b] = min(weights[b], 0.5)
        return weights


def print_correlation_report(report: CorrelationReport) -> None:
    print('Detector Confidence Correlation Matrix')
    print('=' * 70)
    names = report.detector_names
    header = '{:<18}'.format('') + ''.join(f'{n[:10]:>12}' for n in names)
    print(header)
    for i, n in enumerate(names):
        row = '{:<18}'.format(n[:18])
        for j in range(len(names)):
            row += f'{report.matrix[i, j]:>12.2f}'
        print(row)
    print('-' * 70)
    print(f'  Mean off-diagonal correlation: {report.mean_off_diagonal:.3f}')
    if report.mean_off_diagonal < 0.3:
        print('  Detectors are largely independent: high ensemble value.')
    elif report.mean_off_diagonal < 0.6:
        print('  Partial correlation: moderate ensemble value.')
    else:
        print('  High correlation: ensemble adds little over best single detector.')
    if report.high_correlation_pairs:
        print('  Highly correlated pairs (consider down-weighting):')
        for a, b, c in report.high_correlation_pairs:
            print(f'    {a} <-> {b}: {c:.2f}')

# ensemble_explainer.py
# Section 12.15: Ensemble Decision Attribution
# 20260606 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 12 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Dict, List, Tuple

from detector_ensemble import EnsembleResult, AlertTier


@dataclass
class Explanation:
    decision: str
    combined_score: float
    primary_drivers: List[Tuple[str, float]]   # (detector, contribution)
    all_scores: Dict[str, float]
    narrative: str


DETECTOR_DESCRIPTIONS = {
    'isolation_forest': 'anomaly detector',
    'xgboost': 'supervised classifier',
    'gru': 'sequence model',
    'embedding': 'semantic similarity detector',
}


class EnsembleExplainer:
    '''
    Attributes an ensemble decision to its contributing detectors.
    Produces a narrative explanation for debugging, tuning, and audit.
    '''

    def explain(self, result: EnsembleResult) -> Explanation:
        # Rank detectors by contribution to the decision
        contribs = sorted(
            result.contributions.items(),
            key=lambda kv: abs(kv[1]),
            reverse=True,
        )
        primary = [(n, c) for n, c in contribs if c > 0][:2]

        narrative = self._narrative(result, primary)
        return Explanation(
            decision=result.tier.value,
            combined_score=result.combined_score,
            primary_drivers=primary,
            all_scores=result.detector_scores,
            narrative=narrative,
        )

    def _narrative(
        self,
        result: EnsembleResult,
        primary: List[Tuple[str, float]],
    ) -> str:
        if result.tier == AlertTier.PASS:
            return ('Passed: no detector produced a significant attack signal '
                    f'(combined score {result.combined_score:.2f}).')

        action = {
            AlertTier.BLOCK: 'Blocked',
            AlertTier.FLAG: 'Flagged for analyst review',
            AlertTier.MONITOR: 'Logged for monitoring',
        }[result.tier]

        if primary:
            drivers = ' and '.join(
                f'the {DETECTOR_DESCRIPTIONS.get(n, n)} '
                f'(score {result.detector_scores.get(n, 0):.2f})'
                for n, _ in primary
            )
            return (f'{action}: driven primarily by {drivers}. '
                    f'{result.n_agreeing}/4 detectors agreed; '
                    f'combined score {result.combined_score:.2f} '
                    f'via {result.strategy}.')
        return (f'{action}: combined score {result.combined_score:.2f} '
                f'via {result.strategy}.')


def print_explanation(explanation: Explanation) -> None:
    print(f'Decision: {explanation.decision.upper()}')
    print(f'  {explanation.narrative}')
    print('  Per-detector scores:')
    for det, score in sorted(explanation.all_scores.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1], reverse=True):
        desc = DETECTOR_DESCRIPTIONS.get(det, det)
        print(f'    {det:18} ({desc:28}) {score:.3f}')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    from detector_ensemble import DetectorEnsemble

    ensemble = DetectorEnsemble()
    explainer = EnsembleExplainer()

    cases = {
        'injection (strong)': {'isolation_forest': 0.7, 'xgboost': 0.94, 'gru': 0.6, 'embedding': 0.89},
        'multiturn (GRU-driven)': {'isolation_forest': 0.4, 'xgboost': 0.3, 'gru': 0.88, 'embedding': 0.35},
        'benign': {'isolation_forest': 0.1, 'xgboost': 0.05, 'gru': 0.08, 'embedding': 0.12},
    }
    for label, scores in cases.items():
        print(f'\n=== {label} ===')
        result = ensemble.evaluate(scores)
        explanation = explainer.explain(result)
        print_explanation(explanation)

Code

# ensemble_latency_profiler.py
# Section 12.15: Ensemble Latency Profiling
# 20260606 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 12 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from __future__ import annotations

import asyncio
import time
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Callable, Dict, List, Tuple

import numpy as np


@dataclass
class EnsembleLatencyReport:
    per_detector_p99: Dict[str, float]
    parallel_detector_p99: float
    combination_p99: float
    ensemble_p99: float
    budget_ms: float
    slo_met: bool
    dominant_detector: str


class EnsembleLatencyProfiler:
    '''
    Profiles the four-detector ensemble latency.
    Ensemble latency = max(detector latencies) [parallel] + combination.
    '''

    def __init__(self, budget_ms: float = 50.0) -> None:
        self.budget_ms = budget_ms

    async def _run_detector(self, fn: Callable, x) -> float:
        loop = asyncio.get_event_loop()
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        await loop.run_in_executor(None, fn, x)
        return (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000

    async def profile(
        self,
        detectors: Dict[str, Callable],
        combination_fn: Callable,
        model_input,
        n_runs: int = 100,
    ) -> EnsembleLatencyReport:
        per_detector: Dict[str, List[float]] = {n: [] for n in detectors}
        parallel_latencies = []
        combination_latencies = []
        ensemble_latencies = []

        for _ in range(n_runs):
            t_start = time.perf_counter()
            # Parallel detector execution
            results = await asyncio.gather(*[
                self._run_detector(fn, model_input) for fn in detectors.values()
            ])
            for name, lat in zip(detectors.keys(), results):
                per_detector[name].append(lat)
            parallel_lat = max(results)
            parallel_latencies.append(parallel_lat)

            # Combination step
            t_comb = time.perf_counter()
            combination_fn({n: 0.5 for n in detectors})
            combination_latencies.append((time.perf_counter() - t_comb) * 1000)

            ensemble_latencies.append((time.perf_counter() - t_start) * 1000)

        def p99(arr):
            return float(np.percentile(arr, 99))

        per_detector_p99 = {n: p99(v) for n, v in per_detector.items()}
        dominant = max(per_detector_p99, key=per_detector_p99.get)
        ensemble_p99 = p99(ensemble_latencies)

        return EnsembleLatencyReport(
            per_detector_p99=per_detector_p99,
            parallel_detector_p99=p99(parallel_latencies),
            combination_p99=p99(combination_latencies),
            ensemble_p99=ensemble_p99,
            budget_ms=self.budget_ms,
            slo_met=ensemble_p99 <= self.budget_ms,
            dominant_detector=dominant,
        )


def print_ensemble_latency(report: EnsembleLatencyReport) -> None:
    print(f'Ensemble Latency Profile (SLO: {report.budget_ms:.0f}ms P99)')
    print('=' * 60)
    print('  Per-detector P99 (parallel):')
    for n, lat in sorted(report.per_detector_p99.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1], reverse=True):
        print(f'    {n:18} {lat:.2f}ms')
    print(f'  Parallel detector P99: {report.parallel_detector_p99:.2f}ms '
          f'(dominated by {report.dominant_detector})')
    print(f'  Combination P99:       {report.combination_p99:.2f}ms')
    print(f'  Ensemble P99:          {report.ensemble_p99:.2f}ms')
    print(f'  SLO met:               {report.slo_met}')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    def make_detector(latency_ms):
        def predict(x):
            time.sleep(latency_ms / 1000.0)
            return 0.5
        return predict

    detectors = {
        'isolation_forest': make_detector(2),
        'xgboost': make_detector(2),
        'gru': make_detector(12),       # dominant
        'embedding': make_detector(7),
    }
    def combine(scores):
        return float(np.mean(list(scores.values())))

    profiler = EnsembleLatencyProfiler(budget_ms=50.0)
    report = asyncio.run(profiler.profile(detectors, combine, 'request', n_runs=50))
    print_ensemble_latency(report)

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Combining uncalibrated detector scores, producing a meaningless ensemble because the scores are not on a common scale

Why It Happens

The four detectors each produce a score, and the engineer averages them or feeds them to a meta-learner without first calibrating. The scores look like probabilities (they are between 0 and 1), so combining them seems valid.

Consequence

The Random Forest's 0.8 and the XGBoost's 0.8 mean different things when both are uncalibrated: one may correspond to a 60% attack rate and the other to a 90% rate. Averaging them combines incompatible scales, producing a combined score that does not correspond to any meaningful probability. The ensemble decision is based on a meaningless number, and the alert routing thresholds calibrated against it are also meaningless.

Correct Approach

Calibrate every detector with the Section 12.10 calibration before combining. Calibrated scores are on a common probability scale -- a calibrated 0.8 means an 80% attack rate for every detector -- so they can be averaged or fed to the meta-learner coherently. The detector_ensemble.py requires calibrated scores; calibration is a prerequisite, not an optional refinement.

Mistake 2

Description

Assuming the four detectors are independent without measuring the confidence correlation, building an ensemble that shares blind spots

Why It Happens

The detectors use different mechanisms, so the engineer assumes they make independent errors and the ensemble provides genuine defence-in-depth. The correlation is never measured because the independence seems obvious from the different mechanisms.

Consequence

Two of the detectors turn out to be highly correlated -- perhaps the supervised classifier and the embedding detector both rely on similar surface features -- so they share blind spots. The ensemble that appears to have four independent detectors effectively has three, and the shared blind spot of the correlated pair is a blind spot of the ensemble. The defence-in-depth is partly illusory.

Correct Approach

Measure the confidence correlation with the confidence_correlation_matrix.py before trusting the ensemble. Verify the detectors make independent errors (low off-diagonal correlation). Where two detectors are highly correlated, down-weight one so their agreement is not double-counted, and recognise that the correlated pair provides one effective coverage, not two.

Mistake 3

Description

Training the stacking meta-learner on the same data the individual detectors were trained on, producing an overconfident meta-learner

Why It Happens

The training set is the largest labelled dataset, so the engineer uses it to train both the individual detectors and the stacking meta-learner. The meta-learner sees the detectors' scores on the training data and learns to combine them.

Consequence

The individual detectors produce overconfident scores on the training data because they memorised it. The meta-learner trained on these overconfident scores learns a combination that works on the training distribution but generalises poorly. On production traffic, the detectors are less confident, and the meta-learner's combination, tuned for the overconfident training scores, produces a worse ensemble than weighted voting would have.

Correct Approach

Train the stacking meta-learner on the validation split, not the training split. The individual detectors train on the training split; the meta-learner trains on the detectors' scores on the validation split (which reflect their generalisation behaviour); the test split evaluates the full ensemble. This is the temporal-split discipline applied at the meta-learner level.

Mistake 4

Description

Using a single ensemble threshold instead of the three-tier routing, either flooding the analyst queue or missing actionable detections

Why It Happens

A single threshold is simpler than three tiers. The engineer sets one threshold: above it, take action; below it, pass. The action is either blocking (too aggressive) or flagging (floods the queue), depending on how the single threshold is set.

Consequence

A single block threshold either blocks too aggressively (false positives reach the block tier) or misses attacks (the threshold is high to avoid false-positive blocks, so medium-confidence attacks pass). A single flag threshold floods the analyst queue with every detection above it, exceeding the analyst capacity. Neither single threshold matches the action to the confidence.

Correct Approach

Use the three-tier routing from detector_ensemble.py: block on strong agreement (automated action on the highest-confidence detections), flag on majority signal (analyst review, calibrated to capacity), monitor on weak signal (logged for the active learning loop). The tiers match the action to the confidence and respect the analyst capacity.

Mistake 5

Description

Building an ensemble that produces opaque decisions with no attribution to the contributing detectors

Why It Happens

The ensemble produces a combined score and a decision; the individual detector contributions are not surfaced. The engineer treats the ensemble as a black box that produces a decision, which is simpler than implementing the attribution.

Consequence

When the ensemble makes a wrong decision, there is no way to tell which detector drove it, so the fix cannot target the right detector. When an analyst reviews a flagged request, they see only the combined score, not which detectors flagged it, so they cannot judge the detection's basis. When an ensemble decision is challenged in an audit, there is no explanation of why it was made. The opaque ensemble is hard to debug, tune, and defend.

Correct Approach

Implement the attribution with the ensemble_explainer.py. Every ensemble decision is attributed to its contributing detectors with their per-detector confidence, producing a narrative explanation: which detectors flagged the request and how confident each was. The attribution makes the ensemble debuggable (target the driving detector), tunable (see which detectors contribute), and defensible (explain every decision).

Section Summary

Compact

The four-detector ensemble is the Chapter 12 production deliverable. It combines the Isolation Forest (anomaly, novel attacks), the supervised classifiers (known families, high precision), the GRU (multi-turn escalation), and the embedding detector (paraphrase variants) into a single decision whose coverage and precision exceed any individual detector. The justification is coverage complementarity: each detector covers a part of the attack surface the others miss, so their union covers the full attack taxonomy. The value depends on error independence, measured by the confidence correlation -- four correlated detectors provide false defence-in-depth while sharing blind spots. Two combination strategies turn the scores into a decision: weighted voting (scores weighted by per-class reliability) and a stacking meta-learner (a logistic regression that learns the combination, trained on the validation split). The combination requires calibrated probabilities from Section 12.10 and runs on the async parallel architecture from Section 12.12. The three-tier alert routing -- block on strong agreement, flag on majority signal, monitor on weak signal -- aligns the ensemble with the analyst capacity. Ensemble explainability attributes every decision to its contributing detectors, making the ensemble debuggable, tunable, and defensible.

Key Takeaways

  • Coverage complementarity is the primary ensemble justification: each detector covers a part of the attack surface the others miss (anomaly catches novel, supervised catches known, GRU catches multi-turn, embedding catches paraphrase), so their union covers the full taxonomy
  • Confidence correlation determines the ensemble value: four correlated detectors provide false defence-in-depth while sharing blind spots; measure the correlation and down-weight highly correlated pairs
  • The ensemble requires calibrated probabilities: uncalibrated scores from different detectors are not on a common scale and cannot be combined coherently, so Section 12.10 calibration is a prerequisite
  • Stacking learns the combination from the validation split (which detectors to trust for which threat classes); weighted voting sets weights heuristically; stacking outperforms when there is enough data to train the meta-learner
  • The three-tier routing aligns the ensemble with analyst capacity: block on strong agreement (automated), flag on majority signal (analyst review, capacity-calibrated), monitor on weak signal (active learning loop)
  • Ensemble explainability attributes every decision to its contributing detectors with per-detector confidence, making the ensemble debuggable (target the driving detector), tunable (see contributions), and defensible (explain every decision)
