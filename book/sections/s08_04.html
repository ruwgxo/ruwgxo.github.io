Section 8.4: Clean-Label Poisoning - Bypassing Label Consistency Defences

Section Overview

Title

Clean-Label Poisoning: Bypassing Label Consistency Defences

One Line Summary

Clean-label poisoning keeps labels correct and corrupts features instead -- engineering training samples that sit in the target class's feature-space region while carrying the target class's label. The supervision signal is honest. The feature distribution is engineered. Label-consistency defences see no anomaly and the attack passes undetected. Poison Frogs (Shafahi 2018) is the canonical instance; Witches Brew and BullseyePolytope are the subsequent refinements. This is the pivot section: the attack that breaks the defence Section 8.3 taught you to trust.

Why This Section Exists

Section 8.3 established label flipping and the label-consistency defences that catch it. If poisoning research had stopped at label flipping, the training-time threat model would be largely solved -- the defences work, the budgets required are high, the detection rates are excellent. The research did not stop there. Attackers observed that label-consistency defences make a specific structural assumption: poisoned samples are feature-neighbourhood outliers. They responded by constructing attacks that preserve the feature-neighbourhood consistency that the defences rely on. Clean-label poisoning is that response. The pivot matters because it changes the entire analytic frame for training-time defence. Detection can no longer be a label-space operation. Feature-space analysis becomes mandatory. The infrastructure that Section 8.12 builds -- spectral signatures, activation clustering, Neural Cleanse -- exists because clean-label attacks forced the field to move from label- space to feature-space reasoning. Understanding why clean-label attacks defeat label consistency is the prerequisite for understanding why those feature-space primitives were built the way they were. This section also introduces the most sophisticated optimisation problem in Chapter 8's attack catalogue: the feature-collision objective. Unlike label flipping, which requires only selecting samples, clean-label attacks require crafting samples -- solving an optimisation over feature space to produce poisons that sit at the right feature-space location while looking like correct-class members under human review. The optimisation is both the mechanism that makes the attack effective and the vulnerability that defenders exploit: the engineered samples have statistical signatures in activation space that the clean-label optimisation cannot fully suppress. Section 8.12's spectral signatures and activation clustering are designed to detect exactly these signatures.

Position In Chapter

Section 8.4 is the hinge of Chapter 8. Every section before it operates in the label-space defensive regime where label flipping lives and where label-consistency defences work. Every section after it operates in the feature-space and activation-space regime that clean-label attacks force. Sections 8.5 and 8.6 (backdoor attacks) build on the clean-label framework -- BadNets-style backdoors can be constructed as clean-label attacks specifically to avoid the label-consistency tripwire. Sections 8.7-8.9 (LLM-specific attacks) inherit the clean-label pattern in their respective domains. Section 8.12's detection primitives are designed against the clean-label threat model. Section 8.13's defensive training techniques are evaluated primarily by their effect on clean-label attack success rates. If Section 8.3 was the easy problem, Section 8.4 is where the hard problem begins. The defensive architecture shift from label-space to feature-space happens here.

Prose Content

Part 1 The Definitional Shift

Title

The Definitional Shift: Honest Labels, Dishonest Features

Body

Clean-label poisoning inverts the structural property that made label flipping detectable. Label flipping keeps features unchanged and modifies labels, creating a mismatch between a sample's features and its claimed class. The mismatch is exactly what label-consistency defences look for: a sample whose feature-space neighbours carry one class's label but which itself carries another. Clean-label poisoning keeps labels correct and modifies features, creating no such mismatch. The label is honest -- it is the label a human annotator would assign if they looked at the sample. The feature modifications are engineered to sit in a specific feature-space region while remaining visually or semantically consistent with the claimed label under normal inspection. The adversary workflow in clean-label poisoning therefore differs fundamentally from label flipping. The label-flipping adversary selects samples and changes their labels. The clean-label adversary constructs samples -- they start with a base sample of some class, modify its features toward a target feature-space region, and submit the modified sample with its original (correct) label. The modification is constrained to preserve the sample's perceptual identity: an image that started as a dog still looks like a dog to a human annotator, even though its internal features have been engineered to resemble a cat in the model's learned representation space. The sample carries the label 'dog'. The label is correct in the sense that a human would assign 'dog' on visual inspection. The features are incorrect in the sense that the model's learned representation places the sample in the cat region. This inversion preserves the label-consistency defence's input while violating its assumption. The kNN agreement defence looks for samples whose label disagrees with their neighbours' labels in feature space. A clean-label poison constructed to sit in the target class's feature-space region has neighbours who carry the target class's label. The neighbour-label consensus matches the poison's label -- both say 'target class'. The defence computes high agreement and flags no anomaly. The structural assumption that poisoned samples are feature-neighbourhood outliers is false for clean-label attacks because the attacks were specifically engineered to avoid being feature-neighbourhood outliers. The defensive consequence is not that label-consistency defences are useless -- they remain effective against label flipping, and label flipping remains a baseline threat -- but that they are insufficient. A training-time defence programme that stops at label consistency has catastrophic blind spots for the attack class that actually dominates the threat landscape. The programme's confidence in its coverage is inversely proportional to its actual coverage: the defence appears to work perfectly because every label-flipping sample it sees gets caught, but the clean-label samples it does not see are the ones causing the damage. This is the concrete instance of the detectability-effectiveness frontier shift introduced in Section 8.2. The frontier is the curve along which attackers trade stealth against effectiveness. Label-consistency defence defines one point on that frontier: attacks that rely on label anomalies are caught; attacks that avoid label anomalies are not. Clean-label attacks are the adversary's response to that point -- a new attack class that sits at a different frontier position, trading the simplicity of label modification for the sophistication of feature engineering, and gaining detectability reduction as a result. The defender has not lost; the defender has been forced to shift primitive. The next frontier point is feature-space analysis, which Sections 8.12 operationalises.

Part 2 The Feature Collision Objective

Title

The Feature-Collision Objective: Optimisation as the Attack Mechanism

Body

Clean-label attacks require optimisation. Label flipping is a selection problem -- pick samples and change their labels. Clean-label is a construction problem -- craft samples that satisfy multiple simultaneous constraints. The samples must sit at a specific feature-space location (to influence the decision boundary in the adversary's desired direction). The samples must look like natural members of a specific class (to pass human annotation and appear as legitimate training examples). The samples must retain the target-class label honestly (to avoid label-consistency detection). These constraints are in tension with each other. Optimisation is what resolves them. The standard formulation is feature collision. Let f be the trained model's feature extractor, mapping input samples to a representation space. Let x_base be a clean sample of the poisoner's chosen base class, the class whose label the poison will carry. Let x_target be a test-time sample the adversary wants to manipulate -- the sample the adversary wants the model to misclassify. The poison x_p is constructed to satisfy two objectives simultaneously: f(x_p) should be close to f(x_target) in feature space, and x_p should be perceptually close to x_base in input space. The first objective causes the model to learn that features similar to x_target's features are associated with x_base's class label. When x_target is presented at test time, the model's feature extractor produces features near x_target's own features -- which are near the poison's features -- which the model has learned to associate with x_base's class. The target sample gets classified as the base class. The attack succeeds. The two objectives are combined in a loss function: minimise the feature-space distance to x_target plus a regularisation term that penalises deviation from x_base in input space. Gradient descent on this loss produces a sample x_p that sits at the feature-space location of x_target while remaining close enough to x_base in input space to look like a base-class member to human reviewers. The regularisation coefficient controls the trade-off between the two objectives. A coefficient too small produces poisons that are obvious feature-collision artifacts -- the input looks strange enough to be flagged by human annotators. A coefficient too large produces poisons that are perceptually natural but too similar to x_base to produce the desired feature- space collision. The coefficient is tuned empirically for each attack instance. The optimisation occurs in the feature space of a specific model -- the feature extractor f is model-specific. This creates the white-box requirement for the strongest clean-label attacks: the attacker needs access to the target model (or a close surrogate) to compute the gradients that drive the optimisation. In practice, attacks transfer substantially between similar architectures trained on similar data. A poison crafted against ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 often succeeds against ResNet-34 on CIFAR-10, and sometimes against VGG networks on the same data. Transferability is imperfect but meaningful; the attack is not fully dependent on exact target knowledge. From a defender's perspective, the optimisation is both the attack's source of effectiveness and its source of detectability. A crafted poison is not a natural sample. Its position in feature space is optimised, not sampled from a natural data distribution. This optimisation leaves fingerprints that feature- space analysis can detect -- the poison's feature representation has statistical properties that differ from naturally occurring base-class samples, even when the poison's input-space appearance is indistinguishable from a base-class member. Section 8.12's spectral signatures and activation clustering exploit these fingerprints. The detectability-effectiveness frontier has moved, but it has not vanished. The defender has a new primitive to exploit.

Part 3 Poison Frogs

Title

Poison Frogs: The Canonical Clean-Label Attack

Body

Poison Frogs, introduced by Shafahi, Huang, Najibi, Suciu, Studer, Dumitras, and Goldstein in 2018, is the canonical clean-label attack. The name is a reference to the attack's ability to take a sample of one class (for example a fish) and engineer it to behave like a sample of another class (a frog) in feature space, while the input still visually looks like a fish. The paper established the clean-label attack framework, demonstrated feasibility on standard image classification benchmarks, and motivated the subsequent generation of feature-space defensive primitives. The attack targets a specific test sample -- the adversary picks one sample they want the model to misclassify at test time -- and constructs a small number of poison samples that, when added to the training set, cause that specific test misclassification. The paper demonstrates that a single poison sample, or a handful of poisons, is often sufficient to produce targeted misclassification of the chosen test sample. The contamination budget is therefore very small in absolute terms, orders of magnitude below the 5%+ required for label flipping to produce detectable effects. The optimisation follows the feature-collision formulation. The attacker picks a base image (an image of the class whose label the poison will carry), picks a target image (the test image they want the model to misclassify), and solves for a poison image that sits near the target in feature space while remaining perceptually close to the base in input space. The optimisation is gradient descent on the feature-collision loss with a BFGS-like inner loop to refine the perceptual similarity constraint. Typical runtime is a few minutes per poison on a modern GPU, fast enough that producing a pool of poisons for multiple targets is practical. Poison Frogs demonstrated that clean-label attacks succeed at remarkably low contamination budgets. A single poison sample, carefully constructed, can produce targeted misclassification rates above 50% on standard benchmarks. Small pools of 5-10 poisons achieve rates above 90%. The attack is narrowly targeted -- each set of poisons is constructed for a specific target sample, and the poisons do not generalise to other targets without reconstruction -- but within its target, it is highly effective. The paper also demonstrated that kNN-agreement defences fail against Poison Frogs samples. The poisons' feature-space locations are close to the target sample's location; the target sample is typically close to other samples of the attacker's chosen base class (the feature-collision objective ensures this); the poisons' labels match the base class's label. Feature-neighbourhood consistency is preserved by construction. Label-consistency defences see no anomaly. This result was the motivating demonstration for the feature-space detection primitives subsequently developed in Tran et al. 2018 (spectral signatures) and Chen et al. 2018 (activation clustering), both of which appear in Section 8.12. The limitation of Poison Frogs is that it requires white-box model access to construct the poisons optimally. In practice, the attack has been shown to transfer to similar architectures at somewhat reduced success rates. The transferability property is important for real-world threat models: an adversary at rung 4 (model publisher) or rung 2 (dataset contributor) may not have direct access to the target deployment's specific model, but can often construct poisons against a surrogate that transfers to a range of related deployments. The attack economics scale.

Part 4 Witches Brew And Bullseye

Title

Witches Brew and BullseyePolytope: The Refinement Generation

Body

Poison Frogs established the framework. The subsequent generation of clean-label
attacks refined it in two principal directions: increasing the generality of
the attack (poisons that work across many target samples rather than one) and
increasing the attack's robustness (poisons that succeed despite the defender's
attempts to use feature-space detection). The canonical instances are Witches
Brew (Geiping, Fowl, Huang, Czaja, Taylor, Moeller, Goldstein 2021) and
BullseyePolytope (Aghakhani, Meng, Wang, Kruegel, Vigna 2021).

Witches Brew introduces gradient matching as an alternative to feature collision.
Rather than constructing poisons whose features match a target's features, the
attacker constructs poisons whose contribution to the training gradient matches
the contribution that an adversarially-crafted training sample of the target
class would have made. The formulation is subtly different but mathematically
cleaner: the attacker specifies the desired update to the model's decision
boundary, and solves for poisons whose combined gradient updates produce that
boundary change. The resulting attack is more efficient (fewer poisons for
the same effect) and more generalisable (the same poison set can affect
multiple target samples that share relevant features).

The paper demonstrates Witches Brew on ImageNet-scale datasets, where Poison
Frogs struggles to scale. Witches Brew poisons successfully misclassify chosen
targets in ImageNet with contamination rates below 0.1%. The attack also shows
improved transfer across model architectures -- the gradient-matching formulation
produces poisons that are less dependent on the specific feature extractor used
during construction.

BullseyePolytope takes a different refinement path. Where Poison Frogs and
Witches Brew target a single target sample per poison set, BullseyePolytope
constructs poisons that form a polytope (a convex region) in feature space
around the target class's centre. The poisons effectively redraw the target
class's feature-space boundary, causing misclassification of any sample that
falls within the newly-expanded boundary. This produces a broader attack effect
-- many samples get misclassified, not just a single targeted test sample --
at a modest increase in contamination budget. The attack sits between Poison
Frogs (narrowly targeted) and label flipping (broadly untargeted) in the
breadth-budget trade-off.

BullseyePolytope also demonstrates improved stealth against feature-space
defences available at the time. The polytope construction produces poisons
whose feature-space positions are distributed rather than clustered. A cluster
of poisons at a specific feature-space point is detectable by activation
clustering (clusters with unexpected composition); a polytope of poisons
distributed across a region is harder to isolate as a coherent anomaly. The
paper specifically evaluates the attack against spectral signatures and
activation clustering and reports reduced detection rates.

Both attacks demonstrate the same arms-race dynamic that Section 8.2 described
as the detectability-effectiveness frontier. Each defence innovation motivates
an attack refinement that recovers some of the detectability ground. The
frontier moves but does not collapse. Defenders respond with more sophisticated
primitives -- Neural Cleanse for trigger reverse-engineering, STRIP for input-
perturbation analysis, meta-classifiers trained specifically to distinguish
poisoned models. The arms race continues. The defender's goal, per Section
8.2, is to push the frontier far enough that attacks become economically
unattractive at the attacker's preferred access rung -- not to close the
frontier entirely.

Part 5 Clean Label For Text And Llms

Title

Clean-Label for Text and LLMs: Synonym Substitution and Style Transfer

Body

Clean-label poisoning is sometimes treated as an image-classification-specific phenomenon, a consequence of the continuous feature spaces in which image classifiers operate. This framing is wrong. The clean-label construction -- features engineered to sit at a specific location while label remains correct -- applies directly to text and instruction-tuning contexts, with different technical mechanisms for the feature engineering but the same structural attack pattern. In text classification, the feature-space manipulation is accomplished through linguistic transformations that preserve surface meaning while shifting the text's position in the model's learned representation. Synonym substitution replaces words with semantically similar alternatives chosen specifically to shift the sentence's embedding toward the target feature-space region. Style transfer rewrites the text in a different style (formal to casual, or vice versa) that the model has learned to associate with a different class. Syntactic transformations reorder phrases or rewrite sentence structures while preserving semantic content. All of these transformations preserve the label -- a sentence classified as 'positive sentiment' by a human is still 'positive sentiment' after synonym substitution -- while shifting the text's feature- space position. The canonical text clean-label attack target is sentiment classification, spam detection, or toxicity classification. A clean-label spam poisoner submits emails that are legitimate (honest label: 'not spam') but whose linguistic features are engineered to resemble the spam distribution in the model's learned representation. The model, trained on the poisoned dataset, learns a looser definition of 'not spam' that includes spam-adjacent patterns. At deployment, actual spam that happens to match those patterns slips through the classifier. The analogue of Poison Frogs for text is straightforward in structure if technically distinct in execution. For instruction-tuned LLMs, clean-label attacks target the instruction-response pairs used for supervised fine-tuning. The label in this context is the response -- the target output the model is trained to produce for a given instruction. A clean-label poisoner constructs instruction-response pairs where both components are legitimate in isolation (the instruction is a reasonable user request; the response is a plausible answer) but whose combination shifts the model's learned behaviour in the attacker's direction. For example, an instruction-tuning poisoner who wants the model to refuse specific queries might construct pairs where the instructions contain subtle phrasing cues and the responses are refusals -- but the refusals are phrased as legitimate helpfulness-preserving declines, and the instructions are phrased as reasonable ambiguous requests. Neither half of the pair is anomalous. The combination installs the target behaviour. The defensive challenge in text and instruction-tuning contexts is harder than in image classification. Feature-space detection for text operates on embeddings (typically from the same transformer being defended), and poisoned text samples can be constructed to sit in distributions that mirror benign text closely. Activation clustering in transformer attention patterns produces noisier signals than convolutional activation patterns for images. The research on clean-label text defences is less mature than for images as of the chapter's writing, though Section 8.7 will show how to extend the feature-space framework to instruction tuning specifically. Section 8.7 treats LLM-specific poisoning in depth. The point for Section 8.4 is that clean-label attacks are not an artifact of image classification. They are a general property of training-time threat models in which the adversary can construct samples and the defender relies on sample-level feature checks. The principle transfers; the technical details change.

Part 6 Why Feature Space Detection Becomes Mandatory

Title

Why Feature-Space Detection Becomes Mandatory

Body

Clean-label attacks complete the argument that motivated Chapter 8. The chapter opened with Section 8.1's claim that training-time defence requires fundamentally different architecture than inference-time defence. Section 8.2 organised training-time attacks into quadrants and identified Q2 targeted integrity as the dominant threat. Section 8.3 established label-consistency defence as the baseline and showed it catches label flipping. Section 8.4 closes the argument by establishing that the baseline defence is not sufficient -- clean-label attacks in Q2 bypass it entirely, and Q2 is where the threat lives. The conclusion is that label-consistency defence must be complemented by feature- space detection. The complementarity is structural, not accidental. Label- consistency defence catches attacks that modify labels. Feature-space detection catches attacks that modify features. Both attack classes are present in the threat landscape; both defences are therefore required. Neither subsumes the other. An organisation that deploys only label-consistency defence has Q1 and Q4 coverage but no Q2 coverage. An organisation that deploys only feature-space detection has Q2 coverage but misses the loud (and easier to execute) label- flipping attacks that Q1 adversaries sometimes choose. The feature-space detection primitives that Section 8.12 introduces -- spectral signatures, activation clustering, Neural Cleanse, STRIP, and meta-classifiers -- each exploit a different property of clean-label poisons. Spectral signatures exploit the statistical anomalies that the feature-collision optimisation leaves in activation distributions: poisoned samples have learned representations that deviate from the natural distribution of their claimed class, even when the input-space appearance is natural. Activation clustering exploits the clustering pattern that poisons form when multiple poison samples share a feature-space target: the poisoned cluster is identifiable as a sub-cluster within the claimed class. Neural Cleanse exploits the trigger-like properties that clean-label poisons sometimes exhibit, reverse-engineering perturbation patterns that cause the model to shift predictions. STRIP exploits the sensitivity of poisoned models to input perturbation -- poisoned inputs react to perturbation differently than clean inputs. Meta-classifiers learn, from labelled clean-vs-poisoned model pairs, to identify poisoned models directly without needing to identify individual poisoned samples. None of these primitives catches all clean-label attacks. Each has failure modes against specific attack variants. The defensive approach is ensemble -- combining multiple primitives in an auditing pipeline to maximise coverage across the clean-label attack space. Section 8.15's production pipeline implements this ensemble specifically. Section 8.13's defensive training techniques complement detection by reducing clean-label attack success rates at training time, shifting the frontier in the defender's favour before the detection layer even operates. Clean-label attacks, in short, establish the defensive agenda for the rest of Chapter 8. Every subsequent section either introduces an attack variant in the clean-label family (backdoor attacks, LLM instruction-tuning, LoRA adapters) or introduces a defence designed against the clean-label threat model (feature-space detection, defensive training, auditing pipelines). The chapter's analytic arc pivots at Section 8.4.

Part 7 Attack Economics And Threat Reality

Title

Attack Economics and the Reality of Clean-Label Threats

Body

The research literature establishes that clean-label attacks work -- they succeed on standard benchmarks at low contamination rates, they bypass label- consistency defences, they are recipe-reproducible with standard ML infrastructure. The reasonable follow-up question is how often these attacks actually occur in production, and what threat models reasonably prioritise them. The honest answer is that public incident attribution for clean-label poisoning is sparse -- very few organisations have published detailed clean-label attack incidents with forensic detail sufficient to confirm the attack mechanism. This is partly because the attacks are stealthy (they are hard to detect after the fact), partly because attribution requires specialised ML forensic capability that few organisations have, and partly because organisations that discover such attacks have strong incentives not to publicise them. The absence of public attribution is not the same as the absence of occurrence. Multiple properties of clean-label attacks suggest they are in active use at rates higher than the public literature indicates. The attacks are economically rational for motivated adversaries -- targeted misclassification at very low contamination budgets, stealthy against standard defences, transferable across models. The tooling is mature -- reference implementations are available, attacks scale to ImageNet-level datasets, the construction is reproducible. The threat actors with motivation (nation-state adversaries targeting security classifiers, organised criminal groups targeting fraud detection, competitive intelligence actors targeting content moderation) have the access levels required to execute the attacks. The combination of attractive economics, mature tooling, and motivated actors produces a probable threat landscape in which clean-label attacks are more common than public incidents suggest. For defensive planning, this implies that clean-label detection capability should be proportional to the motivational strength of likely adversaries, not to the historical incident rate. Organisations deploying models that adversaries have strong motivations to subvert (security products, content moderation, fraud detection, safety-critical LLMs) should prioritise feature-space detection higher than the public threat statistics would suggest. Organisations deploying models with lower adversarial motivation (internal productivity tools, benign enterprise applications) can rationally prioritise other controls more heavily. The threat assessment is motivation-weighted, not incident-weighted. The section therefore closes with an honest framing of the evidential state: clean-label attacks are proven in research, reproducible in practice, rational from an adversary's perspective, and unproven in attribution but likely in actuality. The defensive response is proportional investment in feature-space detection primitives, scaled to the motivational strength of likely adversaries against the specific model being defended. Sections 8.12 and 8.15 provide the primitives and pipeline; Section 8.4 establishes why the investment is warranted.

Feature Collision Formulation Reference

Title

Feature-Collision Objective Mathematical Reference

Description

Standard formulation of the feature-collision optimisation underlying Poison Frogs and related attacks. Provided in prose form with reference to the implementations in deliverables.

Formulation

Notation

  • f: feature extractor mapping input samples to representation space
  • x_base: clean sample of the base class whose label the poison will carry
  • x_target: test sample the adversary wants misclassified as the base class
  • x_p: the poison sample being constructed
  • beta: regularisation coefficient balancing feature proximity against input-space proximity

Loss Formulation

Loss(x_p) = ||f(x_p) - f(x_target)||^2 + beta * ||x_p - x_base||^2

Solution Approach

  • Initialise x_p with x_base
  • Compute gradient of Loss with respect to x_p via backpropagation through f
  • Update x_p in negative-gradient direction with learning rate schedule
  • Project x_p onto input-space constraint (e.g., valid pixel range for images)
  • Iterate until convergence or iteration budget exhausted

Typical Hyperparameters

  • beta: 0.1 to 10.0, tuned per dataset and target
  • Iteration count: 100-1000 depending on model depth and input complexity
  • Learning rate: 1e-2 to 1e-4, often with cosine decay

Implementation Consideration

The optimisation operates in the target model's feature space. Gradients flow through f back to input space. For production attacks, attackers use the full f including all layers; for defence benchmarking, using intermediate feature representations is common and produces comparable poisons.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 Assuming Image Classification Only

Description

Treating clean-label poisoning as an image-classification-specific threat

Why It Happens

The canonical research (Poison Frogs, Witches Brew, BullseyePolytope) uses image classification benchmarks. The feature-collision mathematics is cleanest to present in continuous image feature spaces. Practitioners reading this literature internalise the attack as image-specific and conclude that text or tabular models face different threats.

Consequence

Text classifiers, tabular models, and (critically) LLMs deployed without clean-label detection capability. The attack transfers directly to these domains -- with different technical mechanisms but the same structural pattern -- and deployments without feature-space detection are exposed.

Correct Approach

Treat clean-label poisoning as a general pattern, not an image-specific attack. Section 8.4 Part 5 covers text variants; Section 8.7 covers LLM-specific variants. Plan feature-space detection capability across all domains, not just image.

Mistake 2 Declaring Victory On Label Consistency Coverage

Description

Assessing training-time defence coverage by presence of label-consistency controls

Why It Happens

Label-consistency defences are widely-deployed, well-understood, and produce high detection rates against label flipping. Programmes that audit their coverage against the label-flipping threat report high coverage numbers. The coverage measurement is technically correct for label flipping and structurally wrong for the broader training-time threat.

Consequence

Detection programme that reports high coverage while being blind to the dominant attack class. The programme's reported posture is inversely related to its actual posture -- high confidence, low coverage. Incidents that do occur are attributed to novel attacks rather than to the pre-existing coverage gap.

Correct Approach

Measure training-time coverage by ability to detect Q2 targeted-integrity attacks, not by ability to detect Q1/Q4 availability attacks. Sections 8.12 and 8.15 provide the right detection capability; Section 8.4 establishes why it is mandatory.

Mistake 3 Underestimating White Box Transfer

Description

Assuming clean-label attacks are limited to adversaries with white-box access to the specific target model

Why It Happens

The strongest clean-label attack results require white-box access. The literature emphasises this requirement when reporting peak attack success rates. Practitioners generalise to the conclusion that black-box adversaries cannot execute clean-label attacks, reducing the threat to the insider or rung-3 threat model only.

Consequence

External and rung-2 adversaries are treated as out-of-scope for clean-label threats. In practice, transfer from surrogate models achieves meaningful (though reduced) attack success rates. Adversaries with only dataset-contribution access routinely achieve targeted misclassification through surrogate-crafted poisons.

Correct Approach

Assume clean-label attacks are feasible for any adversary with rung-2 access or higher. White-box access increases attack efficiency but is not a binary prerequisite. Section 8.4 transferability discussion (Part 3) quantifies the surrogate-to-target degradation.

Mistake 4 Over Relying On Human Annotation

Description

Treating human annotation as an effective defence against clean-label poisoning

Why It Happens

Clean-label poisons are constructed to look natural to human annotators. The perceptual-similarity constraint in the optimisation formulation specifically ensures this. Nonetheless, organisations sometimes assume that humans-in-the- loop during dataset curation will catch poisoning. The intuition is reasonable for egregious samples; it is wrong for well-constructed clean-label poisons.

Consequence

Reliance on a defence that is specifically designed against by the attack. Well-constructed Poison Frogs samples pass human review at rates indistinguishable from clean samples. Annotation-based defence contributes essentially zero detection capability against clean-label attacks.

Correct Approach

Treat human annotation as a quality-assurance control, not a security control. Clean-label detection requires feature-space analysis (Section 8.12 primitives) that humans cannot perform at scale or with the required discrimination.

Mistake 5 Static Benchmarking Against Historical Attacks

Description

Evaluating feature-space detection primitives only against Poison Frogs and concluding coverage is adequate

Why It Happens

Poison Frogs is the oldest, best-known, and most extensively-benchmarked clean- label attack. Detection primitives with high success against Poison Frogs are easy to demonstrate. The detectability-effectiveness frontier from Section 8.2 predicts that attacks will evolve; benchmarking only against historical attacks measures a frontier point that the adversary has already moved past.

Consequence

Detection coverage that catches Poison Frogs but misses Witches Brew, BullseyePolytope, or novel variants. The organisation reports clean-label coverage based on the historical attack, while the actual threat is the contemporary variant.

Correct Approach

Benchmark detection against the attack generation currently active, not the founding attack. Maintain attack catalog freshness; Section 8.13 defensive training and Section 8.12 detection primitives evaluation should be re-run as the attack literature evolves. Treat attack catalog maintenance as an operational function, not a one-time research activity.

Section Summary

Compact

Clean-label poisoning inverts the structural property that made label flipping detectable. Instead of modifying labels and keeping features, the adversary modifies features and keeps labels correct. Samples are constructed via feature- collision optimisation: engineered to sit at a specific feature-space location (producing the adversary's desired decision-boundary shift) while remaining perceptually natural to preserve their claimed label honestly. Poison Frogs (Shafahi et al. 2018) is the canonical instance; Witches Brew (Geiping 2021) generalises across targets; BullseyePolytope (Aghakhani 2021) expands to regions. The label-consistency defences from Section 8.3 fail entirely: they look for feature-neighbourhood mismatches that clean-label construction specifically avoids. Detection must shift from label space to feature space -- spectral signatures, activation clustering, Neural Cleanse, STRIP, meta-classifiers -- all of which Section 8.12 builds. The clean-label framework transfers to text and instruction- tuning domains, though mature detection tooling is image-domain-dominant as of the chapter's writing. Clean-label attacks are economically rational for motivated adversaries across all rungs of the Section 8.1 access ladder; public attribution is sparse but the threat is likely under-reported rather than under-occurring. Section 8.4 is the hinge: it establishes the feature-space defensive regime that the rest of Chapter 8 builds.

Key Takeaways

  • Clean-label poisoning keeps labels honest and engineers features instead -- the adversary response to label-consistency defence
  • Feature-collision optimisation is the mechanism: minimise feature-space distance to target while preserving perceptual similarity to base class
  • Poison Frogs is canonical; Witches Brew and BullseyePolytope refine for scale and generality
  • Label-consistency defences produce essentially zero detection rate against clean-label attacks (~3-8% TPR vs 92% for label flipping)
  • Attack pattern transfers to text, instruction-tuning, and LLM contexts with domain-specific feature-engineering mechanisms
  • Feature-space detection (Section 8.12) is the structurally necessary defensive response -- mandatory, not optional
  • Public attribution is sparse; economic rationality and tooling maturity suggest the threat is under-reported rather than under-occurring

Title

Backdoor Attacks: Trigger-Based Behaviour and BadNets

Connection

Section 8.4 established clean-label poisoning and showed why feature-space detection becomes mandatory. Section 8.5 begins the deepest threat family in Chapter 8: backdoor attacks. BadNets (Gu et al. 2017) is the canonical backdoor and the original feature-space threat. Backdoors combine the feature-engineering insight of clean-label attacks with an additional structural property: conditional activation. The model behaves cleanly on trigger-free inputs (preserving aggregate accuracy, passing standard evaluation) and behaves as the attacker intends on trigger-bearing inputs (high attack success rate where it matters). This is the worst case of Q2 targeted integrity -- the attack that is invisible on every benchmark the defender runs, and reliable on every triggered input the adversary sends. Section 8.5 builds the attack. Sections 8.5 through 8.12 build against it.
