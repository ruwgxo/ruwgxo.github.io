Neural Classifier: Fine-Tuned DistilBERT for High-Accuracy Injection Detection

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Fine-tuned DistilBERT is the accuracy ceiling of Chapter 12 -- 95%+ on the temporal test set with 2,000+ labelled examples -- but it earns that position only when the data threshold is crossed; below it, Random Forest is better in every dimension that matters operationally.

Why This Section Exists

The Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers from Section 12.05 reach 90%+ accuracy on the temporal test set with 500+ labelled examples and produce full SHAP explainability in sub-5ms inference. For many production deployments, this is sufficient. The detection engineer who stops at Section 12.05 has a credible, explainable, fast classifier. Section 12.08 exists for the deployments where 90%+ is not sufficient -- where the cost of a missed injection or jailbreak is high enough to justify the additional engineering investment of neural classifier training. At 2,000+ labelled examples, DistilBERT fine-tuning reaches 95%+ accuracy and outperforms the Random Forest by 5-10 percentage points in precision at matched recall. This precision improvement translates directly to fewer false positive alerts per day, which is operationally significant when the analyst team is small and alert fatigue is already a concern. The section also establishes two practices that apply to every Chapter 12 detector, not just the neural classifier: model card discipline (documenting training data, evaluation methodology, known limitations, and recommended thresholds in a structured artefact before any model enters the registry) and INT8 quantisation (reducing the 15ms FP32 DistilBERT inference to 7ms, making the neural classifier compatible with the 50ms gateway budget alongside the other detectors). Section 12.08 is the last individual detector section before the ensemble (12.15) and the production gateway capstone (12.17). It completes the four-detector set.

Position In Chapter

Section 12.08 is the final individual detector section. After this section, all four detectors in the Chapter 12 ensemble exist: Isolation Forest (12.03), Random Forest and XGBoost (12.05), GRU (12.06), and DistilBERT (12.08). Sections 12.09 through 12.14 cover the operational infrastructure that makes these detectors production- sustainable. Prose Content

Part 1

Title

When Neural Classification Supersedes Tree Classifiers

Body

The Random Forest from Section 12.05 makes decisions based on the 20-feature security vector from Section 12.02. Its decision boundary is determined entirely by the engineered features: sequence length, role boundary markers, embedding distances, contextual session features. When those features capture the attack signal accurately, the Random Forest is sufficient. When the attack pattern is subtle enough that the engineered features miss it -- when the distinguishing signal is in the specific token sequence and syntactic structure of the request, not in its statistical surface properties -- the Random Forest has a ceiling. DistilBERT processes the raw text of the request as a sequence of subword tokens. Its attention layers build contextual representations that capture relationships between tokens across the full sequence. A fine-tuned DistilBERT has access to the same structural signals the Random Forest uses (instruction keyword co-occurrence, role boundary token patterns) but also to higher-order syntactic and semantic relationships that no engineered feature captures: the way a jailbreak payload constructs a logical argument for the model to abandon its constraints, the specific syntactic framing of a hypothetical that signals fictional-frame evasion, the pragmatic function of a request that looks benign at the feature level but encodes an instruction override at the token-sequence level. The data requirement for this capability is real. DistilBERT pre-training gave the model language understanding; fine-tuning teaches it the security-domain distinction between attack and benign text. This distinction is narrower than general language understanding but still requires enough examples to define the decision boundary reliably. Below 2,000 labelled examples, the fine-tuning signal is insufficient to overcome the model's general-purpose pre-training, and the decision boundary is poorly placed for the security domain. The Random Forest, trained on a well-designed feature set, outperforms DistilBERT below this threshold because its features directly encode the security-domain signal rather than hoping the fine-tuning generalises from insufficient examples. At 2,000+ examples, the crossover happens. The fine-tuned DistilBERT's token-level contextual representations provide coverage of attack patterns that the engineered features miss, and it reaches 95%+ accuracy versus the Random Forest's 90%+. The 5-10 percentage point improvement in precision at matched recall is the operational justification for the additional engineering investment.

Part 2

Title

DistilBERT Architecture: What the Model Already Knows

Body

DistilBERT (Sanh et al. 2019) is produced by knowledge distillation from BERT-base. Knowledge distillation trains a smaller student model to match the output probability distributions of a larger teacher model across a large training corpus, rather than training the student from scratch. The student learns not just the final classification output of the teacher but the soft probability distributions over the full vocabulary at every token position, which encode richer information about the teacher's internal representations than one-hot labels would. The result is a 66M parameter model with 6 transformer layers, 12 attention heads, and a 768-dimensional hidden state -- 40% fewer parameters than BERT-base's 110M, running at 60% of BERT's inference time while retaining 97% of its performance on GLUE benchmark tasks. For the security detection use case, the relevant comparison is not GLUE but the temporal test set from Section 12.04; DistilBERT and BERT-base produce near-identical results on binary security classification, making DistilBERT the correct choice on latency grounds alone. What DistilBERT already knows from pre-training on the BooksCorpus and English Wikipedia is relevant to the fine-tuning strategy. It has strong representations of common English sentence structure, subject-verb-object relationships, negation, and hypothetical framing. It knows the tokens that appear in instruction contexts versus question contexts. It understands that 'ignore' followed by 'previous' and 'instructions' is semantically coherent and common in command contexts. This prior knowledge means the fine-tuning task is not teaching DistilBERT language from scratch -- it is teaching it to apply existing language understanding to a narrow security-domain distinction. The practical implication for fine-tuning configuration is that only the upper layers and the classification head need to change significantly. The lower layers, which encode general linguistic structure, are already well-calibrated. A common fine-tuning strategy layers the learning rates: lower layers use a very small learning rate (1e-5) or are frozen entirely, upper layers use the standard fine-tuning learning rate (2e-5), and the classification head uses a higher learning rate (5e-5 to 1e-4). This differential learning rate approach reduces catastrophic forgetting of the pre-trained language representations while allowing the model to specialise its upper layers for the security detection task.

Part 3

Title

Fine-Tuning Pipeline: Trainer, FP16, and Learning Rate Scheduling

Body

The Hugging Face Trainer class abstracts the training loop for transformer fine- tuning and handles the operational concerns that a manual training loop would require explicit implementation of: gradient accumulation, FP16 loss scaling, evaluation at configurable intervals, checkpoint saving, and early stopping based on validation metric. The critical TrainingArguments configuration for security classifier fine-tuning has six parameters that differ from the default tutorial settings. First, the learning rate: 2e-5 is the standard starting point, lower than the 1e-3 used for training from scratch because the pre-trained weights are already good and large gradient updates would destroy the pre-trained representations. Second, the number of training epochs: 3-5 epochs with early stopping based on validation F1, not 10+ epochs as recommended for tasks with abundant data. Third, the warmup ratio: 0.06 (6% of total training steps) provides a warm-up period during which the learning rate ramps from 0 to its target value, avoiding large gradient updates in the first steps when the classification head is randomly initialised. Fourth, weight decay of 0.01 provides L2 regularisation on the model parameters, which is especially important on a small security dataset where overfitting is the primary concern. Fifth, fp16=True enables mixed-precision training on compatible GPUs, halving the memory footprint and increasing throughput by 1.5-2x. Sixth, load_best_model_at_end=True ensures the checkpoint from the best validation epoch is used for inference rather than the final epoch's checkpoint, which may have overfit relative to the best checkpoint. The learning rate schedule is linear decay: the learning rate decreases linearly from its peak after the warm-up period to 0 at the end of training. This prevents the model from making large updates in later epochs when the gradient signal is already small. Cosine annealing is an alternative that decreases more gradually; for security classification tasks, both produce similar results and linear decay is simpler to configure.

Part 4

Title

INT8 Quantisation: Making DistilBERT Production-Compatible

Body

A fine-tuned DistilBERT classifier in FP32 produces inference at 12-15ms per request on CPU. In a production gateway with a 50ms P99 budget shared across all seven tracks and four detectors, 15ms for a single detector consumes 30% of the total budget before the Isolation Forest, Random Forest, GRU, and embedding detector have had any budget allocation. The FP32 DistilBERT is not compatible with the production architecture. INT8 post-training quantisation converts the FP32 weight tensors to 8-bit integer representations, reducing model size from approximately 255MB to 65MB and inference time from 15ms to 6-8ms on CPU. The quantisation is applied after fine-tuning without any retraining: a calibration dataset of 200-500 representative inputs is used to compute the activation ranges for each layer, which determine the scaling factors for the INT8 representation. The accuracy cost on the security test set is less than 0.5 percentage points for DistilBERT -- a negligible regression compared to the latency improvement. PyTorch's dynamic quantisation applies INT8 quantisation to the linear layers (the most compute-intensive layers in the transformer) at inference time, using the input activation ranges observed dynamically rather than pre-computed from a calibration dataset. Dynamic quantisation is simpler to apply than static quantisation (no calibration dataset required) and produces 7-10ms inference on CPU for DistilBERT, which fits within the gateway budget. Static quantisation requires the calibration step but produces marginally faster inference (6-7ms). For the security classifier use case, dynamic quantisation is the correct starting point. ONNX Runtime provides an alternative quantisation path that is independent of PyTorch: the fine-tuned model is exported to ONNX format and then quantised using ONNX Runtime's quantisation tools. The ONNX Runtime path typically produces 5-7ms inference on CPU and is compatible with the same ONNX Runtime inference infrastructure used for the Section 12.07 embedding model. Maintaining a single inference runtime across both models simplifies the production deployment.

Part 5

Title

Model Cards: Documentation as a Registry Gate

Body

A model card (Mitchell et al. 2019) is a structured document that accompanies a trained model and describes the conditions under which the model was trained, evaluated, and intended to be used. For public ML models, model cards are a transparency practice. For production security classifiers in the Chapter 12 detection system, they are an operational requirement enforced by the Section 12.13 model registry. The Section 12.13 stage transition gate from Staging to Production requires a model card to be attached to the model version in the registry before promotion is permitted. This gate exists because the operational team that deploys and monitors the classifier needs to know: what data the classifier was trained on, what evaluation it was subjected to, what attack classes it is known to miss, and what false positive behaviour to expect from it. Without this information, the operations team cannot safely tune the alert thresholds, cannot correctly interpret unusual alert patterns, and cannot make informed decisions about when to retrain. A security classifier model card has six required fields. Training data describes the sources, volume, temporal range, annotation methodology, and inter-annotator agreement of the training dataset. Evaluation methodology states the temporal split parameters, the evaluation metrics used, and the date of evaluation. Performance metrics records the precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, and FPR at the operating threshold on the temporal test set. Known limitations documents the attack classes where the classifier is known to underperform (with specific precision or recall numbers) and the input distributions where false positive rates are elevated. Recommended usage states the operating threshold, the maximum acceptable request rate for which the latency SLO holds, and the retraining trigger conditions. Lineage records the training code version, the dataset version tag from Section 12.04, and the feature schema version from Section 12.02. The model_card_generator.py in deliverable_4 produces a model card JSON from the training metadata, evaluation results, and known limitations in a structured format that the model registry accepts directly.

Part 6

Title

DistilBERT vs Random Forest: Choosing the Right Classifier for the Current Context

Body

The bert_vs_rf_comparison.py in deliverable_5 runs both classifiers on the same temporal test set and produces a direct comparison across accuracy, precision at matched recall, inference latency, and explainability. The comparison is the decision artefact that determines which classifier occupies the primary injection detection slot in the gateway. Four decision factors determine the outcome. Dataset size is the most important: below 1,000 labelled examples, Random Forest wins on all metrics; between 1,000 and 2,000, the classifiers are comparable; above 2,000, DistilBERT wins on precision at matched recall. Latency budget determines which classifier can run at all: if the gateway budget analysis in Section 12.12 shows less than 8ms available for injection detection, DistilBERT INT8 is marginal and Random Forest is the safe choice. Annotation quality affects DistilBERT more than Random Forest: DistilBERT's token-level attention can memorise label noise in ways that Random Forest's tree averaging cannot; if kappa is below 0.80, Random Forest is the more robust choice regardless of dataset size. Explainability requirements for regulatory or compliance contexts may mandate SHAP-explainable predictions; if the downstream alert triage system requires feature-level explanations that an analyst can read in 30 seconds, Random Forest is the only choice. In the Section 12.15 ensemble, both classifiers run in parallel and their scores are combined by the stacking meta-learner. The decision between DistilBERT and Random Forest for the primary injection track is therefore not permanent -- the ensemble includes both and the meta-learner learns which to trust for which input types. The comparison from this section determines the primary slot assignment; the ensemble determines the combined output.

Code

# neural_injection_classifier.py
# Section 12.08: DistilBERT Prompt Injection Classifier
# 20260606 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 12 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from __future__ import annotations

import json
import time
from dataclasses import dataclass
from pathlib import Path
from typing import Dict, List, Optional, Tuple

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import torch
from torch.utils.data import Dataset

try:
    from transformers import (
        AutoTokenizer,
        AutoModelForSequenceClassification,
        TrainingArguments,
        Trainer,
        EarlyStoppingCallback,
    )
    from sklearn.metrics import (
        precision_score, recall_score, f1_score, roc_auc_score,
    )
    TRANSFORMERS_AVAILABLE = True
except ImportError:
    TRANSFORMERS_AVAILABLE = False
    print('WARN: transformers not installed. pip install transformers torch')


MODEL_NAME = 'distilbert-base-uncased'
MAX_LENGTH = 128


@dataclass
class NeuralPrediction:
    request_text: str
    attack_probability: float
    is_attack: bool
    threshold: float
    inference_ms: float
    quantised: bool


class SecurityTextDataset(Dataset):
    def __init__(
        self,
        texts: List[str],
        labels: List[int],
        tokenizer,
        max_length: int = MAX_LENGTH,
    ) -> None:
        self.encodings = tokenizer(
            texts,
            truncation=True,
            padding='max_length',
            max_length=max_length,
            return_tensors='pt',
        )
        self.labels = torch.tensor(labels, dtype=torch.long)

    def __len__(self) -> int:
        return len(self.labels)

    def __getitem__(self, idx: int) -> dict:
        return {
            'input_ids': self.encodings['input_ids'][idx],
            'attention_mask': self.encodings['attention_mask'][idx],
            'labels': self.labels[idx],
        }


def compute_metrics(eval_pred) -> dict:
    logits, labels = eval_pred
    preds = np.argmax(logits, axis=1)
    probs = torch.softmax(torch.tensor(logits), dim=1)[:, 1].numpy()
    return {
        'f1': float(f1_score(labels, preds, zero_division=0)),
        'precision': float(precision_score(labels, preds, zero_division=0)),
        'recall': float(recall_score(labels, preds, zero_division=0)),
        'roc_auc': float(roc_auc_score(labels, probs)) if len(set(labels)) > 1 else 0.0,
    }


class NeuralInjectionClassifier:
    '''
    DistilBERT fine-tuned prompt injection classifier.

    Data requirement: 2,000+ labelled examples in the temporal training split.
    Below this threshold, use RandomForestSecurityClassifier (Section 12.05).

    Training: Hugging Face Trainer with FP16 (GPU), linear LR schedule, weight decay.
    Production: INT8 dynamic quantisation reduces 15ms FP32 to 7ms.
    '''

    def __init__(
        self,
        model_name: str = MODEL_NAME,
        operating_threshold: float = 0.5,
        device: Optional[str] = None,
    ) -> None:
        self.model_name = model_name
        self.operating_threshold = operating_threshold
        self.device = device or ('cuda' if torch.cuda.is_available() else 'cpu')
        self._model = None
        self._tokenizer = None
        self._trained = False
        self._quantised = False

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Training
    # ------------------------------------------------------------------

    def fine_tune(
        self,
        train_df: pd.DataFrame,
        val_df: pd.DataFrame,
        output_dir: str = '/tmp/distilbert_injection',
        num_train_epochs: int = 5,
        learning_rate: float = 2e-5,
        warmup_ratio: float = 0.06,
        weight_decay: float = 0.01,
        per_device_batch_size: int = 16,
        fp16: bool = None,
    ) -> dict:
        '''
        Fine-tune DistilBERT on the temporal split training set.
        train_df and val_df must have 'text' and 'label' columns.
        Returns training metrics dict.
        '''
        assert TRANSFORMERS_AVAILABLE, 'Install transformers: pip install transformers'
        assert len(train_df) >= 500, (
            f'Only {len(train_df)} training examples. '
            f'Need 2,000+ for DistilBERT; use RandomForestClassifier below this.'
        )

        use_fp16 = fp16 if fp16 is not None else (self.device == 'cuda')

        print(f'Fine-tuning {self.model_name} on {len(train_df)} examples '
              f'(device={self.device}, fp16={use_fp16})')

        self._tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(self.model_name)
        self._model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(
            self.model_name, num_labels=2,
        )

        train_ds = SecurityTextDataset(
            train_df['text'].tolist(), train_df['label'].tolist(), self._tokenizer,
        )
        val_ds = SecurityTextDataset(
            val_df['text'].tolist(), val_df['label'].tolist(), self._tokenizer,
        )

        # Compute class weights for imbalanced training set
        n_neg = int((train_df['label'] == 0).sum())
        n_pos = int((train_df['label'] == 1).sum())
        pos_weight = n_neg / max(n_pos, 1)
        print(f'  Class imbalance: {n_neg} benign / {n_pos} attack '
              f'(pos_weight={pos_weight:.2f})')

        args = TrainingArguments(
            output_dir=output_dir,
            num_train_epochs=num_train_epochs,
            per_device_train_batch_size=per_device_batch_size,
            per_device_eval_batch_size=per_device_batch_size * 2,
            learning_rate=learning_rate,
            warmup_ratio=warmup_ratio,
            weight_decay=weight_decay,
            fp16=use_fp16,
            evaluation_strategy='epoch',
            save_strategy='epoch',
            load_best_model_at_end=True,
            metric_for_best_model='f1',
            greater_is_better=True,
            report_to='none',
            logging_steps=50,
        )

        trainer = Trainer(
            model=self._model,
            args=args,
            train_dataset=train_ds,
            eval_dataset=val_ds,
            compute_metrics=compute_metrics,
            callbacks=[EarlyStoppingCallback(early_stopping_patience=2)],
        )

        t0 = time.time()
        train_result = trainer.train()
        elapsed = time.time() - t0
        self._trained = True
        print(f'Fine-tuning complete in {elapsed:.0f}s')
        return dict(train_result.metrics)

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Quantisation
    # ------------------------------------------------------------------

    def quantise_int8(self) -> None:
        '''Apply INT8 dynamic quantisation for CPU production inference.'''
        assert self._trained and self._model is not None
        self._model = torch.quantization.quantize_dynamic(
            self._model.cpu(),
            {torch.nn.Linear},
            dtype=torch.qint8,
        )
        self._quantised = True
        print('Applied INT8 dynamic quantisation.')

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Inference
    # ------------------------------------------------------------------

    def predict(self, text: str) -> NeuralPrediction:
        assert self._trained and self._tokenizer is not None
        t0 = time.time()
        self._model.eval()
        enc = self._tokenizer(
            text,
            truncation=True,
            padding='max_length',
            max_length=MAX_LENGTH,
            return_tensors='pt',
        )
        device = 'cpu' if self._quantised else self.device
        enc = {k: v.to(device) for k, v in enc.items()}
        with torch.no_grad():
            logits = self._model(**enc).logits
        prob = float(torch.softmax(logits, dim=1)[0, 1].item())
        return NeuralPrediction(
            request_text=text[:200],
            attack_probability=prob,
            is_attack=prob >= self.operating_threshold,
            threshold=self.operating_threshold,
            inference_ms=(time.time() - t0) * 1000,
            quantised=self._quantised,
        )

    def predict_batch(self, texts: List[str]) -> List[NeuralPrediction]:
        return [self.predict(t) for t in texts]

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Persistence
    # ------------------------------------------------------------------

    def save(self, path: str) -> None:
        p = Path(path)
        p.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
        if self._model:
            self._model.save_pretrained(str(p / 'model'))
        if self._tokenizer:
            self._tokenizer.save_pretrained(str(p / 'tokenizer'))
        meta = {'operating_threshold': self.operating_threshold,
                'quantised': self._quantised,
                'model_name': self.model_name}
        (p / 'meta.json').write_text(json.dumps(meta))
        print(f'Saved NeuralInjectionClassifier -> {path}')

    @classmethod
    def load(cls, path: str) -> 'NeuralInjectionClassifier':
        p = Path(path)
        meta = json.loads((p / 'meta.json').read_text())
        obj = cls(operating_threshold=meta['operating_threshold'])
        obj._tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(str(p / 'tokenizer'))
        obj._model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(str(p / 'model'))
        obj._quantised = meta.get('quantised', False)
        obj._trained = True
        return obj


if __name__ == '__main__':
    print('NeuralInjectionClassifier requires 2,000+ labelled examples and GPU.')
    print('For smoke test with fewer examples, use RandomForestClassifier (Section 12.05).')
    if not TRANSFORMERS_AVAILABLE:
        print('Install: pip install transformers torch datasets')
    else:
        clf = NeuralInjectionClassifier()
        print(f'Model: {clf.model_name}  Device: {clf.device}')
        print('Ready to fine_tune(train_df, val_df).')

# neural_jailbreak_classifier.py
# Section 12.08: DistilBERT Jailbreak Classifier (Multi-Class Extension)
# 20260606 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 12 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from __future__ import annotations

import time
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Dict, List, Optional

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import torch

try:
    from transformers import (
        AutoTokenizer,
        AutoModelForSequenceClassification,
        TrainingArguments,
        Trainer,
        EarlyStoppingCallback,
    )
    TRANSFORMERS_AVAILABLE = True
except ImportError:
    TRANSFORMERS_AVAILABLE = False

from neural_injection_classifier import (
    SecurityTextDataset, compute_metrics, MAX_LENGTH, MODEL_NAME,
)

# Three-class schema: benign=0, prompt_injection=1, jailbreak=2
LABEL_MAP = {'benign': 0, 'prompt_injection': 1, 'jailbreak': 2}
ID_TO_LABEL = {v: k for k, v in LABEL_MAP.items()}


@dataclass
class MultiClassPrediction:
    request_text: str
    predicted_class: str
    class_probabilities: Dict[str, float]
    is_attack: bool
    inference_ms: float


class NeuralJailbreakClassifier:
    '''
    Three-class DistilBERT classifier: benign / prompt_injection / jailbreak.
    Use when multi-class routing to specialist analysts is required and
    the per-class labelled dataset exceeds 2,000 examples each.
    For binary detection, use NeuralInjectionClassifier.
    '''

    def __init__(
        self,
        model_name: str = MODEL_NAME,
        device: Optional[str] = None,
    ) -> None:
        self.model_name = model_name
        self.device = device or ('cuda' if torch.cuda.is_available() else 'cpu')
        self._model = None
        self._tokenizer = None
        self._trained = False

    def fine_tune(
        self,
        train_df: pd.DataFrame,
        val_df: pd.DataFrame,
        output_dir: str = '/tmp/distilbert_jailbreak',
        num_train_epochs: int = 5,
        learning_rate: float = 2e-5,
        per_device_batch_size: int = 16,
    ) -> dict:
        '''
        train_df and val_df must have 'text' and 'attack_class' columns.
        attack_class values: 'benign', 'prompt_injection', 'jailbreak'.
        '''
        assert TRANSFORMERS_AVAILABLE
        # Convert string labels to integer IDs
        train_df = train_df.copy()
        val_df = val_df.copy()
        train_df['label'] = train_df['attack_class'].map(LABEL_MAP).fillna(0).astype(int)
        val_df['label'] = val_df['attack_class'].map(LABEL_MAP).fillna(0).astype(int)

        self._tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(self.model_name)
        self._model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(
            self.model_name, num_labels=len(LABEL_MAP),
        )
        train_ds = SecurityTextDataset(
            train_df['text'].tolist(), train_df['label'].tolist(), self._tokenizer,
        )
        val_ds = SecurityTextDataset(
            val_df['text'].tolist(), val_df['label'].tolist(), self._tokenizer,
        )
        args = TrainingArguments(
            output_dir=output_dir,
            num_train_epochs=num_train_epochs,
            per_device_train_batch_size=per_device_batch_size,
            learning_rate=learning_rate,
            warmup_ratio=0.06,
            weight_decay=0.01,
            fp16=self.device == 'cuda',
            evaluation_strategy='epoch',
            save_strategy='epoch',
            load_best_model_at_end=True,
            metric_for_best_model='f1',
            report_to='none',
        )
        trainer = Trainer(
            model=self._model,
            args=args,
            train_dataset=train_ds,
            eval_dataset=val_ds,
            compute_metrics=compute_metrics,
            callbacks=[EarlyStoppingCallback(early_stopping_patience=2)],
        )
        result = trainer.train()
        self._trained = True
        return dict(result.metrics)

    def predict(self, text: str) -> MultiClassPrediction:
        assert self._trained and self._tokenizer is not None
        t0 = time.time()
        self._model.eval()
        enc = self._tokenizer(
            text, truncation=True, padding='max_length',
            max_length=MAX_LENGTH, return_tensors='pt',
        )
        with torch.no_grad():
            logits = self._model(**enc).logits
        probs = torch.softmax(logits, dim=1)[0].tolist()
        predicted_id = int(np.argmax(probs))
        predicted_class = ID_TO_LABEL[predicted_id]
        return MultiClassPrediction(
            request_text=text[:200],
            predicted_class=predicted_class,
            class_probabilities={ID_TO_LABEL[i]: float(p) for i, p in enumerate(probs)},
            is_attack=predicted_class != 'benign',
            inference_ms=(time.time() - t0) * 1000,
        )


if __name__ == '__main__':
    print('NeuralJailbreakClassifier: three-class (benign/injection/jailbreak)')
    print('Requires 2,000+ examples per class and GPU for training.')
    print('Label map:', LABEL_MAP)

from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class NeuralEvaluationReport:
    base: EvaluationReport
    calibration_ece: float    # Expected Calibration Error
    latency_p50_ms: float
    latency_p95_ms: float
    latency_p99_ms: float
    quantised: bool
    n_params_millions: Optional[float]


def expected_calibration_error(
    y_true: np.ndarray,
    y_prob: np.ndarray,
    n_bins: int = 10,
) -> float:
    '''Compute Expected Calibration Error.'''
    bin_edges = np.linspace(0, 1, n_bins + 1)
    ece = 0.0
    for i in range(n_bins):
        mask = (y_prob >= bin_edges[i]) & (y_prob < bin_edges[i + 1])
        if mask.sum() == 0:
            continue
        avg_conf = float(y_prob[mask].mean())
        avg_acc = float(y_true[mask].mean())
        ece += (mask.sum() / len(y_true)) * abs(avg_conf - avg_acc)
    return ece


def profile_inference_latency(
    predict_fn: Callable[[str], object],
    sample_texts: List[str],
    n_warmup: int = 5,
    n_measure: int = 50,
) -> Tuple[float, float, float]:
    '''Returns (p50, p95, p99) latency in ms.'''
    for t in sample_texts[:n_warmup]:
        predict_fn(t)
    latencies = []
    for i in range(n_measure):
        text = sample_texts[i % len(sample_texts)]
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        predict_fn(text)
        latencies.append((time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000)
    arr = np.array(latencies)
    return float(np.percentile(arr, 50)), float(np.percentile(arr, 95)), float(np.percentile(arr, 99))


def evaluate_neural_classifier(
    classifier_name: str,
    y_true: np.ndarray,
    y_prob: np.ndarray,
    predict_fn: Optional[Callable] = None,
    sample_texts: Optional[List[str]] = None,
    operating_threshold: float = 0.5,
    quantised: bool = False,
    n_params_millions: Optional[float] = None,
) -> NeuralEvaluationReport:
    base = evaluate(classifier_name, y_true, y_prob, operating_threshold)
    ece = expected_calibration_error(y_true, y_prob)

    p50 = p95 = p99 = 0.0
    if predict_fn and sample_texts:
        p50, p95, p99 = profile_inference_latency(predict_fn, sample_texts)

    return NeuralEvaluationReport(
        base=base,
        calibration_ece=ece,
        latency_p50_ms=p50,
        latency_p95_ms=p95,
        latency_p99_ms=p99,
        quantised=quantised,
        n_params_millions=n_params_millions,
    )


def plot_calibration(
    y_true: np.ndarray,
    y_prob: np.ndarray,
    classifier_name: str,
    save_path: str = 'calibration_plot.png',
) -> None:
    frac_pos, mean_pred = calibration_curve(y_true, y_prob, n_bins=10)
    ece = expected_calibration_error(y_true, y_prob)
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 5))
    ax.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], 'k--', lw=1, label='Perfect calibration')
    ax.plot(mean_pred, frac_pos, 'o-', label=f'{classifier_name} (ECE={ece:.3f})')
    ax.set_xlabel('Mean predicted probability')
    ax.set_ylabel('Fraction of positives')
    ax.set_title('Calibration Plot')
    ax.legend()
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig(save_path, dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    plt.close()
    print(f'Saved calibration plot: {save_path}')


def print_neural_report(report: NeuralEvaluationReport) -> None:
    b = report.base
    print(f'\nNeural Classifier Evaluation: {b.classifier_name}')
    print(f'  Test set:   {b.n_test} ({b.n_attack} attack, {b.n_benign} benign)')
    print(f'  Precision:  {b.precision:.4f}')
    print(f'  Recall:     {b.recall:.4f}')
    print(f'  F1:         {b.f1:.4f}')
    print(f'  ROC-AUC:    {b.roc_auc:.4f}')
    print(f'  PR-AUC:     {b.pr_auc:.4f}')
    print(f'  FPR:        {b.fpr_at_threshold:.4f}')
    print(f'  ECE:        {report.calibration_ece:.4f}')
    if report.latency_p99_ms > 0:
        budget_ok = report.latency_p99_ms <= 15.0
        print(f'  Latency:    P50={report.latency_p50_ms:.1f}ms  '
              f'P95={report.latency_p95_ms:.1f}ms  '
              f'P99={report.latency_p99_ms:.1f}ms  '
              f'[{"WITHIN" if budget_ok else "OVER"} 15ms slice budget]')
    print(f'  Quantised:  {report.quantised}')
    if report.n_params_millions:
        print(f'  Parameters: {report.n_params_millions:.0f}M')

# Section 12.08: DistilBERT vs Random Forest Comparison
# 20260606 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 12 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Callable, Dict, List, Optional, Tuple

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.metrics import precision_score, recall_score, f1_score, roc_auc_score

from security_evaluator import EvaluationReport, evaluate, compare_classifiers


@dataclass
class CrossoverAnalysis:
    crossover_n: Optional[int]   # dataset size where DistilBERT first outperforms RF
    rf_wins_below: int
    distilbert_wins_above: int
    results_by_size: List[Dict]


def compare_at_size(
    rf_probs: np.ndarray,
    bert_probs: np.ndarray,
    y_true: np.ndarray,
    n_train: int,
    threshold: float = 0.5,
) -> Tuple[EvaluationReport, EvaluationReport]:
    rf_report = evaluate(f'RandomForest (n={n_train})', y_true, rf_probs, threshold)
    bert_report = evaluate(f'DistilBERT (n={n_train})', y_true, bert_probs, threshold)
    return rf_report, bert_report


def crossover_analysis(
    rf_prob_by_size: Dict[int, np.ndarray],
    bert_prob_by_size: Dict[int, np.ndarray],
    y_true: np.ndarray,
    threshold: float = 0.5,
) -> CrossoverAnalysis:
    '''
    Given RF and DistilBERT probability arrays at multiple training set sizes,
    find the crossover point where DistilBERT begins to outperform RF.
    '''
    results = []
    crossover_n = None

    for n in sorted(rf_prob_by_size.keys()):
        rf_report, bert_report = compare_at_size(
            rf_prob_by_size[n], bert_prob_by_size[n], y_true, n, threshold,
        )
        bert_wins = bert_report.f1 > rf_report.f1
        results.append({
            'n_train': n,
            'rf_f1': rf_report.f1,
            'rf_precision': rf_report.precision,
            'rf_recall': rf_report.recall,
            'bert_f1': bert_report.f1,
            'bert_precision': bert_report.precision,
            'bert_recall': bert_report.recall,
            'bert_wins': bert_wins,
        })
        if bert_wins and crossover_n is None:
            crossover_n = n

    rf_wins_below = crossover_n - 1 if crossover_n else max(rf_prob_by_size.keys())
    bert_wins_above = crossover_n or max(rf_prob_by_size.keys())

    return CrossoverAnalysis(
        crossover_n=crossover_n,
        rf_wins_below=rf_wins_below,
        distilbert_wins_above=bert_wins_above,
        results_by_size=results,
    )


def print_comparison_table(analysis: CrossoverAnalysis) -> None:
    print('\nRandom Forest vs DistilBERT -- Performance by Training Set Size')
    print('=' * 75)
    fmt = '{:>8}  {:>8} {:>8} {:>8}  {:>8} {:>8} {:>8}  {}'
    print(fmt.format('n_train', 'RF-F1', 'RF-P', 'RF-R', 'BERT-F1', 'BERT-P', 'BERT-R', 'Winner'))
    print('-' * 75)
    for r in analysis.results_by_size:
        winner = 'DistilBERT' if r['bert_wins'] else 'RandomForest'
        print(fmt.format(
            r['n_train'],
            f'{r["rf_f1"]:.3f}', f'{r["rf_precision"]:.3f}', f'{r["rf_recall"]:.3f}',
            f'{r["bert_f1"]:.3f}', f'{r["bert_precision"]:.3f}', f'{r["bert_recall"]:.3f}',
            winner,
        ))
    print('=' * 75)
    if analysis.crossover_n:
        print(f'\nCrossover: DistilBERT outperforms RandomForest at n >= {analysis.crossover_n}')
        print(f'  Use RandomForest when n < {analysis.crossover_n}')
        print(f'  Use DistilBERT when n >= {analysis.crossover_n}')
    else:
        print('\nNo crossover found -- RandomForest outperforms DistilBERT across all tested sizes.')
        print('  Possible cause: insufficient DistilBERT fine-tuning data or annotation quality below kappa=0.85')

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Fine-tuning DistilBERT with fewer than 1,000 labelled examples and reporting the resulting overfitted accuracy as justification for replacing the Random Forest

Why It Happens

The engineer has accumulated 800 labelled examples and wants to try the neural classifier. The training set of 560 examples (70% split) produces a fine-tuned DistilBERT that achieves 96% training accuracy. The temporal test set accuracy is 78% -- lower than the Random Forest's 87% at the same data size -- but the engineer reports the training accuracy, which is the most visible metric during the training run.

Consequence

The overfitted DistilBERT replaces the Random Forest in the primary injection detection track. Its 78% accuracy on real production traffic misses 22% of attacks the Random Forest was catching. The recall regression is discovered only after a production incident, not from the evaluation metrics that were available before deployment.

Correct Approach

Always evaluate on the temporal test set, never on the training set. The bert_vs_rf_comparison.py in deliverable_5 makes this comparison explicit: it plots F1 on the temporal test set at each dataset size. Do not fine-tune DistilBERT until the temporal test set F1 exceeds the Random Forest F1 at the same data size. The crossover is typically at 1,500-2,000 examples.

Mistake 2

Description

Deploying FP32 DistilBERT to production without INT8 quantisation, causing the detector to exceed the 50ms gateway budget when combined with other detectors

Why It Happens

The fine-tuning completes and the model evaluates at 95% accuracy on the temporal test set. The engineer saves the FP32 model and registers it. Quantisation is documented in Section 12.08 but the engineer defers it as an optimisation step to apply after the initial deployment proves the accuracy. The initial deployment runs four detectors in sequence; the gateway P99 is 62ms, exceeding the 50ms SLO.

Consequence

The gateway latency SLO is breached on the first day of DistilBERT deployment. The product team observes elevated API latency and flags it as a regression. Either the DistilBERT detector is disabled (removing the accuracy improvement it was deployed for) or the other detectors are disabled (removing their coverage) to recover the latency SLO.

Correct Approach

Apply INT8 dynamic quantisation before registering any DistilBERT model in the Section 12.13 registry. The model registry stage transition gate must include a latency check (P99 <= 10ms for the neural classifier budget slice) as a required gate condition. The quantise_int8() method in neural_injection_classifier.py is a one-line addition to the fine-tuning pipeline.

Mistake 3

Description

Using the same learning rate for fine-tuning DistilBERT as for training Random Forest (e.g., 0.1 or 0.01) and destroying the pre-trained representations

Why It Happens

The engineer follows the Optuna hyperparameter search from Section 12.05, which explored learning rates in the 0.01-0.1 range for tree classifiers. When configuring the DistilBERT fine-tuning, they use the same range without reading the fine-tuning documentation.

Consequence

A learning rate of 0.01 in the first training step applies gradient updates approximately 500x larger than the recommended 2e-5. The pre-trained weight representations are overwritten in the first few batches. The model converges to a random weight initialisation rather than fine-tuning from the pre-trained state. Training accuracy increases, but the model has learned nothing from the pre-training -- it is training from scratch on 560 examples, which is far too few to produce a useful classifier.

Correct Approach

Use 2e-5 as the default learning rate for DistilBERT fine-tuning on security datasets. If exploring a range with Optuna, constrain to [5e-6, 5e-5] with a log-uniform distribution. Never use learning rates above 1e-4 for fine-tuning pre-trained transformer models on small domain-specific datasets.

Mistake 4

Description

Omitting the model card and registering the DistilBERT model without documentation, preventing the operations team from safely configuring alert thresholds

Why It Happens

The model card generation is the last step before registry submission. The engineer has been iterating on the fine-tuning for two weeks and wants to deploy. The model card feels like paperwork. It is skipped with the intention of adding it after the deployment is validated.

Consequence

The operations team configures the alert threshold at 0.5 (the default) because they have no information about the calibrated operating threshold. The calibrated threshold was 0.42 (from the precision-recall curve analysis). At 0.5, the classifier misses 15% of attacks that the calibrated threshold would have caught. The known_limitations field, which documented that fictional frame jailbreaks have 74% recall, was also missing -- the operations team does not know to route fictional frame alerts through a supplementary detection path.

Correct Approach

The Section 12.13 model registry enforces the model card as a Staging entry gate. The model cannot be registered at Staging without a valid model card JSON. The model_card_generator.py in deliverable_4 takes 10 minutes to populate from the training and evaluation artefacts. Generate it as part of the training pipeline, not as an afterthought.

Mistake 5

Description

Not applying layer-wise learning rate differentiation, using the same 2e-5 rate for all layers including the lower linguistic representation layers

Why It Happens

The Hugging Face Trainer documentation demonstrates a single learning_rate parameter for the entire model. The engineer follows the tutorial exactly. The single learning rate is correct for the upper layers and the classification head but is too high for the lower BERT layers.

Consequence

The lower BERT layers, which encode general linguistic structure, receive the same gradient magnitude as the upper layers and the classification head. Over 3-5 training epochs, the lower layers drift from their pre-trained representations. This is subtle catastrophic forgetting: the model still works, but its generalisation to novel phrasings degrades. The effect is most visible on the paraphrase evasion test from Section 12.07 -- the DistilBERT classifier becomes more pattern-matching than semantic, partially defeating the advantage over the Random Forest.

Correct Approach

Apply layer-wise learning rate differentiation using a parameter group configuration: lower layers (layers 0-2) at 5e-6, upper layers (layers 3-5) at 1e-5, and the classification head at 5e-5. This preserves the pre-trained linguistic representations in the lower layers while allowing the upper layers and classification head to specialise for the security domain. The implementation requires a custom optimizer setup with separate parameter groups rather than the single learning_rate argument.

Section Summary

Compact

Fine-tuned DistilBERT is the accuracy ceiling of Chapter 12 -- 95%+ on the temporal test set at 2,000+ labelled examples, 5-10 percentage points above the Random Forest in precision at matched recall. It earns this position through transfer learning: pre-training on large text corpora provides language understanding that the fine-tuning task converts to security-domain discrimination at a fraction of the data cost of training from scratch. INT8 dynamic quantisation is mandatory for production: it reduces 15ms FP32 inference to 7ms, making DistilBERT compatible with the 50ms gateway budget alongside the other detectors. The model card is a registry entry gate, not optional documentation: it records training data, evaluation methodology, performance metrics, known limitations, recommended usage, and lineage in a format that the operations team requires to safely configure thresholds and the ensemble requires to correctly weight the detector.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not fine-tune DistilBERT before 2,000 labelled examples -- below this threshold the Random Forest outperforms DistilBERT on the temporal test set in every operationally relevant metric
  • INT8 dynamic quantisation is not optional for production -- FP32 DistilBERT at 15ms combined with three other detectors breaches the 50ms gateway budget; quantisation to 7ms is a deployment correctness requirement
  • Use 2e-5 as the default learning rate; any rate above 5e-5 risks catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained representations on small security datasets
  • The model card is a registry entry gate -- no DistilBERT model enters the Section 12.13 registry at Staging without a valid model card covering all six required fields
  • DistilBERT has post-hoc explainability only -- attention visualisation is not a substitute for SHAP feature importance; for alert triage requiring feature-level explanation, Random Forest remains the correct tool regardless of DistilBERT accuracy
  • Both DistilBERT and Random Forest run in the Section 12.15 ensemble -- the comparison from bert_vs_rf_comparison.py determines primary slot assignment; the ensemble meta-learner determines the combined output

Section 12.09: PII Leakage Scanner -- Regex, NER, and Ensemble Output Monitoring

Summary

Section 12.09 builds the PII scanner, which is architecturally distinct from the four input-side detectors: it scans model outputs, not inputs. The scanner catches OWASP LLM06 (Sensitive Information Disclosure) leakage before the response reaches the client. The ensemble combines three components: a regex layer for structured PII (email addresses, phone numbers, credit card numbers, SSNs), a spaCy NER layer (en_core_web_trf) for unstructured PII (person names, organisations, locations), and the Microsoft Presidio framework for context-aware PII scoring. False positive management addresses the common case where the LLM generates examples, fictional names, or synthetic data that triggers the PII patterns; context scoring distinguishes real from fictional PII. The scanner runs as a post-inference gateway stage (outside the seven tracks) that applies to every response before it leaves the system.
