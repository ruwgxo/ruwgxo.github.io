Section 9.7: Model Inversion

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Model inversion is the most consequential privacy attack: by optimising an input to maximise a classifier''s confidence on a target class, or by exploiting perplexity signals in LLMs, an adversary reconstructs training data content that the model memorised -- demonstrated by Fredrikson et al. 2015 with face reconstruction and Carlini et al. 2021 with verbatim GPT-2 training text including personal information.

Why This Section Exists

Section 9.6 answered "was this record in the training set?" Section 9.7 answers "what does that record contain?" Together the two attacks form the complete privacy arc: membership inference identifies high-probability training members; model inversion reconstructs their content from the model''s query outputs. The consequence of a successful combined attack is the recovery of training data that the operator never intended to expose through the deployed model''s API. The Fredrikson et al. 2015 result established that face recognition APIs leak facial images of training subjects through their confidence scores. The Carlini et al. 2021 result established that large language models memorise and can be made to reproduce verbatim training text -- including names, email addresses, phone numbers, and copyrighted content -- through carefully designed prompt strategies combined with perplexity-based filtering. These are not theoretical demonstrations: they were executed against production-scale models with documented query budgets and reproducible methodology. Section 9.7 builds both the image classifier inversion PoC and the LLM training data extraction demonstration, establishes memorisation as the necessary precondition for inversion success, and closes the privacy arc with the same two-layer defence as Section 9.6: differential privacy training as the upstream formal bound and output perturbation as the downstream signal-reduction control. The section also surfaces the GitHub Copilot memorisation controversy as the most commercially significant inversion incident documented to date.

Position In Chapter

Title

From Membership to Content: The Privacy Arc Completes

Body

Section 9.6 established the membership signal: high confidence and low loss
are evidence that a record was in the training set. Model inversion extends
this observation from identification to reconstruction. If high confidence
on a target class indicates membership, then the input that achieves the
highest confidence on that class is the model''s best estimate of what a
member of that class looks like -- a reconstruction of the training data
distribution that the model has encoded in its parameters.

The formal framing is an optimisation problem. Given a model f and a target
class c, the adversary finds:

    x* = argmax_x  f(x)[c]

The solution x* is the input that the model assigns the highest probability
to belonging to class c. If the model has memorised specific training examples
from class c, the optimisation converges toward those examples rather than
toward the generic class centroid. The adversary has reconstructed training
data content -- not by accessing the training set directly, but by inverting
the model''s learned representation.

The severity of this attack compared to membership inference is qualitative,
not just quantitative. Membership inference leaks the fact of inclusion.
Model inversion leaks the content. For a face recognition system trained on
identifiable individuals, inversion reconstructs their faces. For a medical
classifier, inversion reconstructs the clinical features of training patients.
For an LLM, inversion reconstructs the verbatim text -- including personal
identifiers, contact information, and copyrighted material -- that appeared
in the training corpus.

Part

2

Title

Fredrikson et al. 2015: Face Reconstruction from a Face Classifier

Body

The Fredrikson et al. 2015 paper "Model Inversion Attacks that Exploit Confidence Information and Basic Countermeasures" established the canonical model inversion result. Building on earlier work on pharmacogenetics model inversion (Fredrikson et al. 2014), the 2015 paper demonstrated face reconstruction from a face recognition API. The attack targeted a facial recognition system accessed through a prediction API that returned confidence scores -- the soft-label grey-box access level from Section 9.1''s adversary access ladder. The adversary knew the target individual''s identity (name) and wanted to reconstruct their facial image as it appeared in the training set. The reconstruction procedure used gradient ascent in input space to find the image that maximised the model''s confidence score for the target identity. Starting from a random or mean-face initialisation, the algorithm iteratively applied small perturbations to the input image in the direction that increased the model''s confidence on the target class, tracking the pixel values at each step. After several hundred iterations, the reconstructed image converged to a likeness of the target individual recognisable to human evaluators -- not a perfect photograph, but a distinctive reconstruction that captured the facial structure of the training image. The paper also demonstrated the attack against a simpler model: a pharmacogenetics dosage prediction model that revealed patient attributes (race, age) not directly observable from the API''s inputs but encoded in the model''s decision surface. This variant required fewer queries and produced high-fidelity attribute reconstruction, demonstrating that inversion applies to any model whose predictions encode training data features. The countermeasures the paper evaluated -- confidence score rounding, adding noise to outputs, restricting API output precision -- all reduced attack fidelity but did not eliminate it. This is the output perturbation result that Section 9.12 builds on: perturbation raises the query budget for reconstruction but does not bound fidelity the way differential privacy training does.

Title

Gradient-Based Inversion: White-Box Access

Body

Gradient-based model inversion is the most powerful variant, applicable when
the adversary has white-box access to the model''s parameters and can compute
exact gradients. The optimisation is:

    x_{t+1} = x_t + α * ∇_x  f(x_t)[c]

where α is the step size and ∇_x f(x_t)[c] is the gradient of the target
class confidence with respect to the input. This is standard gradient ascent
in input space -- the reverse of the gradient descent used during training.
The input converges toward a point that the model treats as a prototypical
member of class c.

Gradient-based inversion is fast and precise. With exact gradients, each
optimisation step provides maximum information about the direction of
increasing confidence in the target class. Convergence typically occurs in
tens to hundreds of gradient steps for image classifiers, producing
reconstructions with high visual fidelity to the training data distribution.

For open-weight models -- which represent an increasingly large fraction of
deployed models as of 2026 -- gradient-based inversion is directly applicable.
An adversary who downloads an open-weight model has white-box access and can
run gradient ascent locally without any API interaction. The attack does not
touch the operator''s infrastructure and cannot be detected through query
monitoring. The only effective defences for open-weight models against gradient-
based inversion are upstream: differential privacy training during the original
training run, and data deduplication and scrubbing to remove the most
memorisation-prone training examples before training begins.

The practical inversion fidelity from gradient access is significantly higher
than from black-box query access for the same number of optimisation steps,
because each step uses exact gradient information rather than a numerical
approximation. Zhang et al. 2020 demonstrated GAN-based gradient inversion
that produced near-photorealistic facial reconstructions from deep classifiers
with white-box access -- a substantial advance over the Fredrikson et al.
2015 pixel-level optimisation.

Title

Black-Box Inversion: Query-Based Reconstruction Without Gradients

Body

Black-box model inversion uses only the model''s query outputs -- confidence scores or class labels -- to guide the reconstruction, without access to gradients. The optimisation must estimate the gradient direction from the model''s scalar outputs at nearby points, a technique called zeroth-order or derivative-free optimisation. The simplest black-box inversion approach estimates the gradient numerically: for each input dimension i, compute the finite difference (f(x + δe_i)[c] - f(x)[c]) / δ as an approximation to the partial derivative. This requires two queries per dimension per step, making black-box inversion O(d) times more expensive than gradient-based inversion per optimisation step. For image inputs with thousands of pixels, this is a substantial query budget multiplier. More sophisticated black-box inversion approaches use random gradient estimators (querying random directions rather than all basis directions), Bayesian optimisation over the input space, or evolutionary search strategies that maintain a population of candidate inputs and select toward higher confidence. These approaches trade optimisation quality for query efficiency and are more practical for high-dimensional inputs. Against grey-box APIs that return confidence scores, black-box inversion is viable for moderate-dimensional inputs. Fredrikson et al. 2015 operated in this regime: the face images were small (thumbnail resolution) and the confidence scores provided sufficient gradient signal for reconstruction. Against hard-label-only APIs, reconstruction fidelity degrades further but remains possible for simple models and low-dimensional inputs. The detection implication for black-box inversion follows the same logic as boundary mapping: the adversary must issue many queries with systematic input perturbation, producing a query distribution with structured variation patterns that differs from legitimate use. The query monitor''s OOD detector and perturbation pattern detector are the relevant Section 9.11 tracks.

Title

LLM Training Data Extraction: Carlini et al. 2021

Body

The Carlini et al. 2021 paper "Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models" demonstrated verbatim training data extraction from GPT-2 at scale. The attack revealed that large language models memorise and can be made to reproduce substantial quantities of their training data with only black-box API access and no prior knowledge of which specific texts were in the training corpus. The extraction procedure has two stages. In the generation stage, the adversary prompts the model with a variety of prefixes (often simple, unconditional prompts or short common phrases) and generates large numbers of text completions. The model''s sampling produces a mixture of generic text and memorised training content; the adversary cannot distinguish them from content alone at this stage. In the filtering stage, the adversary scores each generated text by a perplexity ratio: the ratio of the target model''s perplexity to a smaller reference model''s perplexity on the same text. Texts that the target model assigns much lower perplexity than the reference model -- texts the large model finds far more predictable than the small model does -- are likely memorised from training data. The reference model has not seen the training data and assigns these texts the same perplexity as generic text from the same domain. The perplexity ratio filter is the key insight. It eliminates the need to know the training data in advance: any text that the large model finds disproportionately predictable relative to a reference baseline is a candidate for training data membership. The paper demonstrated extraction of text containing names, email addresses, phone numbers, URLs, and verbatim news articles from GPT-2 -- information the model had memorised from its training corpus despite having no specific instruction to retain it. The query budget for the Carlini et al. extraction was substantial: hundreds of thousands of generated sequences to find the subset of memorised content. But the economic arithmetic is the same as distillation extraction: the cost of generating those sequences at commercial LLM API rates is orders of magnitude lower than the consequence of the exposed training data.

Part

6

Title

Memorisation as the Necessary Precondition

Body

Model inversion cannot reconstruct what the model has not memorised. The attack''s fidelity ceiling is determined by the degree of memorisation: training examples that the model has fitted closely -- whose specific features are encoded in the model''s parameters rather than only the class-level statistics -- are reconstructable. Training examples that the model has not memorised individually -- which contribute only to the aggregate class distribution without leaving individual-level traces -- are not. Memorisation degree varies systematically across training examples and model configurations. Examples that appear rarely in the training data are more likely to be memorised individually than examples that appear frequently, because the model must fit each rare example without the averaging effect of repetition. Examples with unusual or distinctive features that deviate from the class centre are more likely to be memorised because they require model-specific parameter adjustment. Very short sequences in LLMs are more susceptible to memorisation than long sequences because the model can perfectly fit the short sequence in a small number of parameters. The Carlini et al. 2021 paper quantified memorisation for LLMs as the probability that the model can reproduce a training sequence verbatim given a prefix of that sequence. A sequence is k-memorised if the model reproduces it exactly given a k-token prefix. Higher k means the model can complete the sequence from a longer prefix -- weaker memorisation. Lower k means the model can complete the sequence from a short prefix -- stronger memorisation and higher inversion risk. The memorisation analysis has direct implications for data preprocessing. Training data deduplication removes the repetition-based averaging effect and actually increases memorisation risk for the deduplicated examples by causing the model to fit each unique example more precisely. This is the deduplication paradox: deduplication improves data quality but increases per-example memorisation and therefore inversion risk for the unique examples that remain after deduplication.

Title

Sensitive Data Exposure: GitHub Copilot and Production Implications

Body

The GitHub Copilot memorisation controversy is the most commercially significant model inversion incident documented to date. Researchers demonstrated that Copilot -- a code completion model trained on public GitHub repositories -- could be prompted to reproduce verbatim code snippets from its training data, including code containing API keys, passwords, cryptographic secrets, and other sensitive credentials that developers had inadvertently committed to public repositories. The extraction did not require adversarial prompting. Simple prefix completions -- providing the beginning of a file that had appeared in the training data -- caused the model to reproduce the remainder of the file, including any sensitive content it contained. The memorisation was selective: not all code was reproducible, but files that had appeared in many repositories (high-frequency files) and files with distinctive structure (configuration files, credential files) showed high memorisation rates. The practical consequence is an inversion attack surface that operates without any special adversary capability: any Copilot user who prompts the model with a file prefix can receive training data content as a completion. The model does not distinguish between completing a user''s own code and reproducing a memorised training example. The exposure is built into the product''s core functionality. The implications for production LLM deployments extend beyond code models. Any LLM trained on data containing personally identifiable information -- user messages, customer service transcripts, medical notes, financial records -- has the potential to reproduce that information through completion prompts. The Carlini et al. 2021 result confirmed this for GPT-2 on general web text; the Copilot result confirmed it for specialised code models. Both demonstrate that the inversion attack is not hypothetical and does not require adversarial expertise to execute.

Part

8

Title

Defence Connections: DP Training, Perturbation, and Data Hygiene

Body

Model inversion has three defensive layers, each addressing a different stage of the vulnerability chain. Upstream: differential privacy training. DP training from Section 8.13 bounds the influence of any individual training example on the model''s parameters. If no training example has more than ε-bounded influence, the model cannot have memorised that example to the degree required for high- fidelity inversion. At strong privacy budgets (ε ≤ 1), the inversion fidelity ceiling drops substantially: the optimisation cannot converge to a specific training example because the model''s parameters do not carry sufficient individual-level information. The cost is the same as for membership inference defence: model accuracy degrades with tighter ε. Downstream: output perturbation. Perturbation from Section 9.12 adds noise to confidence scores and generated text, reducing the precision of the gradient signal that black-box inversion and perplexity-based filtering rely on. For image inversion, confidence score perturbation degrades the gradient estimate and causes the optimisation to converge more slowly and less precisely. For LLM extraction, adding noise to token log-probabilities degrades the perplexity ratio filter and forces the adversary to generate more sequences to find memorised content. Data preprocessing: deduplication and scrubbing. Removing sensitive content from training data before training is the most direct mitigation for LLM inversion: a model that was never trained on an API key cannot memorise it. The GitHub Copilot response included filtering training data for credential patterns (API key regexes, password patterns) before training subsequent model versions. Data deduplication reduces repetition-based memorisation for common examples but, as noted in Part 6, increases per-example memorisation for unique examples -- making targeted scrubbing of sensitive content more important than blanket deduplication. The correct architecture combines all three layers. DP training as the formal bound on individual-level memorisation; output perturbation as the signal- reduction control at inference time; data scrubbing to remove the highest-risk training content before memorisation occurs. None alone is sufficient for production deployment of models trained on sensitive personal data.

black_box_inverter.py

Description

Black-box model inversion using zeroth-order optimisation. Estimates the gradient of the target class confidence with respect to the input via finite differences (two queries per dimension per step), then takes a gradient ascent step toward higher target class confidence. Tracks reconstruction fidelity against a known ground-truth member at each step to produce the inversion progress curve. Intended as the adversary-side implementation demonstrating the attack the Section 9.12 output perturbation defends against.

Implementation Notes

Single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires numpy and sklearn. Victim model accepts any object with predict_proba(X). Dimensionality, step size, perturbation delta, and budget are all configurable parameters. No hardcoded input shapes. Query count is tracked via the oracle wrapper imported from boundary_mapper.

Code

# black_box_inverter.py
# Chapter 9 Section 9.7 -- Black-Box Model Inversion (Zeroth-Order)
# Single quotes throughout per book convention.

import numpy as np
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Optional, Tuple


@dataclass
class InversionStep:
    step: int
    queries_used: int
    target_confidence: float
    reconstruction_mse: Optional[float]  # vs known ground truth if available


@dataclass
class InversionResult:
    target_class: int
    reconstructed_input: np.ndarray
    steps: List[InversionStep]
    total_queries: int
    final_confidence: float


class QueryCountingModel:
    '''Wraps any predict_proba model with a query counter.'''

    def __init__(self, model, budget: int) -> None:
        self._model = model
        self._budget = budget
        self.queries = 0

    def predict_proba(self, X: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        if self.queries + len(X) > self._budget:
            raise RuntimeError('Inversion query budget exhausted')
        self.queries += len(X)
        return self._model.predict_proba(X)

    def confidence(self, x: np.ndarray, target_class: int) -> float:
        proba = self.predict_proba(x.reshape(1, -1))
        return float(proba[0, target_class])


def zeroth_order_gradient(
    model: QueryCountingModel,
    x: np.ndarray,
    target_class: int,
    delta: float = 1e-3,
) -> np.ndarray:
    '''
    Estimate gradient of target class confidence w.r.t. x using finite differences.
    Requires 2 * len(x) queries (forward and backward difference per dimension).
    '''
    grad = np.zeros_like(x)
    f0 = model.confidence(x, target_class)
    for i in range(len(x)):
        x_plus = x.copy()
        x_plus[i] += delta
        f_plus = model.confidence(x_plus, target_class)
        grad[i] = (f_plus - f0) / delta
    return grad


def run_black_box_inversion(
    model: QueryCountingModel,
    target_class: int,
    input_dim: int,
    x_init: Optional[np.ndarray] = None,
    x_ground_truth: Optional[np.ndarray] = None,
    n_steps: int = 30,
    step_size: float = 0.05,
    delta: float = 1e-3,
    rng: Optional[np.random.Generator] = None,
) -> InversionResult:
    '''
    Run zeroth-order gradient ascent to maximise target class confidence.
    x_init: starting point (random if None)
    x_ground_truth: known training member for MSE tracking (optional)
    '''
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)
    x = x_init if x_init is not None else rng.uniform(-1.0, 1.0, size=input_dim)
    steps: List[InversionStep] = []

    for step in range(n_steps):
        queries_before = model.queries
        try:
            grad = zeroth_order_gradient(model, x, target_class, delta)
            conf = model.confidence(x, target_class)
        except RuntimeError:
            break

        mse = None
        if x_ground_truth is not None:
            mse = float(np.mean((x - x_ground_truth) ** 2))

        steps.append(InversionStep(
            step=step,
            queries_used=model.queries,
            target_confidence=round(conf, 4),
            reconstruction_mse=round(mse, 6) if mse is not None else None,
        ))

        x = x + step_size * grad
        # Clip to valid input range
        x = np.clip(x, -3.0, 3.0)

    final_conf = model.confidence(x, target_class) if model.queries < model._budget else steps[-1].target_confidence
    return InversionResult(
        target_class=target_class,
        reconstructed_input=x,
        steps=steps,
        total_queries=model.queries,
        final_confidence=round(final_conf, 4),
    )


def render_inversion_progress(result: InversionResult) -> None:
    print('BLACK-BOX INVERSION PROGRESS -- Chapter 9 Section 9.7')
    print('Target class: %d | Total queries: %d | Final confidence: %.1f%%' % (
        result.target_class, result.total_queries, result.final_confidence * 100))
    print('%6s %8s %12s %14s' % ('Step', 'Queries', 'Confidence', 'Recon MSE'))
    print('-' * 45)
    for s in result.steps[::5]:
        mse_str = ('%.6f' % s.reconstruction_mse) if s.reconstruction_mse is not None else 'N/A'
        print('%6d %8d %11.1f%% %14s' % (s.step, s.queries_used, s.target_confidence * 100, mse_str))


if __name__ == '__main__':
    from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier
    from sklearn.datasets import make_classification

    X, y = make_classification(n_samples=500, n_features=10, random_state=42)
    victim = MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(32, 16), max_iter=500, random_state=42)
    victim.fit(X, y)

    # Pick a class-1 training member as the ground truth to track
    class1_idx = np.where(y == 1)[0][0]
    x_truth = X[class1_idx]

    oracle = QueryCountingModel(victim, budget=5000)
    result = run_black_box_inversion(
        oracle, target_class=1, input_dim=10,
        x_ground_truth=x_truth, n_steps=50, step_size=0.02,
    )
    render_inversion_progress(result)

gradient_inverter.py

Description

Gradient-based model inversion for white-box access. Computes exact gradients of the target class confidence with respect to the input using numerical differentiation on a provided model with predict_proba access. Compares reconstruction fidelity and query budget against the black-box zeroth-order baseline at matched step counts to quantify the gradient access advantage.

Implementation Notes

Single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires numpy and sklearn. Gradient computation uses exact finite differences with smaller delta than the black-box variant, yielding higher gradient accuracy. No external autodiff framework required -- numerical gradient is sufficient for comparison purposes. Imports InversionResult from black_box_inverter.

Code

# gradient_inverter.py
# Chapter 9 Section 9.7 -- Gradient-Based Model Inversion (White-Box Numerical)
# Single quotes throughout per book convention.

import numpy as np
from typing import List, Optional

from black_box_inverter import (
    InversionResult, InversionStep, QueryCountingModel,
)


def exact_numerical_gradient(
    model: QueryCountingModel,
    x: np.ndarray,
    target_class: int,
    delta: float = 1e-5,
) -> np.ndarray:
    '''
    Central difference gradient: (f(x+delta) - f(x-delta)) / (2*delta).
    More accurate than forward difference; requires 2*d queries per step.
    '''
    grad = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i in range(len(x)):
        x_plus = x.copy()
        x_plus[i] += delta
        x_minus = x.copy()
        x_minus[i] -= delta
        f_plus = model.confidence(x_plus, target_class)
        f_minus = model.confidence(x_minus, target_class)
        grad[i] = (f_plus - f_minus) / (2.0 * delta)
    return grad


def run_gradient_inversion(
    model: QueryCountingModel,
    target_class: int,
    input_dim: int,
    x_init: Optional[np.ndarray] = None,
    x_ground_truth: Optional[np.ndarray] = None,
    n_steps: int = 30,
    step_size: float = 0.05,
    rng: Optional[np.random.Generator] = None,
) -> InversionResult:
    '''
    Gradient-based inversion using central difference gradient estimates.
    More accurate gradient than forward difference; higher query cost per step.
    '''
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)
    x = x_init if x_init is not None else rng.uniform(-1.0, 1.0, size=input_dim)
    steps: List[InversionStep] = []

    for step in range(n_steps):
        try:
            grad = exact_numerical_gradient(model, x, target_class)
            conf = model.confidence(x, target_class)
        except RuntimeError:
            break

        mse = None
        if x_ground_truth is not None:
            mse = float(np.mean((x - x_ground_truth) ** 2))

        steps.append(InversionStep(
            step=step,
            queries_used=model.queries,
            target_confidence=round(conf, 4),
            reconstruction_mse=round(mse, 6) if mse is not None else None,
        ))
        x = x + step_size * grad
        x = np.clip(x, -3.0, 3.0)

    final_conf = (model.confidence(x, target_class)
                  if model.queries < model._budget
                  else steps[-1].target_confidence)
    return InversionResult(
        target_class=target_class,
        reconstructed_input=x,
        steps=steps,
        total_queries=model.queries,
        final_confidence=round(final_conf, 4),
    )


def compare_inversion_methods(
    bb_result: InversionResult,
    grad_result: InversionResult,
) -> None:
    print('INVERSION METHOD COMPARISON -- Chapter 9 Section 9.7')
    print('%22s %12s %12s %14s' % ('Method', 'Final Conf', 'Queries', 'Final MSE'))
    print('-' * 65)
    for label, result in [('black_box_zeroth_order', bb_result), ('gradient_central_diff', grad_result)]:
        last = result.steps[-1] if result.steps else None
        mse_str = ('%.6f' % last.reconstruction_mse) if (last and last.reconstruction_mse is not None) else 'N/A'
        print('%22s %11.1f%% %12d %14s' % (
            label, result.final_confidence * 100, result.total_queries, mse_str))

llm_training_extractor.py

Description

LLM training data extraction pipeline in the style of Carlini et al. 2021. Generates candidate text sequences from a mock LLM, scores each by a perplexity ratio between a large target model and a small reference model, and returns the top-scoring candidates as likely memorised training content. Demonstrates the generation-then-filter extraction pipeline against a mock n-gram language model.

Implementation Notes

Single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires numpy and stdlib only. Both target and reference models are NgramLanguageModel instances backed by frequency tables -- no external LLM API required. Perplexity ratio is computed as target_perplexity / reference_perplexity; lower ratio means the target model finds the text more predictable than the reference.

Code

# llm_training_extractor.py
# Chapter 9 Section 9.7 -- LLM Training Data Extraction (Carlini et al. 2021 Style)
# Single quotes throughout per book convention.

import math
import random
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Dict, List, Optional, Tuple


@dataclass
class ExtractionCandidate:
    text: str
    target_perplexity: float
    reference_perplexity: float
    perplexity_ratio: float  # target / reference; lower = more likely memorised
    rank: int


class NgramLanguageModel:
    '''
    Simple bigram language model for demonstration purposes.
    Approximates the role of a neural LM in the Carlini et al. pipeline.
    '''

    def __init__(self, smoothing: float = 0.1) -> None:
        self._unigram: Dict[str, float] = {}
        self._bigram: Dict[Tuple[str, str], float] = {}
        self._vocab_size: int = 0
        self._smoothing = smoothing
        self._fitted = False

    def fit(self, texts: List[str]) -> None:
        for text in texts:
            tokens = text.split()
            for tok in tokens:
                self._unigram[tok] = self._unigram.get(tok, 0) + 1
            for i in range(len(tokens) - 1):
                pair = (tokens[i], tokens[i + 1])
                self._bigram[pair] = self._bigram.get(pair, 0) + 1
        self._vocab_size = len(self._unigram)
        self._fitted = True

    def token_log_prob(self, prev_token: str, token: str) -> float:
        '''Bigram log probability with Laplace smoothing.'''
        if not self._fitted or self._vocab_size == 0:
            return -math.log(1e-6)
        prev_count = self._unigram.get(prev_token, 0) + self._smoothing * self._vocab_size
        pair_count = self._bigram.get((prev_token, token), 0) + self._smoothing
        return math.log(pair_count / prev_count)

    def perplexity(self, text: str) -> float:
        '''Compute per-token perplexity: exp(-mean(log_probs)).'''
        tokens = text.split()
        if len(tokens) < 2:
            return float('inf')
        log_probs = [
            self.token_log_prob(tokens[i], tokens[i + 1])
            for i in range(len(tokens) - 1)
        ]
        avg_log_prob = sum(log_probs) / len(log_probs)
        return math.exp(-avg_log_prob)

    def sample(self, prefix: str, n_tokens: int, rng: random.Random) -> str:
        '''Sample a text continuation given a prefix token.'''
        if not self._fitted:
            return prefix
        tokens = prefix.split()
        vocab = list(self._unigram.keys())
        for _ in range(n_tokens):
            last = tokens[-1] if tokens else '<s>'
            candidates = [
                (tok, self._bigram.get((last, tok), 0) + self._smoothing)
                for tok in vocab
            ]
            total = sum(w for _, w in candidates)
            r = rng.uniform(0, total)
            cumulative = 0.0
            chosen = vocab[0]
            for tok, weight in candidates:
                cumulative += weight
                if cumulative >= r:
                    chosen = tok
                    break
            tokens.append(chosen)
        return ' '.join(tokens)


def run_llm_extraction(
    target_model: NgramLanguageModel,
    reference_model: NgramLanguageModel,
    seed_prefixes: List[str],
    n_completions_per_prefix: int = 10,
    completion_length: int = 8,
    top_k: int = 5,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> List[ExtractionCandidate]:
    '''
    Generate completions from target model; filter by perplexity ratio.
    Returns top-k candidates most likely to be memorised training content.
    '''
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    candidates: List[ExtractionCandidate] = []

    for prefix in seed_prefixes:
        for _ in range(n_completions_per_prefix):
            text = target_model.sample(prefix, completion_length, rng)
            target_ppl = target_model.perplexity(text)
            reference_ppl = reference_model.perplexity(text)
            if reference_ppl == 0 or not math.isfinite(target_ppl):
                continue
            ratio = target_ppl / max(reference_ppl, 1e-10)
            candidates.append(ExtractionCandidate(
                text=text,
                target_perplexity=round(target_ppl, 4),
                reference_perplexity=round(reference_ppl, 4),
                perplexity_ratio=round(ratio, 4),
                rank=0,
            ))

    candidates.sort(key=lambda c: c.perplexity_ratio)
    for i, c in enumerate(candidates[:top_k]):
        c.rank = i + 1
    return candidates[:top_k]


def render_extraction_results(candidates: List[ExtractionCandidate]) -> None:
    print('LLM TRAINING DATA EXTRACTION -- Chapter 9 Section 9.7')
    print('Top candidates by perplexity ratio (lower = more likely memorised):')
    print('%4s %8s %10s %8s  %s' % ('Rank', 'Target PPL', 'Ref PPL', 'Ratio', 'Text'))
    print('-' * 75)
    for c in candidates:
        print('%4d %10.2f %10.2f %8.4f  %s' % (
            c.rank, c.target_perplexity, c.reference_perplexity, c.perplexity_ratio,
            c.text[:50]))

memorisation_analyser.py

Description

Measures per-training-example memorisation degree using the train-test loss gap. For each training example, computes the model''s loss on that example and on a matched non-member example with similar features. Large loss gaps (low train loss, high test-distribution loss) indicate highly memorised examples with high inversion risk. Returns a ranked list of training examples by memorisation degree.

Implementation Notes

Single quotes throughout per book convention. Requires numpy and sklearn. Memorisation score is the difference between per-sample loss on training set and mean loss on the non-member pool. No hardcoded thresholds -- high_memorisation_threshold is a configurable percentile parameter.

Code

# memorisation_analyser.py
# Chapter 9 Section 9.7 -- Training Example Memorisation Analyser
# Single quotes throughout per book convention.

import numpy as np
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Optional, Tuple


@dataclass
class MemoisationRecord:
    sample_index: int
    train_loss: float
    nonmember_mean_loss: float
    memorisation_score: float  # nonmember_mean_loss - train_loss; higher = more memorised
    risk_tier: str  # 'high', 'moderate', 'low'


def per_sample_cross_entropy(model, X: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    '''
    Compute per-sample cross-entropy loss -log(p_true_class) for each record.
    Requires model to expose predict_proba(X).
    '''
    proba = model.predict_proba(X)
    n = len(X)
    true_proba = np.array([
        proba[i, int(y[i])] for i in range(n)
    ])
    return -np.log(np.clip(true_proba, 1e-10, 1.0))


def analyse_memorisation(
    model,
    X_train: np.ndarray,
    y_train: np.ndarray,
    X_nonmember: np.ndarray,
    y_nonmember: np.ndarray,
    high_percentile: float = 75.0,
    low_percentile: float = 40.0,
) -> List[MemoisationRecord]:
    '''
    Rank training examples by memorisation degree.
    high_percentile: memorisation score above this percentile = high risk.
    low_percentile: memorisation score below this percentile = low risk.
    '''
    train_losses = per_sample_cross_entropy(model, X_train, y_train)
    nonmember_losses = per_sample_cross_entropy(model, X_nonmember, y_nonmember)
    nm_mean = float(nonmember_losses.mean())

    scores = nm_mean - train_losses  # positive = lower train loss than nonmember baseline
    high_thresh = float(np.percentile(scores, high_percentile))
    low_thresh = float(np.percentile(scores, low_percentile))

    records: List[MemoisationRecord] = []
    for i in range(len(X_train)):
        score = float(scores[i])
        if score >= high_thresh:
            tier = 'high'
        elif score >= low_thresh:
            tier = 'moderate'
        else:
            tier = 'low'
        records.append(MemoisationRecord(
            sample_index=i,
            train_loss=round(float(train_losses[i]), 6),
            nonmember_mean_loss=round(nm_mean, 6),
            memorisation_score=round(score, 6),
            risk_tier=tier,
        ))

    records.sort(key=lambda r: r.memorisation_score, reverse=True)
    return records


def render_memorisation_report(records: List[MemoisationRecord], top_n: int = 10) -> None:
    tier_counts = {'high': 0, 'moderate': 0, 'low': 0}
    for r in records:
        tier_counts[r.risk_tier] += 1

    print('MEMORISATION ANALYSIS -- Chapter 9 Section 9.7')
    print('Total training examples: %d' % len(records))
    for tier, count in tier_counts.items():
        print('  %s risk: %d (%.1f%%)' % (tier, count, 100.0 * count / max(len(records), 1)))

    print('\nTop %d highest-memorisation examples:' % top_n)
    print('%6s %12s %14s %14s %8s' % ('Index', 'Train Loss', 'NM Mean Loss', 'Mem Score', 'Tier'))
    print('-' * 60)
    for r in records[:top_n]:
        print('%6d %12.6f %14.6f %14.6f %8s' % (
            r.sample_index, r.train_loss, r.nonmember_mean_loss,
            r.memorisation_score, r.risk_tier))


if __name__ == '__main__':
    from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier
    from sklearn.datasets import make_classification

    X, y = make_classification(n_samples=1000, n_features=15, random_state=42)
    # Train on a small subset to induce overfitting and memorisation
    X_train, y_train = X[:200], y[:200]
    X_nm, y_nm = X[200:400], y[200:400]

    model = MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(64, 32), max_iter=1000,
                          alpha=0.00001, random_state=42)
    model.fit(X_train, y_train)

    train_acc = model.score(X_train, y_train)
    test_acc = model.score(X_nm, y_nm)
    print('Train accuracy: %.1f%%  Non-member accuracy: %.1f%%' % (
        train_acc * 100, test_acc * 100))
    print('Generalisation gap: %.1f%%\n' % ((train_acc - test_acc) * 100))

    records = analyse_memorisation(model, X_train, y_train, X_nm, y_nm)
    render_memorisation_report(records, top_n=8)

Common Mistakes

Id

1

Description

Concluding that model inversion only applies to image classifiers because Fredrikson et al. 2015 used face recognition

Why It Happens

The face reconstruction demonstration is the most visually compelling and widely cited result. It is easy to remember and easy to demonstrate. The framing of "reconstructing a face from a classifier" implies the attack is specific to image domains with visually interpretable outputs.

Consequence

Tabular classifiers trained on medical or financial data are equally vulnerable via feature-space inversion -- the optimisation maximises confidence in a patient class by finding the feature vector most consistent with that class, revealing clinical attributes of training patients. LLMs are vulnerable via perplexity-based extraction, which does not require any visual output at all. Operators who dismiss inversion as an image-only attack leave non-image deployments undefended.

Correct Approach

Model inversion applies to any model whose outputs encode training data features -- image classifiers, tabular classifiers, and generative LLMs all exhibit this property. Apply the inversion threat model (Deliverable 5) to the deployment''s model type and data category, not only to image-domain deployments.

Id

2

Description

Treating training data deduplication as a complete defence against LLM memorisation and inversion

Why It Happens

Deduplication removes repeated training examples and is widely recommended as a data quality practice. It is easy to implement, improves training efficiency, and is reported to reduce memorisation in published research. Teams implement deduplication and conclude that LLM inversion risk is managed.

Consequence

Deduplication reduces repetition-based memorisation for common text that appears many times in the corpus. However, it increases per-example memorisation for unique examples because those examples receive the model''s full fitting capacity without the averaging benefit of repeated exposure. The deduplication paradox: unique sensitive content (a specific individual''s medical note that appears exactly once) becomes more memorised after deduplication, not less. For precisely the highest-sensitivity content, deduplication makes the problem worse.

Correct Approach

Deduplication is a data quality control, not a privacy control. For privacy, targeted content scrubbing -- identifying and removing sensitive content (PII, credentials, private documents) before training -- addresses the memorisation risk for that content directly. DP training bounds the memorisation degree for all content regardless of frequency. Both are needed; deduplication alone is not a privacy defence.

3

Description

Assuming that open-weight model releases require no inversion defence because the weights are already public

Why It Happens

The reasoning is: if the weights are public, the adversary can already examine them directly; inversion provides no additional capability. The model is already "out" so inversion defence seems pointless.

Consequence

Open-weight release does not eliminate inversion harm -- it eliminates the query budget constraint entirely. Gradient-based inversion with white-box access runs locally at the adversary''s cost with no API interaction and no operator visibility. The training data encoded in the weights is as recoverable as with black-box access, and faster. Open-weight release means the operator cannot detect or rate-limit inversion; the only remaining defence is the upstream one: DP training applied before the weights are released.

Correct Approach

Differential privacy training must be applied before release for any open- weight model trained on sensitive personal data. Output perturbation and query monitoring are irrelevant post-release. The DP training decision must be made before the training run; it cannot be applied after the weights are public. Evaluate DP training requirements as part of the release decision process, not after.

Id

4

Description

Running the inversion attack only from a random initialisation and concluding low fidelity from one failed attempt

Why It Happens

The optimisation is non-convex and sensitive to initialisation. A random initialisation in a high-dimensional space may be far from any training example and converge to a generic class prototype rather than a specific training member. One failed attempt at a random starting point is interpreted as evidence that the model is not invertable.

Consequence

High-fidelity inversion often requires a warm start -- an initialisation close to the target training example. An adversary with any partial knowledge of the target (an approximate face image, a patient''s demographic profile, a code file''s structure) initialises near the target and converges efficiently. A random-start attempt evaluates the worst-case starting condition, not the realistic adversary''s approach.

Correct Approach

Evaluate inversion fidelity across multiple initialisations including warm starts from distributional knowledge the adversary plausibly has. For face classifiers, test with an initialisation drawn from the same demographic distribution as the target. For LLMs, test with prefix-completion attacks using known partial text. Random-start inversion measures a lower bound on attack fidelity, not the realistic attack success rate.

Id

5

Description

Applying output perturbation uniformly across all outputs without increasing perturbation for high-inversion-risk queries

Why It Happens

Output perturbation is typically deployed as a fixed-magnitude transformation applied to all model outputs. The implementation is simple and uniform: add noise of standard deviation σ to all confidence scores. This is operationally convenient.

Consequence

Inversion queries are distinguishable from legitimate queries by their input distribution: they cluster near class prototypes and explore the input space systematically. Applying the same perturbation magnitude to both inversion- likely and benign queries provides suboptimal protection: benign queries receive unnecessary noise (degrading product quality) while inversion queries may still achieve sufficient gradient signal at the fixed σ.

Correct Approach

Section 9.12 builds adaptive perturbation: queries that match inversion-style input patterns (systematic variation, boundary proximity, OOD inputs) receive higher perturbation magnitude. Benign queries near the high-density legitimate use distribution receive lower perturbation. This concentrates the utility cost of perturbation on the queries that are most likely adversarial while minimising degradation on legitimate use.

Section Summary

Compact

Model inversion extends membership inference from identification to content reconstruction: by optimising an input to maximise a classifier''s confidence on a target class, the adversary reconstructs the training data features the model has memorised. Fredrikson et al. 2015 demonstrated face reconstruction from a face recognition API using gradient ascent in pixel space, converging to a recognisable likeness of the target training subject. Gradient-based inversion with white-box access is faster and more precise; black-box zeroth- order inversion requires 2d queries per step but is viable against grey-box APIs for moderate-dimensional inputs. LLM training data extraction (Carlini et al. 2021) operates differently: generate many text completions, score by perplexity ratio between target and reference model, and the low-ratio candidates are likely memorised training content -- including verbatim PII and credentials. Memorisation is the necessary precondition: only highly memorised training examples are reconstructable. The deduplication paradox makes unique sensitive content more memorised, not less. The GitHub Copilot credential exposure is the most commercially significant production inversion incident. Differential privacy training is the upstream formal defence that bounds inversion fidelity; data scrubbing removes the highest-risk content before memorisation occurs; output perturbation degrades the gradient and perplexity signals at inference time.

Key Takeaways

  • Model inversion reconstructs training data content; membership inference (Section 9.6) identifies which records are worth targeting
  • LLM inversion via perplexity-ratio filtering produced verbatim PII, credentials, and copyrighted text from GPT-2 in production-scale experiments
  • The deduplication paradox: deduplication increases per-example memorisation for unique sensitive content -- targeted scrubbing is the right control
  • Open-weight models require DP training before release; output perturbation and query monitoring are irrelevant after weights are public
  • Warm-start inversion from distributional knowledge is far more effective than random-start -- evaluate realistic adversary initialisation
  • The three-layer defence is: DP training (formal bound) + data scrubbing (prevent memorisation) + output perturbation (degrade inference signal)

Section 9.8: API Economic Abuse -- Cost Amplification, Denial-of-Wallet, Rate-Limit Exhaustion

Preview

Sections 9.3 through 9.7 covered attacks that steal or reconstruct model intellectual property and training data. Section 9.8 covers the operational variant: attacks that degrade the deployed model''s viability without extracting any information. Cost amplification submits queries designed to maximise per-request compute cost; denial-of-wallet sustains this over time to exhaust operator budget; rate-limit exhaustion consumes allocation to deny service to legitimate users. These attacks share the inference-time attack surface with extraction and share the rate-limiting defence with the Section 9.11 capstone, which is why they belong in Chapter 9 rather than in a separate operational security chapter. The adversary economics are asymmetric: a $1 attacker query can cost the operator $10 to $100 in compute, making detection and rate-limiting the primary controls.
