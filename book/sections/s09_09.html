Section 9.9: Real-World Incident

Section Overview

One Line Summary

Three documented extraction incidents -- Tramer et al. 2016 against commercial MLaaS APIs, Carlini et al. 2021 against GPT-2, and the Alpaca/Vicuna LLM distillation pattern -- demonstrate that extraction operates at production scale against major vendors, reveal the architectural gaps that made it possible, and map the specific points where the Section 9.10 through 9.12 controls would have triggered.

Why This Section Exists

Sections 9.3 through 9.8 built the attack catalogue from first principles: why each attack works, what budget it requires, what query patterns it produces. Section 9.9 grounds that catalogue in documented reality. The question defenders ask when evaluating whether to invest in extraction controls is not "is this theoretically possible?" but "has this actually happened?" The answer, documented in peer-reviewed publications and public disclosures, is yes -- at production scale, against major commercial vendors, using the same techniques the preceding sections described. Three incident classes are reconstructed here. The Tramer et al. 2016 commercial MLaaS extractions established that the theoretical extraction bounds are achievable in practice against production APIs. The Carlini et al. 2021 GPT-2 training data extraction demonstrated that LLMs memorise and leak training data verbatim through their inference API. The Alpaca and Vicuna LLM distillation campaigns, conducted openly as academic experiments, established that a few thousand dollars in API fees can produce a functional clone of a commercial LLM capability, with the query distribution analytically indistinguishable from legitimate evaluation use. The incidents share a root cause structure: no query pattern monitoring, no output watermarking, and no extraction-aware rate limiting. Each gap corresponds directly to a control that Sections 9.10 through 9.12 build. The detection opportunity mapping in this section shows precisely where each control would have triggered and what it would have revealed. The lessons learned become the pre-production verification checklist that Section 9.13 incorporates into the chapter summary.

Position In Chapter

Section 9.9 of 13. Bridge between the attack catalogue (Sections 9.3 through 9.8) and the production defence sections (9.10 through 9.12). The root cause gaps documented here are the requirements that drive the Section 9.10 detection primitive design. The detection opportunity map is the traceability document connecting production controls to documented failures.

Title

Why Documented Incidents Precede Production Defence

Body

The production controls in Sections 9.10 through 9.12 are expensive to build and maintain. Query monitoring requires persistent per-user state infrastructure. Output watermarking adds per-response processing. Adaptive rate limiting requires careful policy calibration. Every production control competes for engineering time against features that directly generate revenue. The argument for investing in extraction controls is strengthened considerably by evidence that the attacks they defend against have already occurred at production scale. Section 9.9 provides that evidence in three forms. The Tramer et al. 2016 commercial MLaaS extractions were conducted by academic researchers against production APIs of major cloud providers -- not as malicious attacks but as disclosed research. The techniques they demonstrated -- equation-solving against linear classifiers, path-finding against decision trees, surrogate training against neural networks -- are now documented in the peer-reviewed literature and reproducible by anyone with an API key and a few hours of compute time. The Carlini et al. 2021 GPT-2 extraction was not a disclosure of a model''s decision surface but of its training data: verbatim text sequences from the GPT-2 training corpus were recovered through systematic inference queries. The extraction required no special model access, no vulnerability exploit, and a modest query budget. The Alpaca and Vicuna projects are not adversarial incidents in the conventional sense -- they were openly conducted academic experiments with published code and results. They are incidents in the threat model sense: they demonstrated that a functional clone of a commercial LLM capability is achievable for a few thousand dollars in API fees, using a query distribution indistinguishable from legitimate evaluation use. The fact that these demonstrations were academic does not reduce their threat model relevance -- any adversary can replicate the methodology. The incidents share a structure: the attack vector was the documented technique, the enabling condition was the absence of controls the preceding sections build, and the detection gap was total in every case. Grounding the production controls in these documented failures converts the Section 9.11 and 9.12 implementation from precautionary architecture into targeted remediation of demonstrated vulnerabilities.

Title

Incident 1: Tramer et al. 2016 -- Commercial MLaaS Extraction

Body

The Tramer et al. 2016 paper "Stealing Machine Learning Models via Prediction APIs" targeted four commercial MLaaS platforms: Amazon ML, Microsoft Azure ML, BigML, and Google Prediction API. The research was conducted with the knowledge and cooperation of the platform vendors and published as disclosed security research. Against Amazon ML''s logistic regression endpoint, equation-solving extraction recovered the model''s weight vector using a query count matching the theoretical O(d log(1/ε)) bound. For a model with d = 100 features and target precision ε = 0.001, the extraction required approximately 1,000 API calls. The extracted model''s predictions matched the victim''s on a held-out test set to within noise. The query budget was so small relative to Amazon ML''s rate limits that no rate-limit monitor would have fired. Against BigML''s decision tree endpoint, path-finding extraction recovered the full tree structure with exact fidelity on the target input distribution. The extraction required a few thousand API calls -- comparable to a developer running integration tests during a build pipeline. The query pattern was unusual (one-feature-at-a-time systematic variation) but no monitoring system was in place to detect it. Against Microsoft Azure ML''s neural network endpoints, surrogate training on API outputs achieved substantial prediction fidelity. The authors noted that soft-label outputs (probability distributions rather than hard class labels) reduced the required query budget by an order of magnitude compared to hard-label-only access. The root cause across all four platforms was identical: no query pattern monitoring, throughput-only rate limits that the attacks never approached, and in some cases soft-label API designs that provided extraction subsidies to adversaries. The platforms'' response included reducing soft-label output precision in some cases, but no systematic query pattern monitoring was deployed. The controls that would have caught these attacks -- query diversity monitoring, boundary query detection, extraction-aware rate limits -- were not part of any of the platforms'' production security architecture at the time.

Title

Incident 2: Carlini et al. 2021 -- GPT-2 Verbatim Training Data Extraction

Body

The Carlini et al. 2021 paper "Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models" demonstrated verbatim recovery of training data sequences from GPT-2 using black-box inference queries. The attack does not replicate the model''s capabilities -- it reconstructs specific text sequences that the model memorised during training. The attack mechanism combined membership inference with targeted generation. The authors used the GPT-2 API (and local model weights for the white-box variant) to generate text sequences, then identified which generated sequences were memorised training data by measuring their perplexity: sequences assigned lower perplexity by GPT-2 than by a reference language model were candidates for verbatim training data. The paper reported recovering thousands of verbatim training data sequences, including personally identifiable information such as names, email addresses, phone numbers, and unique identifiers from documents in the GPT-2 training corpus. The query budget was modest by commercial standards. The attack required generating approximately 600,000 tokens of text, which at GPT-2''s then- current API pricing represented a small number of dollars. The output monitoring that would have caught this -- detecting repetitive generation of unusual proper nouns, email addresses, and structured identifiers -- was not in place. The incident revealed three architectural gaps. First, GPT-2 was trained without differential privacy, meaning individual training examples could influence the model''s parameters sufficiently to be verbatim extractable. Second, no output monitoring was in place to detect repeated generation of training-corpus-derived content. Third, no per-user rate limit on generated token volume existed that would have flagged the systematic generation campaign as anomalous. OpenAI''s subsequent models incorporated training procedures intended to reduce memorisation, and the published research contributed to the broader adoption of differential privacy training and output monitoring in production LLM deployments. The Carlini et al. work remains the canonical demonstration that LLM inference APIs can leak training data verbatim.

Title

Incident 3: Alpaca and Vicuna -- Commercial LLM Distillation at Scale

Body

The Alpaca and Vicuna projects, conducted openly as academic research, demonstrated that the LLM distillation pipeline described in Section 9.4 is operationally feasible at a cost that makes it economically rational for any organisation seeking to replicate a commercial LLM capability without the original training cost. The Alpaca project (Stanford, 2023) fine-tuned Meta''s LLaMA 7B model on 52,000 instruction-following examples generated by querying GPT-3.5-turbo. The query cost was approximately $500 in API fees. The resulting model exhibited instruction-following behaviour qualitatively similar to GPT-3.5 on common tasks. The prompts were drawn from a seed set of 175 human-authored instructions, expanded through the "self-instruct" technique that generates diverse variations of the seed prompts by querying the victim API itself. The Vicuna project followed a similar methodology, fine-tuning LLaMA 13B on approximately 70,000 conversations collected from ChatGPT user-shared logs and additional GPT-4 queries. The Vicuna authors reported that Vicuna achieved 92 percent of GPT-4''s capability on several benchmark tasks at a total distillation cost under $300. Both projects are published as open-source code with detailed methodology documentation. Any adversary seeking to replicate a commercial LLM capability using the same approach can follow the published procedure with minimal adaptation. The detection gap in both cases was total: the query distributions -- diverse instruction-following prompts drawn from user-shared logs and benchmark tasks -- were analytically indistinguishable from legitimate evaluation use. No input-side monitoring would have separated a distillation campaign from a research team benchmarking the model''s capabilities. The only control that would have provided post-hoc evidence is output watermarking: if GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4 had embedded watermarks in their outputs, those watermarks would have transferred into the Alpaca and Vicuna student models and been detectable in their outputs. Neither model had deployed output watermarking at the time of these experiments.

Title

Root Cause Analysis: What the Three Incidents Share

Body

The three incident classes differ in technique, target, and consequence, but their root cause structure is remarkably consistent. Each incident exploited a deployment that lacked the same set of controls. The root cause analysis converts this consistency into a requirements document for the Section 9.10 through 9.12 defence architecture. Root cause 1: No query pattern monitoring. None of the affected deployments maintained per-user query distribution statistics across sessions. The Tramer et al. attacks produced distinctive query patterns -- one-feature- at-a-time variation for tree extraction, boundary-seeking input pairs for linear extraction -- that would have been detectable with the query diversity monitor and boundary query detector from Section 9.10. The Carlini et al. campaign produced repeated generation of rare token sequences. The Alpaca and Vicuna campaigns produced query distributions with distinctive benchmark topic concentration. None of these signals was monitored. Root cause 2: Throughput-only rate limits. Every affected deployment had rate limits, but those limits were calibrated to infrastructure capacity rather than extraction economics. The Tramer et al. attacks completed within normal usage volumes. The Carlini et al. generation campaign stayed within per-user token budgets that were set for product continuity, not for training data leakage prevention. The Alpaca and Vicuna campaigns issued queries at rates consistent with legitimate developer evaluation. Root cause 3: No output watermarking. The output of every affected deployment was unwatermarked. Surrogate models trained on these outputs inherit none of the victim''s cryptographic identity. Post-hoc ownership verification is impossible: there is no mechanism for the operator to demonstrate that a deployed surrogate was trained on their outputs rather than independently. Root cause 4: Soft-label outputs without perturbation. Several Tramer et al. targets returned probability distributions or confidence scores, providing adversaries with a query budget discount of 5 to 50x. No output perturbation was applied to reduce the information value of these distributions. Root cause 5: No differential privacy training. The Carlini et al. extraction was enabled by GPT-2''s memorisation of training data sequences. DP training with a reasonably tight budget would have reduced the per-sequence memorisation sufficient to prevent verbatim recovery. DP training was not part of the GPT-2 training pipeline.

Title

Detection Opportunity Mapping: Where Controls Would Have Triggered

Body

The five root causes map directly to five control categories. For each incident, the detection opportunity map shows the earliest point at which a deployed control would have fired and what it would have revealed to the defender. For the Tramer et al. MLaaS extractions, the earliest trigger would have been the boundary query detector (Section 9.10) firing on the equation- solving input pairs: opposite-label inputs at small distances from each other, issued in a systematic sequence. The query diversity monitor would have fired on the path-finding extraction as the one-feature-at-a-time variation pattern accumulated across sessions. Both signals would have triggered within the first fifty to one hundred queries -- before more than a fraction of the extraction was complete. For the Carlini et al. GPT-2 extraction, the earliest trigger would have been the output entropy monitor (Section 9.10) detecting the repeated generation of low-perplexity sequences containing unusual proper nouns and structured identifiers. A rate limit on generated token volume per user per day would have slowed the extraction campaign. Neither control was present. For the Alpaca and Vicuna distillation campaigns, no input-side control would have triggered: the query distributions were legitimate by design. The only control that would have provided evidence is output watermarking (Section 9.12): had a watermark been embedded in the victim''s outputs, it would have survived into the student models and been detectable in Alpaca''s and Vicuna''s responses. The watermark verification workflow from Section 9.12 would have produced ownership evidence enabling legal action against redistribution. The detection opportunity map is asymmetric across incident classes: input-side controls (query monitoring, rate limiting) cover the Tramer et al. and Carlini et al. incident patterns; output-side controls (watermarking) cover the Alpaca and Vicuna pattern. Neither surface alone provides complete coverage. This is the architectural conclusion that Section 9.1 previewed and that Sections 9.11 and 9.12 implement: complete extraction defence requires both input-side and output-side controls deployed together.

Title

Operator Response Patterns: Disclosure, Remediation, and Adoption

Body

The three incident classes produced different operator responses, which reflect both the nature of the disclosure and the maturity of the operators'' security practices at the time. The Tramer et al. 2016 disclosures prompted the affected MLaaS platforms to review their output designs: Amazon ML and other providers reduced the precision of returned confidence scores and in some cases switched to hard-label-only outputs for certain model types. Rate limit adjustments were made at some platforms, though the adjustments were driven by throughput capacity concerns rather than extraction economics. No platform deployed systematic query pattern monitoring as an extraction-specific control in the immediate aftermath. The Carlini et al. 2021 disclosure prompted broader discussion of training data memorisation and its privacy implications. The paper contributed to increased adoption of differential privacy training in the LLM research community and to greater attention to output monitoring for memorised content. OpenAI and others began to incorporate memorisation reduction techniques into their training pipelines, though the specific techniques and their effectiveness remain proprietary. The Alpaca and Vicuna disclosures produced a mixed response. Meta revised the terms of service for LLaMA model releases to prohibit commercial use and training on outputs of commercial models. OpenAI''s terms of service already prohibited using their API outputs to train competing models -- a prohibition that the Alpaca and Vicuna projects technically violated, though the academic context complicated enforcement. The incidents accelerated industry interest in output watermarking as a mechanism for post-hoc enforcement of terms-of-service prohibitions on model distillation. The pattern across all three incident classes is instructive: operator response focused on policy and output design changes rather than on detection infrastructure. The query monitoring and watermarking controls that Section 9.11 and 9.12 build were not deployed as a direct response to these incidents by any of the affected operators, even though the technical case for them is clear from the incident analysis.

Part

8

Title

Lessons for Production LLM Deployments

Body

Eight lessons emerge from the three incident classes that translate directly into the pre-production verification checklist and the Section 9.13 chapter summary. Lesson 1: Absence of detected incidents is not evidence that incidents are absent. None of the three incident classes was detected in real time by the operator. All three were either disclosed by the researchers or publicly documented by the experimenters. An operator without extraction monitoring cannot know whether their API has been used for a distillation campaign that has never been published. Lesson 2: Soft-label outputs are the single most impactful architectural decision for extraction resistance. Removing confidence scores and probability distributions from API responses reduces the adversary''s effective query budget by 5 to 50x. This change costs nothing in implementation and has minimal impact on legitimate use cases for the vast majority of deployments. Lesson 3: Rate limits must be calibrated against extraction economics, not infrastructure capacity. The Tramer et al. attacks never approached any throughput rate limit. Extraction-aware rate limits -- calibrated against the query budget required for the target extraction technique -- would have imposed meaningful cost on the adversary. Lesson 4: Output watermarking must be deployed before any extraction campaign to be effective. The Alpaca and Vicuna campaigns collected their training data before any watermarking was considered. A watermark deployed after the fact provides no evidence about pre-deployment collections. Lesson 5: Differential privacy training is the only control that formally bounds training data extraction risk. The Carlini et al. extraction was enabled by GPT-2''s memorisation. DP training is the upstream intervention that reduces memorisation below the verbatim extraction threshold. Lesson 6: Input-side and output-side controls are not substitutes -- they cover different incident classes. The Tramer et al. and Carlini et al. patterns are catchable with input-side monitoring. The Alpaca and Vicuna pattern requires output-side watermarking. Both surfaces are required. Lesson 7: Detection controls must be designed against documented incident patterns, not against theoretical attacks. The boundary query detector catches the Tramer et al. pattern; the output entropy monitor catches the Carlini et al. pattern; watermarking catches the Alpaca pattern. Each control was designed for a documented failure mode, not a hypothetical one. Lesson 8: Operator response to extraction incidents consistently focuses on policy changes rather than detection infrastructure. Policy changes address the specific technique that was disclosed; detection infrastructure catches novel technique variants. The lessons from prior incidents are not complete protection against future incidents -- only monitoring catches what policy changes miss.

incident_timeline.py

Description

Encodes the three documented extraction incident classes as structured timeline data. Provides a renderer that produces a formatted incident timeline report suitable for inclusion in a security briefing or post- incident review. The data structure follows the standard incident format: summary, timeline phases, attack vector, fidelity achieved, and root cause categories.

Implementation Notes

Single quotes throughout per book convention. No external dependencies. Incident data is schema-free: IncidentRecord and TimelinePhase dataclasses drive all rendering. No hardcoded per-incident formatting.

root_cause_analyser.py

Description

Generates root cause analysis documents for extraction incidents in standard RCA format (summary, attack vector, root causes, impact, detection gaps, remediation). Provides a gap_score() function that quantifies the fraction of root causes addressed by a given set of deployed controls, enabling defenders to measure their remediation coverage against documented incidents.

Implementation Notes

Single quotes throughout per book convention. No external dependencies. RCA format is data-driven via RCADocument dataclass. Root cause to control mapping is schema-free. Gap scoring is computed from the intersection of incident root causes and deployed controls.

Code

# root_cause_analyser.py
# Chapter 9 Section 9.9 -- Extraction Incident Root Cause Analyser
# Single quotes throughout per book convention.

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Dict, List, Set, Tuple

from incident_timeline import DOCUMENTED_INCIDENTS, IncidentRecord


ROOT_CAUSE_TO_CONTROL: Dict[str, List[str]] = {
    'no_query_pattern_monitoring': ['query_monitor', 'boundary_query_detector', 'query_diversity_monitor'],
    'throughput_only_rate_limits': ['rate_limiter_extraction_aware'],
    'soft_label_outputs_without_perturbation': ['output_perturbator', 'hard_label_api_design'],
    'no_output_watermarking': ['output_watermarker', 'watermark_detector'],
    'no_differential_privacy_training': ['dp_training_pipeline'],
    'no_output_monitoring_for_memorised_content': ['output_entropy_monitor', 'memorisation_detector'],
    'no_cross_user_collusion_detection': ['cross_user_correlator'],
    'query_distribution_indistinguishable_from_legitimate': ['output_watermarker'],
}


@dataclass
class RCADocument:
    incident_id: str
    incident_name: str
    summary: str
    attack_vector: str
    root_causes: List[str]
    impact: str
    detection_gaps: List[str]
    remediation_controls: List[str]
    residual_risk: str


def generate_rca(incident: IncidentRecord) -> RCADocument:
    all_controls: List[str] = []
    for cause in incident.root_causes:
        all_controls.extend(ROOT_CAUSE_TO_CONTROL.get(cause, []))
    unique_controls = sorted(set(all_controls))
    detection_gaps = [
        f'Root cause [{c}] was not covered by any deployed detection control'
        for c in incident.root_causes
    ]
    return RCADocument(
        incident_id=incident.incident_id,
        incident_name=incident.name,
        summary=(
            f'{incident.technique} applied against {incident.target}. '
            f'Query budget: {incident.total_query_budget}. '
            f'Fidelity: {incident.fidelity_achieved}.'
        ),
        attack_vector=incident.technique,
        root_causes=incident.root_causes,
        impact=f'Extraction fidelity: {incident.fidelity_achieved}. Disclosure type: {incident.disclosure_type}.',
        detection_gaps=detection_gaps,
        remediation_controls=unique_controls,
        residual_risk=(
            'Residual risk remains for distributed campaigns below per-user thresholds '
            'and for novel technique variants not covered by existing detection signatures.'
        ),
    )


def gap_score(
    incident: IncidentRecord,
    deployed_controls: List[str],
) -> Tuple[float, List[str], List[str]]:
    '''
    Compute the fraction of incident root causes addressed by deployed controls.
    Returns (score, covered_causes, uncovered_causes).
    score = covered_cause_count / total_cause_count.
    '''
    deployed_set: Set[str] = set(deployed_controls)
    covered: List[str] = []
    uncovered: List[str] = []
    for cause in incident.root_causes:
        controls_for_cause = ROOT_CAUSE_TO_CONTROL.get(cause, [])
        if any(c in deployed_set for c in controls_for_cause):
            covered.append(cause)
        else:
            uncovered.append(cause)
    score = round(len(covered) / max(len(incident.root_causes), 1), 3)
    return score, covered, uncovered


def render_rca(doc: RCADocument) -> None:
    print(f'ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS: {doc.incident_name}')
    print(f'ID: {doc.incident_id}')
    print(f'\nSummary:')
    print(f'  {doc.summary}')
    print(f'\nAttack Vector:')
    print(f'  {doc.attack_vector}')
    print(f'\nRoot Causes:')
    for c in doc.root_causes:
        print(f'  - {c}')
    print(f'\nImpact:')
    print(f'  {doc.impact}')
    print(f'\nDetection Gaps:')
    for g in doc.detection_gaps:
        print(f'  - {g}')
    print(f'\nRemediation Controls:')
    for ctrl in doc.remediation_controls:
        print(f'  - {ctrl}')
    print(f'\nResidual Risk:')
    print(f'  {doc.residual_risk}')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    example_deployed = [
        'query_monitor', 'boundary_query_detector',
        'rate_limiter_extraction_aware', 'output_watermarker',
    ]
    print('ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS REPORT -- Chapter 9 Section 9.9')
    print('=' * 70)
    for incident in DOCUMENTED_INCIDENTS:
        doc = generate_rca(incident)
        render_rca(doc)
        score, covered, uncovered = gap_score(incident, example_deployed)
        print(f'\nGap Score with example deployed controls: {score:.1%}')
        print(f'  Covered:   {covered}')
        print(f'  Uncovered: {uncovered}')
        print()

def assess_deployment(deployed_controls: List[str]) -> Tuple[float, List[ChecklistItem], List[ChecklistItem]]:
    '''
    Assess a deployment against the lessons learned checklist.
    Returns (coverage_score, passed_items, failed_items).
    coverage_score = fraction of checklist items with all required controls deployed.
    '''
    deployed_set: Set[str] = set(deployed_controls)
    passed: List[ChecklistItem] = []
    failed: List[ChecklistItem] = []
    for item in CHECKLIST:
        if all(c in deployed_set for c in item.required_controls):
            passed.append(item)
        else:
            failed.append(item)
    score = round(len(passed) / max(len(CHECKLIST), 1), 3)
    return score, passed, failed


def render_checklist(deployed_controls: List[str]) -> None:
    score, passed, failed = assess_deployment(deployed_controls)
    print('PRE-PRODUCTION EXTRACTION DEFENCE CHECKLIST -- Chapter 9 Section 9.9')
    print('=' * 70)
    print(f'Controls deployed: {deployed_controls}')
    print(f'Checklist coverage: {score:.1%} ({len(passed)}/{len(CHECKLIST)} items passed)\n')
    priority_order = ['critical', 'high', 'medium']
    for priority in priority_order:
        items = [item for item in CHECKLIST if item.priority == priority]
        print(f'--- {priority.upper()} PRIORITY ---')
        for item in items:
            status = 'PASS' if item in passed else 'FAIL'
            print(f'  [{status}] {item.item_id}: {item.verification_question}')
            if item not in passed:
                missing = [c for c in item.required_controls if c not in set(deployed_controls)]
                print(f'         Missing controls: {missing}')
                print(f'         Evidence: {item.incident_evidence}')

Common Mistakes

Id

1

Description

Treating the Tramer et al. 2016 results as historical artefacts that no longer apply to current APIs

Why It Happens

The Tramer et al. 2016 paper targeted MLaaS platforms from the mid-2010s. Defenders assume the affected platforms have since mitigated the specific vulnerabilities and that current deployments are fundamentally different. The paper''s age is mistaken for obsolescence.

Consequence

The underlying mechanism -- that linear classifiers extract in O(d) queries and that every API query reveals boundary information -- is architecture- independent and time-independent. Current deployments of linear or tree-based classifiers via prediction APIs are as vulnerable to equation-solving and path-finding extraction as the 2016 targets were. The specific API endpoints have evolved; the attack surface has not.

Correct Approach

Apply the Tramer et al. threat model to any current deployment of a classifier via a prediction API. Audit the API output format, the model architecture, and the rate limit policy against the extraction budget estimates documented in Section 9.3. The pre-production checklist (Deliverable 5) includes explicit verification steps that apply regardless of the deployment year.

Id

2

Description

Concluding that terms-of-service prohibitions on model distillation make output watermarking unnecessary

Why It Happens

The Alpaca and Vicuna projects violated OpenAI''s terms of service by using API outputs to train competing models. Legal terms exist; the argument goes that legal recourse is available without technical controls. Output watermarking seems redundant when the behaviour is already prohibited.

Consequence

Terms-of-service enforcement requires evidence that the violation occurred. Without a watermark, there is no mechanism to demonstrate that a suspect surrogate model was trained on the operator''s outputs rather than independently trained. Legal recourse requires technical evidence; terms of service create the right to that evidence but not the evidence itself. The Alpaca and Vicuna releases were never legally challenged because the operators lacked the forensic mechanism to attribute the surrogates to their API outputs.

Correct Approach

Watermarking is the technical foundation that makes terms-of-service enforcement feasible. Terms of service create the legal right; watermarking creates the technical evidence. Both are required for actionable enforcement. Deploy output watermarking before any distillation campaign occurs -- not as a response to a discovered violation.

Id

3

Description

Using the Alpaca and Vicuna query budgets (50K-70K) as the extraction detection threshold

Why It Happens

The documented incidents provide concrete numbers. 52,000 queries for Alpaca, 70,000 for Vicuna. Setting a detection threshold at, say, 30,000 queries per user over 30 days seems to catch these campaigns before completion. The incident documentation is mistaken for a threshold specification.

Consequence

A patient adversary who knows the threshold spreads their campaign over more accounts or longer time windows to stay below 30,000 queries per account. The documented campaigns were not optimised for detection evasion -- they were openly conducted academic experiments. A covert campaign would be designed to evade any threshold derived from published work. Thresholds derived from documented incidents are known to adversaries.

Correct Approach

Design detection thresholds from extraction economics, not from documented incident query counts. The query budget required for a target fidelity level is the threshold target, not the query count from a specific published experiment. The budget-fidelity models in Sections 9.2 and 9.4 provide the economically grounded threshold inputs.

Id

4

Description

Treating the output watermark as a prevention control rather than a post-hoc evidence control

Why It Happens

Watermarking "catches" extraction in the sense that it enables detection of the surrogate after deployment. Defenders sometimes interpret "catches the attack" as "prevents the attack" and reduce investment in input-side prevention controls (query monitoring, rate limiting) on the assumption that watermarking makes prevention unnecessary.

Consequence

The extraction campaign completes, the surrogate is deployed, and the watermark enables post-hoc identification. But the model has been stolen. The operator has evidence but not the model back. Prevention is the goal; watermarking provides evidence of theft after the fact, not prevention of it. Reducing input-side controls because watermarking exists increases the operator''s exposure to completed extraction campaigns.

Correct Approach

Watermarking and query monitoring are complementary, not substitutable. Query monitoring prevents campaigns that produce detectable input patterns. Watermarking provides evidence for campaigns that evade input-side detection. The Alpaca and Vicuna campaigns would have evaded query monitoring; watermarking would have caught the surrogates. The Tramer et al. campaign would have been caught by query monitoring before completion. Both controls are required.

Id

5

Description

Reading the incident RCA as a complete list of all controls needed and stopping at the documented gaps

Why It Happens

The root cause analysis identifies five gaps: no query monitoring, throughput- only rate limits, no watermarking, soft-label outputs, no DP training. These are the five documented gaps in three documented incidents. A defender who closes all five concludes that their deployment is fully protected.

Consequence

The five root causes are the documented gaps in documented incidents -- not a complete enumeration of all possible extraction attack vectors. Novel technique variants, improved active learning strategies, and future attack classes that combine extraction with other threat vectors may exploit gaps not present in the 2016 or 2021 or 2023 incidents. A defence optimised only against documented incidents is brittle against future variation.

Correct Approach

The incident RCA closes known gaps. Red team exercises test the defence against novel variants beyond documented techniques. The pre-production checklist (Deliverable 5, item L7) explicitly requires a quarterly red team extraction exercise precisely because absence of detected incidents is not evidence that attacks are absent or that future attacks will match documented patterns.

Section Summary

Compact

Three documented incident classes demonstrate that model extraction operates at production scale against major commercial vendors. The Tramer et al. 2016 MLaaS extractions achieved exact fidelity against linear and tree classifiers and substantial fidelity against neural networks in query counts that no throughput-based rate limit would have flagged. The Carlini et al. 2021 GPT-2 extraction recovered thousands of verbatim training sequences including PII through perplexity-based membership inference, with no output monitoring in place. The Alpaca and Vicuna distillation campaigns produced functional clones of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 capabilities for $300 to $500 in API fees, with query distributions analytically indistinguishable from legitimate evaluation use. All three incidents share five root causes: no query pattern monitoring, throughput-only rate limits, no output watermarking, soft-label outputs without perturbation, and no differential privacy training. Input- side controls (query monitor, rate limiter) cover the Tramer et al. and Carlini et al. incident patterns. Output-side watermarking covers the Alpaca and Vicuna pattern. No single control surface covers all three. The eight lessons from the incidents drive the pre-production verification checklist and the Section 9.13 chapter summary.

Key Takeaways

  • Extraction is documented, reproducible, and achievable for hundreds of dollars -- not a theoretical threat
  • None of the three incident classes was detected in real time; absence of detection is not absence of attack
  • Input-side monitoring catches Tramer et al. and Carlini et al. patterns; watermarking catches Alpaca/Vicuna; both are required
  • The Alpaca/Vicuna query distribution is indistinguishable from legitimate evaluation -- only output watermarking closes this gap
  • Terms-of-service prohibitions require technical watermark evidence to be actionable; legal rights without forensic evidence are unenforceable
  • Detection thresholds derived from documented incident query counts are known to adversaries; calibrate from extraction economics instead
