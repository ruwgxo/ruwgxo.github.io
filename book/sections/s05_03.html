Title

What OWASP Is and Why It Matters for AI Security

Content

The Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) is a non-profit foundation whose mission is to improve software security through open-source projects, community-driven research, and freely available educational resources. Security professionals know OWASP primarily through its flagship output: the OWASP Top 10 Web Application Security Risks, first published in 2003 and updated periodically. That document became the de facto standard for web application risk communication — the shorthand that security teams, developers, and compliance officers worldwide use to discuss common threats. When LLMs began entering enterprise deployments at scale in 2023, the security community needed an equivalent reference. The OWASP LLM Top 10 project was initiated in response: a community-driven effort to catalogue, rank, and standardise the most significant security risks specific to LLM applications. why_owasp_matters_for_ai_security: lingua_franca_effect: | Security frameworks succeed when they create shared vocabulary. When a security engineer says "we have an LLM01 finding," a CISO, a compliance officer, a developer, and a procurement manager should all understand what that means without requiring a technical briefing. OWASP creates this shared vocabulary. Within two years of publication, LLM01 through LLM10 had become the standard reference in security assessments, vendor RFPs, compliance questionnaires, and regulatory guidance documents. risk_register_anchor: | Enterprise risk management runs through risk registers: structured catalogues of identified risks with likelihood, impact, owner, and control documentation. Adding "OWASP LLM Top 10" as a risk category is immediately legible to GRC teams. Security engineers who frame their findings in OWASP terms have an easier path to risk register entries, remediation budget, and executive awareness than those who use technically precise but organisationally unfamiliar terminology. compliance_integration: | Regulatory frameworks reference OWASP. The EU AI Act's technical annex on cybersecurity requirements for high-risk AI systems aligns with OWASP LLM Top 10 categories. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework playbook cites OWASP LLM Top 10 as a reference for MEASURE function activities. SOC 2 assessors increasingly ask about OWASP LLM Top 10 coverage for clients deploying LLM-powered applications. Fluency with OWASP is fluency with the compliance landscape. vendor_assessment: | When your organisation evaluates a vendor's LLM product, the procurement questionnaire will include OWASP LLM Top 10 coverage questions. When you assess a third-party LLM integration, OWASP provides the assessment structure. When you write a bug bounty programme for your LLM deployment, OWASP categories define the scope. OWASP is the assessment framework. what_owasp_is_not: | OWASP LLM Top 10 is a risk classification and communication framework, not a technical specification or a control catalogue. It tells you what the ten most significant risk categories are and why they matter. It does not tell you exactly how to detect LLM01 in your specific deployment, or which detection rule to write, or how to tune your classifier. That is the role of MITRE ATLAS (attack techniques), NIST AI RMF (risk management process), and this book's Part 3 (detection engineering). Think of OWASP LLM Top 10 as the map legend — it defines the symbols. The detailed terrain is filled in by the other frameworks and by your own detection engineering work.

Ranking Methodology: How OWASP Scores LLM Risks

Content

Understanding how OWASP ranks risks is essential for two reasons: it lets you interpret the list correctly (high rank ≠ highest theoretical severity), and it lets you challenge or adapt the rankings intelligently for your specific deployment context. three_ranking_dimensions: prevalence: definition: "How commonly is this risk observed in real-world LLM deployments?" data_sources: | - Bug bounty reports against LLM-powered products - Security researcher disclosures and CVE-adjacent findings - Red team engagement findings shared by working group members - Publicly documented incidents (Bing Chat, GPT-4, Claude, Bard) caveat: | Prevalence data for LLM risks is sparse compared to web application risks. Many LLM security incidents are not publicly disclosed (vendor NDA, reputational concern, absence of CVE equivalent for AI flaws). The prevalence ranking reflects observed-and-reported incidents, which likely undercount actual frequency. exploitability: definition: "How easily can an adversary exploit this risk with available tools?" scoring_factors: | - Access required (API access only = high exploitability; training infrastructure access = low exploitability) - Technical skill required - Availability of public tooling and documented techniques - Time to first exploit (days vs months) example: | LLM01 (Prompt Injection) ranks high on exploitability: any user with API access can attempt prompt injection with zero specialised tooling. LLM03 (Training Data Poisoning) ranks low on exploitability: the adversary needs access to training infrastructure or a poisoning pathway into the data pipeline. impact: definition: "What is the potential damage if this risk is successfully exploited?" scoring_factors: | - Confidentiality: sensitive data exposure - Integrity: model behaviour corruption, misinformation generation - Availability: service disruption - Financial: cost attack, IP theft - Legal/regulatory: compliance violation, liability - Reputational: brand damage from harmful model outputs example: | LLM10 (Model Theft) scores high on financial and IP impact — a proprietary model represents millions in training compute — but lower on immediate confidentiality and availability impact. LLM06 (Sensitive Information Disclosure) scores high on regulatory and confidentiality impact, potentially triggering GDPR breach notification requirements. how_rankings_should_be_interpreted: rank_reflects_the_average_deployment_not_yours: | OWASP rankings reflect the average enterprise LLM deployment. Your deployment may have a fundamentally different risk profile. An LLM deployed in an air-gapped environment with no external API access has a very different LLM04 (Model DoS) risk than a public-facing ChatGPT competitor. An LLM with no fine-tuning on proprietary data has a very different LLM06 (Sensitive Information Disclosure) profile than one fine-tuned on customer records. The correct use of OWASP is as a starting checklist — not as a pre-prioritised risk register. You must apply your own likelihood assessment based on your deployment context. high_rank_does_not_mean_highest_impact_for_you: | LLM01 (Prompt Injection) ranks first because it is nearly universally applicable, highly exploitable, and easy to demonstrate. But for a healthcare organisation that fine-tuned an LLM on patient data, LLM06 (Sensitive Information Disclosure) may represent a higher business impact even though it ranks sixth — because the regulatory consequences of a HIPAA violation from training data extraction are existential, while a successful prompt injection may produce embarrassing but non-critical output. use_as_a_communication_shorthand_not_a_technical_specification: | When you tell a CISO "we have an LLM01 exposure," you have communicated the risk category in a term they can look up, connect to regulatory frameworks, and include in risk register reporting. You have not communicated the detection approach, the technical severity, or the remediation cost. OWASP starts the conversation; your technical analysis finishes it.

Title

Mapping OWASP LLM Top 10 to the Traditional Web Top 10

Content

Security engineers familiar with the OWASP Web Application Top 10 will recognise structural similarities in the LLM Top 10. Some LLM risks are direct analogues of web risks applied to a new attack surface. Others are genuinely novel — they have no web equivalent because they exploit properties unique to probabilistic, data-trained systems. Understanding this mapping helps in two ways: it lets you leverage existing mental models where they apply, and it flags where you must genuinely start from scratch. direct_analogues: llm01_prompt_injection_vs_a03_injection: web_risk: "A03:2021 — Injection (SQL, OS, LDAP injection)" llm_risk: "LLM01 — Prompt Injection" what_is_the_same: | The fundamental mechanism is identical: untrusted data is interpreted as instruction. In SQL injection, user input is concatenated into a SQL query and executed as SQL. In prompt injection, user input is concatenated into a prompt and executed as model instruction. In both cases, the system cannot distinguish data from instruction because they are represented in the same medium. what_is_different: | SQL injection has a reliable mitigation: parameterised queries that physically separate data from instruction at the parser level. Prompt injection has no equivalent mitigation — language models inherently process data and instructions as the same token stream. There is no 'parameterised prompt' that makes the model structurally unable to treat user input as instruction. This makes LLM01 fundamentally harder to prevent than A03. llm02_insecure_output_vs_a03_and_a07: web_risk: "A03:2021 — Injection (XSS) / A07:2021 — Identification Failures" llm_risk: "LLM02 — Insecure Output Handling" what_is_the_same: | LLM output rendered in a browser without sanitisation is exactly XSS — the injection vector is the LLM rather than a user-controlled form field, but the exploit mechanism and impact are identical. what_is_different: | The LLM introduces a layer of indirection. The human-readable LLM output may look safe to a reviewer while containing injected payloads designed to be processed by the rendering engine or downstream system. The output is both a finished product (for the user) and a potential attack payload (for the downstream system) simultaneously. llm04_model_dos_vs_a05: web_risk: "A05:2021 — Security Misconfiguration (including resource exhaustion)" llm_risk: "LLM04 — Model Denial of Service" what_is_the_same: | Resource exhaustion through crafted inputs is a classic pattern. HTTP request flooding, slowloris attacks, and XML billion laughs all exploit the same principle: consume disproportionate server resources per unit of attacker effort. what_is_different: | Token-based billing means LLM DoS has a financial dimension with no web equivalent. A successful DoS against a web server consumes the victim's compute. A successful cost attack against an LLM API consumes the victim's money — irreversibly, since consumed tokens cannot be refunded. Sponge examples also have no web equivalent: there is no web analogue for inputs crafted to maximise neural network activation without triggering obvious abuse patterns. llm05_supply_chain_vs_a06: web_risk: "A06:2021 — Vulnerable and Outdated Components" llm_risk: "LLM05 — Supply Chain Vulnerabilities" what_is_the_same: | Both risks concern the security of dependencies — components your application relies on that you did not produce yourself. The supply chain attack pattern (compromise the dependency, propagate to all users of that dependency) is structurally identical. what_is_different: | A vulnerable npm package can be patched with a version bump. A backdoored model weight file cannot be patched — the only remediation is to discard the weights and retrain or download from a trusted source. The attack surface is also different: pip install and npm install have ecosystem-wide scanning and known vulnerability databases. Model hubs like HuggingFace have no equivalent scanning infrastructure. llm06_sensitive_disclosure_vs_a02: web_risk: "A02:2021 — Cryptographic Failures (sensitive data exposure)" llm_risk: "LLM06 — Sensitive Information Disclosure" what_is_the_same: | Both risks concern sensitive data being accessible to parties who should not have it. The regulatory and compliance implications are similar: GDPR breach notification, HIPAA incident response, reputational damage. what_is_different: | In traditional systems, sensitive data exposure is caused by inadequate encryption or access control — discrete, fixable failures. In LLMs, sensitive data exposure is caused by model memorisation — the model learned to associate certain inputs with sensitive output patterns during training. This is not a configuration error; it is a property of how the model was trained on data it should not have been trained on. The fix is not to encrypt the model's weights — it is to retrain without the sensitive data, implement differential privacy, or add output filters. genuinely_novel_llm_risks: llm07_insecure_plugin_design: no_web_equivalent: | The closest web analogue is CSRF or confused deputy attacks — but LLM plugin exploitation is qualitatively different. In CSRF, the adversary tricks a browser into making a request with the victim's session. In LLM plugin exploitation, the adversary tricks the LLM into calling a plugin with adversary-specified parameters using application-level permissions, as a consequence of processing adversary-controlled text. The attack chain is entirely mediated by the LLM's language understanding. llm08_excessive_agency: no_web_equivalent: | Traditional web applications execute deterministic code — they do exactly what the code says. LLM agents make autonomous decisions about which actions to take based on probabilistic language processing. The concept of 'excessive agency' has no web equivalent because web applications do not autonomously decide which database to query, which email to send, or which file to delete based on their interpretation of the user's intent. The autonomy is the vulnerability. llm09_overreliance: no_web_equivalent: | Traditional software does not confabulate. A SQL query returns the data that is in the database, or it returns an error. An LLM produces fluent, confident-sounding text regardless of whether that text is accurate. The security risk of overreliance — systems and humans making decisions based on authoritative-seeming but incorrect LLM output — is unique to probabilistic generative systems. llm10_model_theft: partial_web_equivalent: | Source code theft has a web equivalent (exposed .git directories, code repository compromise). But model extraction via API query is genuinely novel: by observing outputs, an adversary can reconstruct a functional approximation of a model that has never been accessed directly. There is no web equivalent of inferring a server's source code by making HTTP requests and observing responses.

OWASP as a Risk Communication Tool: Beyond the Checklist

Content

The most common misuse of OWASP LLM Top 10 is treating it as a binary checklist: "Does our system have LLM01? Yes/No." This misses the framework's primary value, which is as a risk communication and prioritisation structure. three_correct_uses: ciso_briefing: scenario: | Your CISO has fifteen minutes to understand the LLM security posture before a board audit committee meeting. They need: what are the risks, which are most likely to affect us, what are we doing about it. owasp_application: | Present a heat map of LLM01-LLM10 with your organisation's likelihood and impact assessment for each. Colour-code: red (not controlled, high risk), amber (partially controlled), green (controlled). This gives the CISO a structured, industry-standard view of risk posture in the time available. what_not_to_do: | Do not present a raw technical list of vulnerabilities. Title

Limitations of the OWASP LLM Top 10 Classification

Content

Critical engagement with the OWASP LLM Top 10 is as important as fluency with it. A framework that is accepted uncritically becomes a ceiling rather than a floor. The following limitations should inform how you use and extend the OWASP classification. limitation_01_static_taxonomy_in_fast_moving_field: description: | The OWASP LLM Top 10 is a point-in-time snapshot of a field evolving monthly. Agentic AI deployments, multimodal models, and voice AI interfaces have introduced attack surfaces that were nascent when v1.1 was published. The list was not designed for systems where the LLM browses the web, controls CI/CD pipelines, or manages calendar and email autonomously at scale. implication: | Do not treat the absence of a risk from the Top 10 as evidence that the risk does not exist. Emerging risks — adversarial audio for voice AI, visual prompt injection in multimodal models, agent orchestration attacks in multi-agent systems — require you to go beyond the list. limitation_02_ranking_conflates_exploitability_and_impact: description: | LLM01 (Prompt Injection) ranks first partly because it is highly exploitable (any user with API access can attempt it) and partly because it is well-documented. But for many deployment contexts, LLM03 (Training Data Poisoning) or LLM06 (Sensitive Information Disclosure) represent higher business impact even if they are less exploitable or less frequently demonstrated. implication: | Always re-rank the OWASP LLM Top 10 for your specific deployment context. The generic ranking is a starting point, not a final prioritisation. A healthcare AI fine-tuned on patient data should place LLM06 at least equal to LLM01 in its risk register regardless of OWASP's ordering. limitation_03_prevention_focused_not_detection_focused: description: | The OWASP LLM Top 10 primarily describes prevention controls: don't trust user input, validate outputs, use least-privilege plugins. This maps well to developers building LLM applications. It maps poorly to security operations engineers building detection programmes. The framework does not tell you what logs to collect, what anomalies to detect, or how to respond when a risk is exploited. implication: | OWASP tells you what to prevent. MITRE ATLAS tells you what attacker behaviour to detect. NIST AI RMF tells you how to run the risk management process. You need all three frameworks, not just OWASP. limitation_04_limited_evidence_base: description: | The traditional OWASP Web Top 10 is backed by statistical analysis of vulnerability disclosures across thousands of penetration tests and bug bounty programmes. The OWASP LLM Top 10 is backed by expert consensus and a smaller evidence base. The ranking may not reflect actual frequency distributions in enterprise deployments — we simply do not have enough systematically collected data yet. implication: | Treat the OWASP LLM Top 10 ranking as expert opinion, not empirical fact. When your own incident data diverges from OWASP's ranking — e.g. if you observe more LLM04 (DoS) incidents than LLM01 (Injection) in your environment — your data should take precedence over the generic ranking. limitation_05_coarse_granularity: description: | Each OWASP LLM Top 10 category covers a cluster of related techniques. LLM01 (Prompt Injection) includes direct injection, indirect injection, multi-turn injection, and multi-modal injection — techniques with different attack prerequisites, different detection approaches, and different mitigations. Treating them as a single risk category may cause imprecision in detection programme design. implication: | Use OWASP for risk communication and compliance mapping. Use MITRE ATLAS at the technique level for detection engineering. The ATLAS technique (e.g. AML.T0051 — LLM Prompt Injection) is more precise than the OWASP category for the purpose of writing detection rules. limitation_06_agentic_and_multimodal_gaps: description: | v1.1 was authored when most enterprise LLM deployments were completion APIs. The explosive growth of agentic frameworks (AutoGPT, LangChain, Crew AI), multimodal models (GPT-4V, Claude 3 Vision, Gemini), and voice AI (real-time voice APIs) introduced attack surfaces for which the current list provides incomplete coverage. How OWASP Categories Evolve: Contribution and Tracking

Content

Understanding the OWASP contribution process matters for two reasons: it helps you track upcoming changes before they are finalised, and it positions you to contribute findings from your own detection work — which is both professionally visible and genuinely useful to the community. contribution_process: step_1_community_identification: description: | New risk candidates are identified through: published research papers, bug bounty disclosures, security conference presentations, and working group member practitioner experience. RESPONSE_SCORES = {"yes": 1.0, "partial": 0.5, "no": 0.0, "not_applicable": None}


def score_question(response_str: str) -> Optional[float]:
    return RESPONSE_SCORES.get(response_str.lower().strip())


def run_audit(questions: List[AuditQuestion],
              deployment_name: str = "LLM Deployment") -> Dict:
    """Run interactive audit checklist."""
    print(f"\nOWASP LLM TOP 10 AUDIT — {deployment_name}")
    print("=" * 65)
    print("For each question, respond: yes / no / partial / not_applicable")
    print("=" * 65)

    responses: Dict[str, AuditResponse] = {}
    risk_groups: Dict[str, List[str]] = {}

    for q in questions:
        if q.risk_id not in risk_groups:
            risk_groups[q.risk_id] = []
            print(f"\n--- {q.risk_id} ---")
        risk_groups[q.risk_id].append(q.id)

        print(f"\n  [{q.id}] {q.question}")
        print(f"  Evidence type: {q.evidence_type}")
        raw = input("  Response (yes/no/partial/not_applicable): ").strip()
        note = input("  Evidence note (optional, press Enter to skip): ").strip()
        score = score_question(raw)

        responses[q.id] = AuditResponse(
            question_id=q.id,
            response=raw,
            evidence_note=note,
            score=(score * q.weight) if score is not None else 0.0,
        )

    return compute_results(questions, responses, deployment_name)


def compute_results(questions: List[AuditQuestion],
                    responses: Dict[str, AuditResponse],
                    deployment_name: str) -> Dict:
    """Compute per-risk and overall coverage scores."""
    risk_ids = sorted(set(q.risk_id for q in questions))
    risk_results = {}

    for risk_id in risk_ids:
        risk_qs = [q for q in questions if q.risk_id == risk_id]
        applicable = [
            (q, responses[q.id]) for q in risk_qs
            if responses[q.id].response.lower() != "not_applicable"
        ]
        if not applicable:
            risk_results[risk_id] = {"score": None, "status": "not_applicable"}
            continue
        total_weight = sum(q.weight for q, _ in applicable)
        earned = sum(r.score for _, r in applicable)
        pct = earned / total_weight * 100 if total_weight > 0 else 0

        risk_results[risk_id] = {
            "score_pct": round(pct, 1),
            "status": "green" if pct >= 75 else "amber" if pct >= 40 else "red",
            "questions_answered": len(applicable),
            "questions_total": len(risk_qs),
        }

    applicable_risks = [v for v in risk_results.values() if v.get("score_pct") is not None]
    overall_score = (
        sum(v["score_pct"] for v in applicable_risks) / len(applicable_risks)
        if applicable_risks else 0
    )

    result = {
        "deployment": deployment_name,
        "audit_date": str(date.today()),
        "overall_score_pct": round(overall_score, 1),
        "overall_status": "green" if overall_score >= 75 else "amber" if overall_score >= 40 else "red",
        "per_risk_results": risk_results,
        "gaps": [
            {"risk_id": r, "score_pct": v["score_pct"], "status": v["status"]}
            for r, v in risk_results.items()
            if v.get("status") == "red"
        ],
    }

    print_audit_report(result)
    return result


def print_audit_report(result: Dict) -> None:
    """Print structured audit report."""
    status_icons = {"green": "✅", "amber": "⚠️ ", "red": "🚨"}
    print(f"\n{'='*65}")
    print(f"AUDIT RESULTS — {result['deployment']}")
    print(f"Date: {result['audit_date']}")
    print(f"{'='*65}")
    print(f"Overall Score:   {result['overall_score_pct']}%  "
          f"{status_icons.get(result['overall_status'], '?')}")
    print(f"\nPer-Risk Breakdown:")
    for risk_id, v in result["per_risk_results"].items():
        if v.get("score_pct") is None:
            print(f"  {risk_id}: N/A (not applicable)")
        else:
            icon = status_icons.get(v["status"], "?")
            print(f"  {risk_id}: {v['score_pct']:5.1f}% {icon}")
    if result["gaps"]:
        print(f"\nGAPS REQUIRING IMMEDIATE ATTENTION (score < 40%):")
        for gap in sorted(result["gaps"], key=lambda x: x["score_pct"]):
            print(f"  🚨 {gap['risk_id']}: {gap['score_pct']}%")

Key Takeaways

Takeaway 01

OWASP LLM Top 10 is the industry lingua franca for LLM security risk — the standard reference for risk registers, compliance questionnaires, vendor assessments, and board reporting. Fluency is professionally mandatory for AI security engineers.

Takeaway 02

The ranking methodology combines prevalence, exploitability, and impact. Rankings reflect the average deployment, not yours. Always re-rank for your specific context — a healthcare AI with fine-tuned patient data may rank LLM06 above LLM01.

Takeaway 03

Four OWASP LLM Top 10 risks have direct web security analogues (LLM01 ↔ A03 Injection, LLM02 ↔ A03 XSS, LLM04 ↔ DoS, LLM05 ↔ A06 Components). Four are genuinely novel with no web equivalent (LLM07, LLM08, LLM09, LLM10). Understanding which is which prevents misapplication of web security thinking.

Takeaway 04

OWASP LLM Top 10 is a risk classification framework, not a detection engineering specification. It tells you what to communicate and what to prioritise. MITRE ATLAS tells you what attacker behaviour to detect. NIST AI RMF tells you how to run the risk management process. Use all three.

Takeaway 05

OWASP LLM Top 10 has six significant limitations: static taxonomy in a fast-moving field, conflated exploitability and impact in ranking, prevention focus rather than detection focus, limited evidence base, coarse category granularity, and gaps in agentic and multimodal coverage. Know the limitations to use the framework correctly.

Takeaway 06

OWASP has achieved regulatory integration: EU AI Act Article 15 and Article 9, NIST AI RMF Playbook, SOC 2 CC9, and HIPAA Security Rule all align with or reference OWASP LLM Top 10 categories. Framing your security programme in OWASP terms connects security investment to compliance requirements.

Takeaway 07

The OWASP LLM Top 10 contribution process is public and open. Track the GitHub repository for upcoming category changes. Build to MITRE ATLAS technique granularity rather than just OWASP category coverage — ATLAS tracks emerging techniques faster and provides the precision needed for detection rule development.

Takeaway 08

The three-framework integration pipeline is: OWASP (identify the risk category) → MITRE ATLAS (identify the techniques) → NIST AI RMF (manage the risk) → Chapter 11-15 (build the detection). Each framework answers a different question; none answers all of them alone.
