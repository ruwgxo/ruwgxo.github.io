Section Overview

One Line Summary

Threat hunting finds novel attacks that deployed rules miss — not by looking for anything suspicious, but by testing specific falsifiable hypotheses against production data; every hunt either produces a new detection rule or validates existing coverage, so the detection programme improves regardless of outcome.

Why This Section Exists

Sections 11.02 through 11.11 built the reactive detection infrastructure: rules for known attack patterns, coverage measurement against known technique taxonomies, FPR management for deployed rules, and a OWASP coverage audit. Reactive detection is necessary but insufficient. It catches attacks that match known patterns; it is blind to novel techniques, novel evasion strategies, and novel attack actors who have studied the detection programme and adapted their behaviour to avoid its rules. Threat hunting closes this gap proactively. A detection engineer who periodically asks "what attack patterns might be present in our production logs that no deployed rule is catching?" — and then tests that question systematically against real data — finds the gaps that the coverage heat map cannot see, because the coverage heat map only measures coverage of named techniques. An attacker using a novel, unnamed technique is invisible to the heat map by definition. AI systems offer a unique hunting advantage that traditional log analysis lacks: the ability to compute semantic similarity across thousands of prompts in seconds. A hunter who clusters the past week''s prompt embeddings and inspects the anomalous clusters is doing something no traditional SIEM query can replicate — finding structural similarity in natural language without requiring a pattern match. This section builds that capability. Section 11.12 also completes the Section 11.08 maturity model: deploying the formal hunting procedure and establishing the quarterly cadence moves the Proactive Hunting dimension from L1 to L3.

Prose Content

Part 1 Reactive Vs Proactive

Heading

Reactive Detection, Threat Intelligence, and Threat Hunting: Three Distinct Disciplines

Body

Detection engineering produces three distinct outputs that are frequently conflated: reactive detection rules, threat intelligence consumption, and threat hunting. Understanding the distinction is the precondition for understanding what hunting adds that the other two cannot provide. Reactive detection rules match known patterns. A Sigma rule for prompt injection fires when a prompt contains a known injection prefix. It is effective against attacks that match the rule and blind to attacks that do not. The coverage heat map measures exactly this boundary: the boundary between what rules can see and what they cannot. Reactive detection is the foundation; everything else is built on it. Threat intelligence consumption brings external knowledge of new attack techniques into the detection programme. When a threat intelligence feed reports a new adversarial ML technique, the detection engineering workflow (Section 11.10) converts it into a detection hypothesis and eventually a rule. Threat intelligence extends the reactive detection foundation by adding rules for newly published techniques before they are used against the organisation. It is still reactive in the sense that it follows the attacker''s publication of the technique rather than discovering the technique in production logs. Threat hunting discovers attack patterns present in production logs that no deployed rule captures — because no rule exists for them yet. A hunter does not wait for a threat intelligence report. They ask: given what I know about attacker behaviour, what patterns might be present in our logs right now that our rules cannot see? They then test that question systematically against real data and either confirm a novel attack pattern (producing a new rule) or confirm that no such pattern is present (validating that existing coverage is effective for this threat scenario). The three disciplines are complementary. Reactive detection handles what is known. Threat intelligence handles what is newly published. Hunting handles what is novel and currently present. A detection programme without hunting is perpetually behind the attacker; it can only detect techniques it already knows about.

Part 2 Hypothesis Discipline

Heading

Hunting Is Not Fishing: The Hypothesis Requirement

Body

The most common error in threat hunting programmes is "fishing": opening a log analysis tool, browsing through data without a specific question, and looking for something that appears unusual. Fishing is not hunting. It does not produce reproducible results, it does not scale to production log volumes, and it cannot confirm or deny anything — an analyst who browses logs and finds nothing unusual cannot conclude that no attack is present, only that nothing caught their eye. Every hunt starts with a specific, falsifiable hypothesis. A hypothesis specifies: what pattern would be present if the hypothesised attack is occurring; which data source that pattern would appear in; and what analysis method would confirm or deny the hypothesis. A hypothesis that cannot be tested against data is not a hypothesis — it is speculation. The quality test for a hunting hypothesis: can it be falsified? "Are adversaries targeting our system?" cannot be falsified with data. "Are any users in the past seven days querying the model with prompts whose embeddings cluster within 0.15 cosine distance of the known-injection embedding centroid but do not match any Track 1 keyword or centroid-gate rule?" can be falsified: run the embedding similarity search against the past seven days of prompts, apply the distance threshold, filter out Track 1 keyword and centroid-gate hits, and examine what remains. The five hunting hypotheses in the threat_hunting_playbook.py deliverable all follow this structure. Each names the attack scenario, the expected observable pattern, the data source, the analysis method, and the falsification criterion. A hunt that produces zero results is not a failed hunt — it is a successful validation of existing coverage for the hypothesised attack scenario.

Part 3 Ai Hunting Data Sources

Heading

Data Sources for AI System Threat Hunting

Body

Five data sources are available for AI system threat hunting, each contributing signal that the others cannot provide: Data Source 1 — API gateway request logs (ai_gateway category). The primary source for prompt-level hunting. Contains prompt_text (or prompt hash), token_count, gateway_action, and all track scores. Enables: keyword pattern analysis, entropy distribution analysis, semantic similarity clustering, and anomalous track score combinations. Volume: the full production request corpus; typically sampled for hunting (7-day window, 100k request sample for weekly hunts). Data Source 2 — Session logs (ai_session category). The primary source for behavioural hunting across request sequences. Contains query_variance, query_entropy_avg, output_entropy_avg, and budget_consumed_pct. Enables: session-level outlier detection, identification of users whose session behaviour is anomalous relative to their own baseline, and detection of slowly-escalating query patterns. Data Source 3 — Model serving metrics. Metrics emitted by the model serving infrastructure: inference latency distribution, token generation rate, KV-cache hit rate. These are not request-level logs but aggregated time-series metrics. Enables: detecting prompt-induced computational anomalies (recursive self-reference causing latency spikes), unusual KV-cache eviction patterns from unusually structured prompts, and resource exhaustion precursors. Data Source 4 — LLM output logs. The response content after inference. Currently the output-side gap in the gateway (Section 11.11). When the Chapter 14 output validation layer is deployed, output logs become available for hunting: hunting for responses that contain code patterns, PII regex matches, or system prompt reflection patterns in the output corpus without individual rule triggers. Data Source 5 — Embedding similarity search. A derived source: computing prompt embeddings and running approximate nearest-neighbour search across the corpus. Enables: finding prompts that are semantically close to known-bad examples without matching any surface pattern rule; identifying clusters of structurally similar prompts that may represent a coordinated campaign; and detecting gradual semantic drift in the query distribution that may indicate an evolving attack campaign. This is the uniquely AI data source — traditional log analysis has no equivalent.

Part 4 Five Hypotheses

Heading

Five AI-Specific Hunting Hypotheses

Body

The five hypotheses encoded in threat_hunting_playbook.py target the detection gaps most relevant to the seven-track gateway. Each is written in the standard hypothesis format: scenario, expected pattern, data source, analysis method, falsification criterion, and pivot chain to Sigma rule if confirmed. Hypothesis 1 — Embedding cluster anomaly (novel injection variants). Scenario: adversaries are using injection variants that are semantically similar to known injections but surface-form different enough to evade Track 1's keywords and centroid gate. Expected pattern: a cluster of prompts within 0.20 cosine distance of the known-bad embedding centroid that have not been flagged by any deployed rule. Data source: api_gateway request logs (prompt embeddings computed offline). Analysis method: compute embeddings for the past 7-day prompt corpus; apply DBSCAN clustering; identify clusters with high centroid proximity to the known-bad centroid; filter out prompts already flagged by Track 1; examine remaining prompts manually. Falsification criterion: zero prompts in the high-proximity unflagged cluster → coverage confirmed for this attack scenario. Pivot chain: if confirmed → new injection variant → hypothesis for Section 11.10 Phase 1 → Track 1 keyword addition or centroid update. Hypothesis 2 — Session entropy outlier (systematic model behaviour probing). Scenario: an actor is probing the model''s response consistency by submitting near-identical prompts with minor variations — a reconnaissance pattern that precedes a more targeted extraction or injection campaign. Expected pattern: a user whose query_variance is in the bottom 5th percentile across all users in the 30-day window, with a session_request_count in the top 10th percentile for their user cohort, but who has not triggered Track 6 rate detection (because their absolute request count is below the rule threshold). Data source: ai_session logs, per-user baseline from false_positive_manager.py. Analysis method: compute per-user query_variance percentile and request_count percentile; identify the intersection of bottom-5th query_variance and top-10th request_count; cross-reference against Track 6 alert history to confirm unflagged. Falsification criterion: no users in the intersection set → Track 6 is catching all sub-threshold systematic probing. Hypothesis 3 — Coordinated timing pattern (multi-user coordinated campaign). Scenario: multiple accounts are submitting prompts in a coordinated pattern — same prompt structure, interleaved across accounts at regular intervals — suggesting a distributed attack that each individual account keeps below per-user detection thresholds. Expected pattern: three or more users submitting prompts with cosine similarity > 0.90 to each other within the same 15-minute window, where no single user''s activity triggers any deployed rule. Data source: api_gateway logs (prompt embeddings, timestamps, user_ids). Analysis method: sliding 15-minute window across the corpus; for each window, compute pairwise cosine similarities across distinct users; flag windows with 3+ users and pairwise similarity > 0.90. Falsification criterion: no qualifying windows → no coordinated campaign pattern detected in this period. Hypothesis 4 — System prompt drift (prompt injection via accumulated context). Scenario: an adversary is using multi-turn conversation to gradually shift the effective system prompt through accumulated context — each turn contributing a small instruction fragment that, combined across turns, overrides the original system prompt intent. Expected pattern: a user whose session-level prompt embedding centroid (computed across all turns) drifts more than 0.30 cosine distance from the session''s first turn embedding in a direction toward the known-bad centroid. Data source: api_gateway logs (prompt embeddings per turn, session_id). Analysis method: compute per-turn embeddings within each session; compute rolling centroid across turns; measure cosine drift from first-turn embedding; flag sessions with drift > 0.30 toward the known-bad centroid. Falsification criterion: no qualifying sessions → accumulated context drift attack not present in this period. Hypothesis 5 — Output entropy spike (model exfiltration via response patterns). Scenario: an adversary who has successfully injected instructions is causing the model to generate unusually high-entropy outputs — a signal consistent with exfiltrating structured data (training data, system prompt content) in the response. Expected pattern: a user whose output_entropy_avg is more than 2 standard deviations above the fleet-wide output_entropy_avg for their model and prompt_length cohort, with no corresponding Track 6 alert (Track 6's output entropy monitor did not fire). Data source: ai_session logs (output_entropy_avg); model serving metrics (per-model baseline output entropy). Note: requires Chapter 14 output validation layer for full signal; partial signal available from session-level entropy metrics now. Falsification criterion: no qualifying users → no output exfiltration pattern detected in this period.

Part 5 Hunt To Rule

Heading

Hunt-to-Rule Conversion: Closing the Self-Improvement Loop

Body

A confirmed hunt finding is evidence of an attack pattern that no deployed rule captures. The immediate action is containment if the attack is active; the detection engineering action is converting the finding into a rule before the hunting session closes. A finding that is not converted to a rule within five business days of confirmation degrades back to a gap — the next time the hunt runs, it will find the same pattern again, and the detection programme has not improved. The hunt-to-rule conversion uses the Section 11.10 detection engineering workflow starting at Phase 1 (hypothesis). The confirmed hunt finding is the hypothesis input: "I observed prompts matching pattern X in the past 7-day corpus that were not caught by any deployed rule. I hypothesise that a rule matching pattern X would detect this attack class." Phase 2 (design) specifies the log field to match, the detection operator, and the FPR target. Phase 3 (build) produces the Sigma rule or Python detector. Phase 4 (test) writes the positive fixture from the actual confirmed hunting examples and adds them to the known-bad corpus. Phase 5 deploys. Hunt findings that confirm ATLAS technique coverage gaps (the hunt found no detection for a technique that the heat map showed as partially covered) are added to the detection backlog as P1 items, bypassing the normal likelihood × impact priority score. A confirmed hunt finding is evidence that the attack is not merely theoretically possible but actively occurring or recently occurred in the production environment — that is higher priority than a scoring model based on threat intelligence alone. A hunt that finds nothing is not wasted effort. It is a validation of existing coverage for the hypothesised attack scenario. The validation is recorded in the hunt report and contributes to the detection programme''s confidence level for that scenario: "we hunted for coordinated timing patterns in the past 7-day corpus using the Hypothesis 3 procedure and found no qualifying windows." This is a defensible claim that reactive detection alone cannot make.

Part 6 Cadence

Heading

Hunting Cadence: Weekly, Monthly, and Quarterly

Body

The appropriate hunting cadence depends on the threat environment: Weekly hunting for active threat campaigns. When threat intelligence indicates that an adversary is actively targeting AI systems in the organisation''s sector, or when a recent security incident suggests an active campaign, the weekly cadence runs a focused subset of hypotheses against the most recent 7-day log window. Weekly hunts are narrow: one or two hypotheses per session, selected from the playbook based on the active campaign''s known tactics. The 7-day window keeps the data volume manageable for a 2–4 hour hunt session. Monthly hunting for routine monitoring. The standard cadence for a programme without active campaign intelligence. All five hypotheses run against the past 30-day window. The 30-day window captures behavioural patterns that are too slow for a 7-day window to reveal (Hypothesis 4 system prompt drift requires multiple sessions over days or weeks to manifest). Monthly hunts take 4–6 hours and produce a hunt report that feeds the detection backlog. Quarterly deep-technique hunting. A longer, more exploratory session aligned with the quarterly coverage review. The hunter selects hypotheses based on newly published ATLAS techniques or new attack research identified in the taxonomy expansion check from Section 11.05. New hypotheses are written for the quarter''s notable techniques and tested against the full 90-day log window. Quarterly hunts produce the hypotheses that become the next quarter''s playbook additions. The cadence gates in the Section 11.08 maturity model: L3 requires at least quarterly hunting with the formal procedure. L4 requires monthly hunting with red team findings driving hypothesis selection. L5 requires continuous automated hunting supplementing manual quarterly sessions. After deploying the threat_hunting_playbook.py and running the first formal quarterly hunt, the Proactive Hunting dimension advances from L1 to L3.

'''prompt_cluster_analyser.py
DBSCAN-based prompt clustering for threat hunting.
Identifies anomalous prompt clusters by proximity to known-bad embedding centroid.

from __future__ import annotations
import argparse
import json
import sys
from dataclasses import dataclass

import numpy as np

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Data model
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

@dataclass
class ClusterResult:
    cluster_id: int
    size: int
    centroid: np.ndarray
    centroid_distance_to_known_bad: float
    flagged_fraction: float       # fraction already flagged by deployed rules
    unflagged_count: int
    proximity_label: str          # 'high' | 'medium' | 'low'
    anomalous: bool               # True if high-proximity and unflagged_count > 0

@dataclass
class PromptRecord:
    prompt_id: str
    embedding: np.ndarray
    already_flagged: bool         # gateway_action != 'pass'
    user_id: str = ''

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Clustering
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def load_corpus(corpus_path: str) -> list[PromptRecord]:
    '''
    Load prompt corpus from JSON file.
    Expected format: list of {prompt_id, embedding (list[float]),
    already_flagged (bool), user_id (str)}.
    '''
    with open(corpus_path) as f:
        raw = json.load(f)
    records: list[PromptRecord] = []
    for item in raw:
        records.append(PromptRecord(
            prompt_id=item['prompt_id'],
            embedding=np.array(item['embedding'], dtype=np.float32),
            already_flagged=item.get('already_flagged', False),
            user_id=item.get('user_id', ''),
        ))
    return records

def run_dbscan(
    records: list[PromptRecord],
    eps: float = 0.15,
    min_samples: int = 5,
) -> dict[int, list[PromptRecord]]:
    '''
    Run DBSCAN clustering on prompt embeddings.
    Returns mapping of cluster_id -> list of PromptRecord.
    Cluster -1 contains noise points (no cluster).
    '''
    from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN

    embeddings = np.stack([r.embedding for r in records])
    # DBSCAN with cosine metric (1 - cosine_similarity as distance)
    model = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=min_samples, metric='cosine', n_jobs=-1)
    labels = model.fit_predict(embeddings)

    clusters: dict[int, list[PromptRecord]] = {}
    for record, label in zip(records, labels):
        clusters.setdefault(int(label), []).append(record)
    return clusters

def compute_centroid(records: list[PromptRecord]) -> np.ndarray:
    '''Compute mean embedding centroid for a list of records.'''
    embeddings = np.stack([r.embedding for r in records])
    centroid = embeddings.mean(axis=0)
    return centroid / (np.linalg.norm(centroid) + 1e-8)

def cosine_distance(a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray) -> float:
    '''Cosine distance (1 - cosine similarity) between two unit vectors.'''
    a = a / (np.linalg.norm(a) + 1e-8)
    b = b / (np.linalg.norm(b) + 1e-8)
    return float(1.0 - np.dot(a, b))

def analyse_clusters(
    clusters: dict[int, list[PromptRecord]],
    known_bad_centroid: np.ndarray,
    high_proximity_threshold: float = 0.20,
    medium_proximity_threshold: float = 0.40,
) -> list[ClusterResult]:
    results: list[ClusterResult] = []
    for cluster_id, records in clusters.items():
        if cluster_id == -1:
            continue  # skip noise
        centroid = compute_centroid(records)
        dist = cosine_distance(centroid, known_bad_centroid)
        flagged = sum(1 for r in records if r.already_flagged)
        unflagged = len(records) - flagged
        flagged_frac = flagged / len(records) if records else 0.0

        if dist <= high_proximity_threshold:
            prox_label = 'high'
        elif dist <= medium_proximity_threshold:
            prox_label = 'medium'
        else:
            prox_label = 'low'

        anomalous = prox_label == 'high' and unflagged > 0

        results.append(ClusterResult(
            cluster_id=cluster_id,
            size=len(records),
            centroid=centroid,
            centroid_distance_to_known_bad=round(dist, 4),
            flagged_fraction=round(flagged_frac, 3),
            unflagged_count=unflagged,
            proximity_label=prox_label,
            anomalous=anomalous,
        ))

    results.sort(key=lambda r: r.centroid_distance_to_known_bad)
    return results

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Coordinated pattern detection (Hypothesis H3)
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

def detect_coordinated_patterns(
    records: list[PromptRecord],
    window_minutes: int = 15,
    similarity_threshold: float = 0.90,
    min_users: int = 3,
    timestamps: list[float] | None = None,
) -> list[dict]:
    '''
    Slide a time window across the corpus and flag windows where
    3+ distinct users have pairwise cosine similarity > threshold.
    timestamps: Unix timestamps aligned with records (optional).
    Returns list of flagged window descriptors.
    '''
    if timestamps is None or len(timestamps) != len(records):
        return []   # timestamps required for this analysis

    window_sec = window_minutes * 60
    flagged_windows: list[dict] = []
    ts_array = np.array(timestamps)

    for i, t_start in enumerate(timestamps):
        t_end = t_start + window_sec
        window_mask = (ts_array >= t_start) & (ts_array < t_end)
        window_records = [r for r, m in zip(records, window_mask) if m]

        # Group by user_id
        by_user: dict[str, list[PromptRecord]] = {}
        for r in window_records:
            by_user.setdefault(r.user_id, []).append(r)

        distinct_users = [uid for uid in by_user if uid]
        if len(distinct_users) < min_users:
            continue

        # Compute per-user mean embeddings
        user_embeddings = {
            uid: compute_centroid(recs)
            for uid, recs in by_user.items()
            if uid
        }

        # Check pairwise similarity
        user_ids = list(user_embeddings.keys())
        high_sim_pairs = 0
        for j in range(len(user_ids)):
            for k in range(j + 1, len(user_ids)):
                sim = 1.0 - cosine_distance(
                    user_embeddings[user_ids[j]],
                    user_embeddings[user_ids[k]],
                )
                if sim >= similarity_threshold:
                    high_sim_pairs += 1

        if high_sim_pairs >= 1 and len(distinct_users) >= min_users:
            flagged_windows.append({
                'window_start': t_start,
                'window_end': t_end,
                'distinct_users': len(distinct_users),
                'high_sim_pairs': high_sim_pairs,
                'user_ids': user_ids[:5],  # anonymise; show max 5
            })
            break  # one flag per window is sufficient for the hunt report

    return flagged_windows

Common Mistakes

Id

CM11.12.1

Mistake

Treating threat hunting as "browsing logs to see what looks suspicious"

Why It Happens

Log browsing is familiar. Security engineers spend time in log analysis tools and develop intuitions about what looks unusual. The informal version of this activity is called hunting but lacks the discipline that produces reproducible, scalable, and falsifiable results.

Why It Matters

Informal log browsing cannot be validated. An analyst who browses 7-day logs and finds nothing cannot conclude that no attack is present — they can only conclude that nothing caught their eye. The same analyst on a different day might find something different. The results are not reproducible, the methodology cannot be reviewed, and the "nothing found" outcome provides no coverage validation.

Correct Approach

Every hunt session starts with a written hypothesis before any data is opened. The hypothesis specifies the expected pattern, the data source, and the analysis method. "Nothing found" is only a meaningful outcome when it means "I tested this specific falsifiable hypothesis and the data does not support it" — not "I looked around and didn''t see anything suspicious."

Id

CM11.12.2

Mistake

Discarding hunt findings that confirm coverage rather than recording them

Why It Happens

A hunt that finds nothing feels like a wasted session. The analyst moves on without filing a report. Only positive findings receive documentation.

Why It Matters

"Coverage validated" findings are defensible claims to leadership and auditors. "We ran Hypothesis H1 against the 7-day corpus of 94,000 prompts and found zero unflagged prompts within 0.20 cosine distance of the known-bad centroid" is a meaningful security statement. Without the report, the organisation cannot make that claim. The accumulated validated-coverage reports are also the evidence base for advancing the Proactive Hunting dimension to L3 and beyond.

Correct Approach

File a hunt report for every completed hunt, regardless of outcome. The threat_hunting_playbook.py --record command requires an outcome field: confirmed_finding, coverage_validated, or inconclusive. All three are valid and all three are recorded. Reports are stored in hunt_reports/ and reviewed at the quarterly programme review.

Running hypothesis H5 (output entropy spike) without the Chapter 14 output validation layer

Why It Happens

H5 is listed in the playbook. The analyst runs it before Chapter 14 is deployed, expecting to use session-level output_entropy_avg as a proxy for the full output analysis.

Why It Matters

Session-level output_entropy_avg is a coarse proxy. A successful exfiltration attack that produces high-entropy output in a few responses within an otherwise normal session may not elevate the session-level average significantly enough to flag. Running H5 on the session-level metric only and finding nothing provides false confidence — the full output log signal (available after Chapter 14) may tell a different story.

Correct Approach

Flag H5 in the playbook as "partial signal until Chapter 14." Run H5 now using session-level output_entropy_avg as a preliminary hunt; note in the report that the hunt is limited to the session-level metric and should be re-run against full output logs after Chapter 14 is deployed. Do not treat a "coverage validated" outcome from the session-level H5 hunt as equivalent to a full H5 validation.

CM11.12.4

Mistake

Converting every hunt finding directly to a rule without the Section 11.10 workflow

Why It Happens

A confirmed finding creates urgency. The analyst writes a quick Sigma rule to capture the pattern, deploys it manually, and considers the finding closed.

Why It Matters

A rule written in response to urgency without tests, without metadata, without CI validation, and without FPR target is an L1 rule added to an L3 programme. It bypasses every governance control established in Sections 11.02 through 11.09, potentially adds false positives to the SOC queue from day one, and may conflict with existing rules in ways that only systematic review would catch.

Correct Approach

Every confirmed hunt finding enters the Section 11.10 detection engineering workflow at Phase 1 (hypothesis). The finding is the hypothesis input. The rule goes through all seven phases: design, build, test (using the hunt''s confirmed examples as positive fixtures), deploy via CI, monitor FPR. The five business day target for rule creation is achievable through the workflow without skipping governance steps.

CM11.12.5

Mistake

Using a fixed DBSCAN epsilon across all prompt corpora without calibrating for the embedding model

Why It Happens

The default epsilon (0.15 cosine distance) is specified in the playbook and deliverable. Practitioners use it without checking whether it is appropriate for their embedding model''s distance distribution.

Why It Matters

Different embedding models produce different distance distributions. The cosine distance between two random prompts varies by model: a model that produces more clustered embeddings will require a smaller epsilon; a model that spreads prompts more evenly in the embedding space will require a larger epsilon. Using 0.15 with a model calibrated for 0.30 produces many tiny clusters, each of which appears anomalous in proximity analysis but most of which are noise. The hunt produces a large number of false findings.

Correct Approach

Before the first hunt session, calibrate the DBSCAN epsilon by computing the k-nearest-neighbour distance distribution for k=5 across a sample of production prompts. Plot the sorted k-NN distances (the "elbow plot") and select epsilon at the elbow — the point where distance begins to increase steeply. This calibrated epsilon is specific to the embedding model and corpus and should be documented in the hunt report metadata.

Section Summary

What You Learned

Threat hunting finds novel attacks that reactive detection rules miss — by testing specific, falsifiable hypotheses against production data rather than browsing logs for anything suspicious. AI systems offer the unique hunting advantage of embedding- space analysis: clustering thousands of prompts by semantic similarity to find structural attack patterns without predefined signatures. The five playbook hypotheses cover the most significant detection gaps in the seven-track gateway: novel injection variants (H1), sub-threshold systematic probing (H2), coordinated multi-user campaigns (H3), accumulated context injection (H4), and output exfiltration patterns (H5). Every hunt produces either a confirmed finding (which enters the Section 11.10 workflow as a new rule) or a validated coverage outcome (which is a defensible security claim). Deploying the formal procedure and quarterly cadence advances the Proactive Hunting maturity dimension from L1 to L3.

Key Terms

Term

Threat hunting

Definition

Proactive, hypothesis-driven analysis of production data to find attack patterns that no deployed detection rule captures. Distinct from reactive detection (rules for known patterns) and threat intelligence (consuming externally published technique knowledge). Finds novel attacks currently present in production logs.

Term

Hunting hypothesis

Definition

A specific, falsifiable statement about an attack pattern that would be present in a named data source if the hypothesised attack is occurring. Specifies the expected pattern, the data source, the analysis method, and the falsification criterion. "Look for bad things" is not a hypothesis.

Term

Coverage validated

Definition

A hunt outcome in which the hypothesis was tested systematically and the data does not support the hypothesised attack pattern. A defensible claim: "we tested this specific hypothesis against N records and found no qualifying pattern." Distinct from "nothing looked suspicious."

Term

Hunt-to-rule conversion

Definition

The process of converting a confirmed hunt finding into a deployed Sigma rule via the Section 11.10 seven-phase detection engineering workflow. The confirmed hunting examples become the positive test fixtures. Target: rule deployed within five business days of confirmed finding.

Term

Embedding-space hunting

Definition

Threat hunting using semantic similarity in the embedding space of an LLM or sentence encoder. Identifies structurally similar prompts without requiring surface-pattern matching. The unique AI security hunting capability with no equivalent in traditional log analysis.
