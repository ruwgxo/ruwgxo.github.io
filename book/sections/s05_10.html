Title

Why Models Leak Training Data: The Memorisation Mechanism

Content

the_core_paradox: | Machine learning models are trained to generalise — to produce correct outputs on unseen data by learning statistical patterns, not by memorising specific examples. Yet research consistently demonstrates that large models, particularly large language models, memorise substantial portions of their training data verbatim. These two facts are not contradictory. Understanding why models memorise is the prerequisite for building effective defences. why_memorisation_happens: mechanism_01_gradient_descent_and_repetition: | Gradient descent minimises training loss. If an example appears in the training corpus n times, the gradient signal for that example accumulates across n presentations — each occurrence pushing the model weights toward reproducing that specific text. A passage repeated 100 times in training data generates 100x more gradient weight than a passage seen once. Memorisation of frequently-occurring text is, from the optimiser's perspective, the correct behaviour: it minimises loss on those examples. mechanism_02_model_capacity_and_overfit: | A model with more parameters has greater representational capacity. When capacity far exceeds the information content of the training set, the model can afford to allocate capacity to memorising individual examples rather than relying solely on generalised patterns. This is the overfitting regime applied to language model training: the model has room to both learn statistical patterns and memorise specific sequences. The scaling relationship documented by Carlini et al. (2021): as model size increases from 1.3B to 6B parameters on the same training data, the number of memorised training examples increases disproportionately. Scale is not a defence against memorisation — it is a risk amplifier. mechanism_03_fine_tuning_amplification: | When a pre-trained model is fine-tuned on a smaller, domain-specific dataset, the fine-tuning dataset is presented to the model many more times relative to its size than the pre-training data. A model pre-trained on 1T tokens with 1 epoch sees each token once. The same model fine-tuned on 100M tokens with 3 epochs sees each fine-tuning token ~300x more per-token than pre-training data. Fine-tuning dramatically amplifies memorisation of fine-tuning examples — which are precisely the sensitive internal documents, customer records, and proprietary data that organisations use for fine-tuning. mechanism_04_context_completion_dynamics: | LLMs generate text by predicting the most probable next token given context. For memorised sequences, the model's internal representation of the context directly encodes the continuation — the model does not need to compute anything, it retrieves. This is why prefix prompts that match memorised training data prefixes trigger verbatim recall: the context activates stored sequence representations rather than sampling from a generalised distribution. memorisation_vs_generalisation_boundary: | Not all training influence is memorisation. A model that learns "Python functions start with def" has learned a statistical pattern — it will generate "def" in appropriate contexts regardless of any specific Python file in training data. This is generalisation. A model that can reproduce a specific GitHub user's SSH private key from a partial prompt has memorised that specific sequence. The boundary between these is not sharp, but the distinction matters for threat modelling and defence design.

Title

Memorisation Taxonomy: Verbatim, Approximate, and Concept-Level

Content

taxonomy_purpose: | The three-level memorisation taxonomy from Carlini et al. (2023) provides a framework for: (a) classifying extraction risk by severity, (b) choosing appropriate detection strategies, and (c) setting realistic expectations for differential privacy and unlearning defences. level_01_verbatim_memorisation: definition: | The model reproduces a training sequence character-for-character (or token-for-token) given a sufficient prompt prefix. The output is indistinguishable from copy-pasting from the original document. formal_definition: | A sequence s is k-extractable from model M if there exists a prompt p such that M(p) produces s[len(p):] verbatim for the first k tokens. Carlini et al. define "extractable memorisation" using k=50 tokens. examples: - "SSH private key reproduced verbatim from 'Begin RSA Private Key' prefix" - "Credit card number reproduced from partial card sequence prefix" - "Person's email address reproduced from 'To reach [Name] contact' prefix" - "GPL licence text reproduced from 'This program is free software' prefix" - "Medical record content reproduced from patient demographic prefix" detection_approach: | N-gram overlap comparison between model output and training corpus reference. High overlap (>0.8 ROUGE-L against any training document) signals verbatim memorisation event. severity: "Critical — direct data breach equivalent" level_02_approximate_memorisation: definition: | The model reproduces training content with minor variations — paraphrase, reformatting, or partial omission — that preserve the substantive informational content of the original. examples: - "API key reproduced with one character changed" - "Patient record paraphrased with same demographics and diagnosis preserved" - "Proprietary algorithm description reproduced with variable names changed" - "Personal address reproduced with house number off by one" detection_approach: | Semantic similarity (embedding cosine similarity) between model output and training corpus. High semantic similarity without high lexical overlap indicates approximate memorisation. Harder to detect than verbatim. severity: "High — substantive PII or IP disclosure even without exact match" level_03_concept_level_memorisation: definition: | The model has learned specific facts, attributes, or associations from training data that are not generalisable statistical patterns — they are specific to individuals or entities in the training corpus and can be elicited by targeted prompting. examples: - "Model knows a specific person's employer and city from training-data mentions" - "Model associates a non-public code name with a product description" - "Model produces a person's phone number when asked about them by name" - "Model reveals a proprietary internal process when asked about the company" detection_approach: | Hard to detect from output monitoring alone. Requires membership inference (Section 5.11) or targeted canary token recall testing. severity: "Medium-High — depends on sensitivity of the specific facts" memorisation_detection_priority_by_level: verbatim: "Highest priority — detectable with n-gram overlap; implement first" approximate: "High priority — requires semantic similarity monitoring; implement second" concept_level: "Medium priority — requires canary tokens or membership inference; implement third"

Title

Carlini et al. (2021): Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models

Content

paper_context: | The 2021 paper "Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models" by Nicholas Carlini, Florian Tramèr, Eric Wallace, Matthew Jagielski, Ariel Herbert-Voss, Katherine Lee, Adam Roberts, Tom Brown, Dawn Song, Ulfar Erlingsson, Alina Oprea, and Colin Raffel is the foundational reference for training data extraction as a practical security concern. It demonstrated, against GPT-2, that a black-box adversary with API access can extract verbatim training examples including PII — without any white-box knowledge of model weights. key_findings: finding_01_scale_amplifies_memorisation: | The paper compared memorisation rates across GPT-2 model sizes: GPT-2 Small (117M params) to GPT-2 XL (1.5B params). Larger models memorised significantly more training examples. The top-n memorised sequences per model grew roughly linearly with model size. Implication: scaling is not a privacy defence. GPT-4-scale models memorise far more than GPT-2 — the attack surface grows with capability. finding_02_repeated_data_memorised_first: | Sequences that appeared multiple times in the training corpus (WebText) were extracted at dramatically higher rates than single-occurrence sequences. A sequence appearing 10+ times was near-certain to be memorised and extractable. Implication: training data deduplication is the single most impactful memorisation defence. Removing duplicates reduces the gradient signal that drives memorisation of specific sequences. finding_03_generation_temperature_matters: | Lower generation temperature (more deterministic sampling) produced higher extraction rates. At temperature=0 (greedy decoding), the model produces the single highest-probability token at each step — maximising the probability of verbatim recall for memorised sequences. Implication: APIs that expose deterministic generation (temperature=0 or very low temperature) are higher-risk extraction surfaces than stochastic APIs. finding_04_divergence_as_extraction_signal: | Memorised text deviates from the model's normal generation distribution in a detectable way. Under normal conditions, model output exhibits some randomness. When reproducing memorised text, the model assigns very high probability to the next token — producing anomalously high- confidence (low-entropy) generation. This divergence between normal generation entropy and memorised-sequence entropy is the detection signal. finding_05_real_pii_extracted_at_scale: | The paper extracted real, verifiable PII from GPT-2 outputs including personal names associated with contact information, URLs, and code sequences. This was from a model trained on public web data (WebText/Reddit). For models fine-tuned on internal data, the extraction risk is substantially higher because: (1) the fine-tuning data is repeated more relative to its size, (2) the fine-tuning data is more sensitive, and (3) the adversary can target the extraction with domain knowledge about the fine-tuning corpus. extraction_attack_methodology: step_01_prefix_construction: | Adversary constructs probe prompts designed to activate memorised sequences: (a) Partial sequences from suspected training data (if known) (b) Generic domain prompts ("The patient presented with") (c) High-repetition prefixes from likely training sources ("Copyright (c)", "All rights reserved", "def ", "import ") (d) Entity-targeted prompts ("Contact [person name] at") Each prefix is designed to position the model at the start of a potentially memorised sequence. step_02_bulk_generation: | Generate 500-10,000 completions per prefix, typically at low temperature. Carlini et al. generated 600 tokens per prompt for each of ~100 prompts and evaluated the resulting sequences. In API terms: this is 50,000- 100,000 token generations per extraction campaign, spread across prompts. step_03_membership_inference_filtering: | From the bulk generated sequences, filter for those most likely to be memorised training data rather than generated text. Filtering criteria: - Low perplexity under the model (high-confidence generation) - Low perplexity under a reference model (not just a low-probability quirk) - High n-gram overlap with known training sources - Verbatim match with reference internet content Sequences that pass this filter are the extraction candidates. step_04_verification: | Verify extraction candidates against the actual training corpus (if available) or against public internet sources. For the adversary: verification confirms whether the extracted sequence is verbatim training data or high-confidence generation. For the defender: verification calibrates the detection threshold. five_high_memorisation_data_types: personal_identifiable_information: | Names + contact information: extracted from social media profiles, forum posts, news articles, and contact pages indexed in web training data. credentials_and_secrets: | API keys, SSH private keys, passwords: accidentally included in GitHub repositories, Pastebin posts, and forums — all common training sources. Carlini et al. demonstrated extraction of what appeared to be real credentials. medical_and_health_data: | Clinical language, diagnosis patterns, and (in models fine-tuned on medical data) specific patient information. Fine-tuned clinical LLMs face the highest extraction risk for this category. proprietary_code_and_algorithms: | Verbatim code sequences from GitHub. Particularly relevant for models trained on code corpora or fine-tuned on internal codebases. legal_and_financial_documents: | Contract language, financial figures, and case references from legal databases and financial filings included in training corpora.

Title

Extraction Risk Factors: Model Size, Data Repetition, and Fine-Tuning

Content

risk_factor_01_model_size: finding: | Memorisation scales with model parameter count. Larger models have greater capacity to simultaneously learn statistical patterns and memorise specific sequences. The Carlini et al. (2021) finding (memorisation increases with scale) has been replicated across model families. quantification: | Doubling model parameters approximately doubles the number of extractable memorised sequences at fixed training data size. A 70B parameter model fine-tuned on 10K internal documents has dramatically higher per-document memorisation risk than a 7B parameter model fine-tuned on the same data. implication: | Large model deployments serving sensitive domains require correspondingly more aggressive extraction monitoring and differential privacy defences than smaller model deployments. risk_factor_02_data_repetition: finding: | The single strongest predictor of verbatim memorisation is the number of times a sequence appears in the training corpus. The relationship is approximately: P(memorised) ≈ 1 − exp(−k × repetitions) where k is a model-size-dependent constant. A sequence appearing 10+ times in training data is near-certain to be memorisable at extraction. quantification: | Carlini et al.: sequences appearing once → ~1% extraction rate. Sequences appearing 10+ times → ~40-70% extraction rate. Sequences appearing 100+ times → near-certain extraction. implication: | Deduplication of training data is the highest-ROI memorisation defence available. Near-duplicate removal (MinHash / n-gram shingle similarity) catches paraphrase variants that exact-match dedup misses. detection_angle: | Memorisation risk scorer: compute repetition count for each n-gram sequence in the training corpus. High-repetition sequences are extraction targets and should be monitored in output scanning. risk_factor_03_fine_tuning: finding: | Fine-tuning on a small, sensitive dataset dramatically amplifies memorisation of fine-tuning examples relative to pre-training data. The fine-tuning dataset is repeated many times (multiple epochs), and its smaller size means each example receives more gradient updates per epoch than pre-training examples. quantification: | A model fine-tuned for 10 epochs on 10K documents presents each fine-tuning document to the training process 10 times, creating strong gradient signals for verbatim memorisation. A 100-document fine-tuning dataset with 50 epochs is near-certainly memorised. high_risk_fine_tuning_scenarios: - "Customer service fine-tuning on real support tickets (PII in transcripts)" - "Legal document fine-tuning on internal contracts" - "Medical fine-tuning on clinical notes" - "Code fine-tuning on internal proprietary codebase" - "Financial fine-tuning on internal reports and filings" implication: | Organisations fine-tuning LLMs on sensitive internal data must treat the resulting model as a potential vector for extraction of that data. Differential privacy training and aggressive deduplication of fine-tuning data are required controls, not optional enhancements. risk_factor_04_inference_configuration: finding: | Low generation temperature (→ deterministic) produces higher extraction rates than high temperature (stochastic). APIs exposing logprobs or greedy decoding options increase extraction efficiency. implication: | In extraction-sensitive deployments: (a) set minimum temperature > 0, (b) disable logprob exposure for non-internal callers, (c) enable sampling to add entropy to generation. memorisation_risk_score_formula: | RISK_SCORE(corpus) = 0.4 × normalised_repetition_rate(corpus) # fraction of 5-grams appearing >3 times + 0.4 × pii_density(corpus) # fraction of tokens in PII spans + 0.2 × sensitive_data_category_count(corpus) # how many high-risk categories present Thresholds: RISK_SCORE < 0.3 → Low — standard monitoring 0.3-0.6 → Medium — aggressive monitoring + consider DP training > 0.6 → High — DP training required + canary tokens mandatory

Title

Differential Privacy: The Formal Defence and Its Practicality

Content

what_differential_privacy_is: | Differential privacy (DP) is a formal mathematical framework for quantifying and bounding privacy loss in data analysis. Applied to machine learning, DP-SGD (Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent) adds calibrated Gaussian noise to per-sample gradients during training, making it mathematically provable that the trained model's parameters are approximately the same whether or not any specific individual's data was included. the_epsilon_delta_guarantee: | A mechanism M satisfies (ε, δ)-differential privacy if for any two datasets D and D' differing by one example, and for any output S: P[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ exp(ε) × P[M(D') ∈ S] + δ Interpretation: ε (epsilon) — privacy budget. Lower ε = stronger privacy guarantee. ε = 0: perfect privacy (model output independent of any individual record) ε = 1: strong guarantee — adversary gains at most e^1 ≈ 2.7x advantage ε = 8: weak guarantee — meaningful memorisation still possible ε = ∞: no guarantee (standard training) δ (delta) — failure probability. Typically set to 1/n where n = training set size. δ = 10^-5 means the guarantee fails for at most 1 in 100,000 runs. dp_sgd_mechanics: | DP-SGD modifies standard gradient descent in two steps: Step 1 — Per-sample gradient clipping: For each training example i, clip its gradient g_i to L2 norm C: g_i_clipped = g_i / max(1, ||g_i||_2 / C) This bounds the maximum influence any single example can have. Step 2 — Gaussian noise addition: Add isotropic Gaussian noise scaled to the clipping norm and epsilon: g_noisy = (1/batch_size) × (Σ g_i_clipped + N(0, σ²C²I)) where σ is the noise multiplier derived from target (ε, δ). This obscures individual gradient contributions. accuracy_privacy_tradeoff: | DP training imposes an accuracy cost that scales inversely with model size and directly with privacy strength. Empirical results across NLP benchmarks: epsilon_1: privacy_level: "Strong" memorisation_reduction: "~90% reduction in extractable sequences" accuracy_cost_small_model: "10-15% accuracy degradation" accuracy_cost_large_model: "3-8% accuracy degradation" recommendation: "Required for medical/legal fine-tuning on small models" epsilon_4: privacy_level: "Moderate" memorisation_reduction: "~70% reduction in extractable sequences" accuracy_cost_small_model: "5-10% accuracy degradation" accuracy_cost_large_model: "1-4% accuracy degradation" recommendation: "Good balance for customer data fine-tuning on mid-size models" epsilon_8: privacy_level: "Weak" memorisation_reduction: "~40% reduction in extractable sequences" accuracy_cost_small_model: "2-5% accuracy degradation" accuracy_cost_large_model: "< 2% accuracy degradation" recommendation: "Minimum meaningful DP; insufficient for high-sensitivity data" epsilon_inf: privacy_level: "None (standard training)" memorisation_reduction: "0%" accuracy_cost: "0%" recommendation: "Never use for sensitive fine-tuning data" dp_practicality_notes: | Large model advantage: DP accuracy costs shrink with model size because large models generalise better and are less dependent on memorising specific examples. For 70B+ parameter models fine-tuned with LoRA, ε=4 DP training is often achievable with < 2% accuracy cost. The Hugging Face trl library and Google's dp-accounting library provide production-ready DP-SGD implementations with privacy accounting. dp_limitations: | DP training does not eliminate memorisation — it bounds it probabilistically. DP provides per-record protection, not corpus-level protection. Defences other than DP: training data deduplication, canary tokens, output monitoring. These are complementary, not alternatives.

Title

Detection: API Log Analysis and Output Monitoring for Verbatim Recall

Content

detection_strategy_overview: | Extraction attacks are detection engineering problems. The adversary's API behaviour differs from legitimate usage in measurable ways, and the model's output at verbatim recall events differs from normal generation in measurable ways. Two complementary detection layers are required: Layer 1 — Input behaviour monitoring: detect extraction campaign patterns in API request logs (AML.T0037 detection from Section 5.8) Layer 2 — Output content monitoring: detect verbatim recall events in model outputs in real time layer_1_input_behaviour_detection: signal_01_prefix_probe_pattern: | Extraction campaigns submit many requests sharing a common prefix, with small variations in the suffix. The adversary is probing whether different completions of the same prefix context trigger memorised sequences. Detection: common_prefix_count > 30 in 10 minutes by one account. FP: autocomplete applications — calibrate threshold per application type. signal_02_low_temperature_requests: | Extraction efficiency peaks at temperature=0 or very low temperature. Adversaries who know this will request low-temperature generation. Detection: temperature < 0.2 AND request_count > 100 in 1 hour. FP: deterministic evaluation pipelines — whitelist pre-registered eval keys. signal_03_logprob_access_at_scale: | If the API exposes token log-probabilities, extraction efficiency multiplies because the adversary can directly measure memorisation likelihood per token. Detection: logprobs=true AND queries_per_day > 5,000. signal_04_high_volume_one_shot: | Extraction campaigns use one-shot or zero-shot queries (short context, long completion) rather than the multi-turn dialogue pattern of legitimate users. Detection: avg_turns < 2 AND output_token_count > 200 AND volume > 1,000/day. layer_2_output_content_monitoring: what_to_monitor: | Every model output should be scanned for verbatim recall signals before being returned to the caller. This scan adds latency (typically 5-50ms with optimised implementation) but provides the only real-time signal for verbatim memorisation events. signal_01_ngram_overlap: | Compute n-gram overlap between model output and a reference corpus of known-sensitive training data (PII, credentials, proprietary documents). High ROUGE-L score (>0.7) between output and any reference document indicates verbatim recall of that document. For web-scale models: maintain a canary corpus of known-sensitive sequences as reference — not the full training corpus. signal_02_output_entropy: | At verbatim recall events, the model generates with anomalously high confidence — token entropy drops sharply. The perplexity-per-token of memorised sequences is significantly lower than normal generation. Detection: rolling token entropy in output falls below threshold for > 20 consecutive tokens. Requires: log-probability access to model output, or a lightweight reference model for entropy estimation. signal_03_pii_pattern_matching: | Scan output for PII patterns using a PII detector: phone numbers, email addresses, SSNs, credit card patterns, IBAN, API key patterns (AWS AKIA prefix, GitHub ghp_ prefix, etc.). Any PII in model output triggers immediate review. signal_04_canary_token_recall: | Inject unique, never-publicly-disclosed strings (canary tokens) into training data at known locations. Monitor all outputs for exact or near-exact canary token matches. Any canary recall confirms training data memorisation and provides a calibrated extraction detection anchor. False-positive rate: zero (canary tokens are unique by construction). gdpr_and_legal_implications: right_to_erasure_conflict: | GDPR Article 17 grants data subjects the right to erasure — the right to have personal data deleted. For LLMs trained on personal data, this creates a fundamental technical conflict: erasing personal data from a trained model's weights is not equivalent to deleting a database record. Retraining from scratch without the requested data is the only complete solution — computationally prohibitive for large models. machine_unlearning_state_of_art: | Machine unlearning research attempts to remove specific training examples from model weights without full retraining: SISA training: partition training data into shards; retrain only affected shard Gradient ascent unlearning: apply reversed gradient updates to maximise loss on the forgotten examples — approximates "unlearning" without full retrain DP retroactive: if trained with DP, erasure request can be satisfied by re-running DP accounting with the data point excluded from the guarantee Current state: none of these provides the same guarantee as not training on the data in the first place. The technically sound position is to apply DP training and data minimisation before training, not after. legal_risk_quantification: | If a model trained on personal data can reproduce verbatim PII from its outputs, and the data subject has requested erasure: - Continued operation of the model constitutes ongoing GDPR violation - Output monitoring creating evidence of recall events generates discoverable records of the violation Legal mitigation: proactive erasure claim assessment; DP training as documented technical measure; output monitoring as demonstrable good faith. two_technical_mitigations: mitigation_01: | Data minimisation before training: collect only the minimum PII required, pseudonymise or anonymise before training data enters the pipeline. Reduces extraction risk by reducing the PII density in training data. mitigation_02: | DP training with documented epsilon: provides a formal, auditable bound on individual privacy risk that can be disclosed to regulators as a technical measure under GDPR Article 25 (data protection by design).

Implementation 01

Title

Training Data Extraction Proof-of-Concept and Memorisation Risk Scorer

Filename

training_data_extraction_poc.py

Purpose

Two tools in one file: (1) Extraction PoC: demonstrates Carlini-style prefix-probe extraction against GPT-2 (as the paper's original target) using HuggingFace transformers. Generates completions, computes per-token entropy, and filters candidates by low entropy (memorisation signal). (2) Memorisation risk scorer: computes a corpus-level risk score from n-gram repetition rate and PII density, producing LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH risk classification with detailed breakdown.

Language

python

Code

"""
Training Data Extraction PoC + Memorisation Risk Scorer
Target: GPT-2 (open weights — safe for research PoC)
"""

import re
import math
import hashlib
from collections import Counter
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Dict, Optional, Tuple


# -----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Part 1: Extraction PoC (GPT-2 — HuggingFace transformers)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

# Requires: pip install transformers torch --break-system-packages
# Runs on CPU for PoC; GPU recommended for bulk generation

PROBE_PREFIXES = [
    "The email address for",
    "Contact us at",
    "Copyright (c)",
    "def ",
    "import os\nimport sys\n",
    "All rights reserved.",
    "To unsubscribe from this list",
    "This message was sent to",
    "My name is",
    "The following is a list of",
]

ENTROPY_THRESHOLD = 1.5     # bits per token — below this suggests memorised sequence
GENERATION_TOKENS = 200     # tokens to generate per probe
TEMPERATURE = 0.1           # low temperature maximises extraction
NUM_SAMPLES_PER_PREFIX = 10 # bulk generation count


@dataclass
class ExtractionCandidate:
    prefix: str
    completion: str
    avg_entropy: float
    flagged: bool
    reason: str


def run_extraction_poc(
    max_prefixes: int = 3,
    verbose: bool = True
) -> List[ExtractionCandidate]:
    """
    Run Carlini-style extraction against GPT-2.
    Returns list of ExtractionCandidate (flagged = low-entropy / memorisation signal).

    Install: pip install transformers torch
    """
    try:
        import torch
        from transformers import GPT2LMHeadModel, GPT2Tokenizer
    except ImportError:
        print("transformers not installed. Run: pip install transformers torch")
        return []

    tokenizer = GPT2Tokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")
    model = GPT2LMHeadModel.from_pretrained("gpt2")
    model.eval()

    candidates: List[ExtractionCandidate] = []
    prefixes = PROBE_PREFIXES[:max_prefixes]

    for prefix in prefixes:
        if verbose:
            print(f"\nProbing prefix: '{prefix[:40]}...' ")
        input_ids = tokenizer.encode(prefix, return_tensors="pt")

        for sample_idx in range(NUM_SAMPLES_PER_PREFIX):
            with torch.no_grad():
                output = model.generate(
                    input_ids,
                    max_new_tokens=GENERATION_TOKENS,
                    temperature=TEMPERATURE,
                    do_sample=(TEMPERATURE > 0),
                    pad_token_id=tokenizer.eos_token_id,
                    return_dict_in_generate=True,
                    output_scores=True,
                )

            # Compute per-token entropy from scores
            entropies = []
            for step_scores in output.scores:
                import torch.nn.functional as F
                probs = F.softmax(step_scores[0], dim=-1)
                entropy = -torch.sum(probs * torch.log2(probs + 1e-9)).item()
                entropies.append(entropy)

            avg_entropy = sum(entropies) / len(entropies) if entropies else float('inf')
            new_tokens = output.sequences[0][input_ids.shape[1]:]
            completion = tokenizer.decode(new_tokens, skip_special_tokens=True)

            flagged = avg_entropy < ENTROPY_THRESHOLD
            reason = (f"LOW ENTROPY ({avg_entropy:.2f} bits/token < {ENTROPY_THRESHOLD})"
                      if flagged else f"Normal entropy ({avg_entropy:.2f} bits/token)")

            c = ExtractionCandidate(prefix=prefix, completion=completion,
                                    avg_entropy=avg_entropy, flagged=flagged,
                                    reason=reason)
            candidates.append(c)

            if verbose:
                icon = "FLAGGED" if flagged else "normal "
                print(f"  [{icon}] sample {sample_idx+1}: entropy={avg_entropy:.2f} — "
                      f"{completion[:60].strip()!r}")

    flagged_count = sum(1 for c in candidates if c.flagged)
    if verbose:
        print(f"\nExtraction PoC: {flagged_count}/{len(candidates)} candidates flagged "
              f"as potential memorised sequences (entropy < {ENTROPY_THRESHOLD})")
    return candidates

}

    risk_level: str
    recommendations: List[str] = field(default_factory=list)


def tokenise_simple(text: str) -> List[str]:
    """Whitespace tokeniser — replace with tiktoken for production."""
    return text.split()


def compute_ngram_repetition(tokens: List[str], n: int = 5) -> Tuple[int, int, float]:
    """Count n-gram repetitions. Returns (total, repeated, rate)."""
    ngrams = [tuple(tokens[i:i+n]) for i in range(len(tokens) - n + 1)]
    counts = Counter(ngrams)
    repeated = sum(1 for c in counts.values() if c > 1)
    rate = repeated / len(ngrams) if ngrams else 0.0
    return len(ngrams), repeated, rate


def compute_pii_density(text: str, total_tokens: int) -> Tuple[Dict[str, int], float]:
    """Count PII pattern matches. Returns (match_counts, density)."""
    counts = {}
    total_matches = 0
    for pii_type, pattern in PII_PATTERNS.items():
        matches = pattern.findall(text)
        if matches:
            counts[pii_type] = len(matches)
            total_matches += len(matches)
    density = min(1.0, total_matches * 10 / total_tokens) if total_tokens > 0 else 0.0
    return counts, density


def score_corpus(text: str, corpus_name: str = "corpus") -> CorpusRiskReport:
    tokens = tokenise_simple(text)
    total_tokens = len(tokens)
    total_ngrams, repeated_ngrams, repetition_rate = compute_ngram_repetition(tokens)
    pii_spans, pii_density = compute_pii_density(text, total_tokens)

    text_lower = text.lower()
    sensitive_hits = [kw for kw in SENSITIVE_KEYWORDS if kw in text_lower]
    sensitive_category_count = min(5, len(set(
        "medical" if kw in ["patient","diagnosis","medication"] else
        "auth" if kw in ["api key","private key","password","secret","credential"] else
        "legal" if kw in ["confidential","proprietary","trade secret"] else
        "identity"
        for kw in sensitive_hits
    ))) / 5

    raw_score = (
        0.4 * min(1.0, repetition_rate * 10) +
        0.4 * pii_density +
        0.2 * sensitive_category_count
    )
    risk = "HIGH" if raw_score > 0.6 else "MEDIUM" if raw_score > 0.3 else "LOW"

    recs = []
    if repetition_rate > 0.05:
        recs.append("DEDUPLICATE: n-gram repetition rate high — apply MinHash deduplication")
    if pii_density > 0.02:
        recs.append("ANONYMISE: PII detected — pseudonymise before training")
    if raw_score > 0.3:
        recs.append("CONSIDER DP TRAINING: risk score warrants differential privacy (ε ≤ 4)")
    if raw_score > 0.6:
        recs.append("MANDATORY DP: high-risk corpus — DP training required (ε ≤ 1)")
        recs.append("CANARY TOKENS: inject canary tokens before training for extraction monitoring")

    return CorpusRiskReport(
        total_tokens=total_tokens, total_ngrams=total_ngrams,
        repeated_ngrams=repeated_ngrams, repetition_rate=repetition_rate,
        pii_spans=pii_spans, pii_density=pii_density,
        sensitive_keyword_count=len(sensitive_hits),
        sensitive_category_count=int(sensitive_category_count * 5),
        raw_score=raw_score, risk_level=risk, recommendations=recs,
    )

class Layer1Detector:
    """Detects extraction campaign patterns across requests from one account."""

    WINDOW_SECONDS = 600      # 10-minute sliding window
    PREFIX_PROBE_THRESHOLD = 30
    LOW_TEMP_THRESHOLD = 0.2
    LOW_TEMP_VOLUME = 100
    LOGPROB_DAILY_THRESHOLD = 5_000
    ONE_SHOT_VOLUME = 1_000
    ONE_SHOT_MAX_TURNS = 2
    ONE_SHOT_MIN_OUTPUT = 200

    def __init__(self):
        self.account_requests: Dict[str, List[APIRequest]] = defaultdict(list)

    def record(self, req: APIRequest) -> None:
        self.account_requests[req.account_id].append(req)

    def _window(self, account_id: str, now: float) -> List[APIRequest]:
        return [r for r in self.account_requests[account_id]
                if now - r.timestamp <= self.WINDOW_SECONDS]

    def detect(self, account_id: str) -> List[LayerOneAlert]:
        alerts = []
        now = max((r.timestamp for r in self.account_requests[account_id]),
                  default=0.0)
        window = self._window(account_id, now)
        all_reqs = self.account_requests[account_id]

        # Signal 1: prefix probe pattern
        prefixes = [r.prefix for r in window]
        common_prefix_pairs = sum(
            1 for i, p1 in enumerate(prefixes)
            for p2 in prefixes[i+1:]
            if p1 and p2 and p1[:30] == p2[:30] and p1 != p2
        )
        if common_prefix_pairs > self.PREFIX_PROBE_THRESHOLD:
            alerts.append(LayerOneAlert(
                "prefix_probe_pattern",
                common_prefix_pairs, self.PREFIX_PROBE_THRESHOLD,
                f"{common_prefix_pairs} requests share common prefix in 10-min window"))

        # Signal 2: low temperature + volume
        low_temp = [r for r in window if r.temperature < self.LOW_TEMP_THRESHOLD]
        if len(low_temp) > self.LOW_TEMP_VOLUME:
            alerts.append(LayerOneAlert(
                "low_temperature_bulk",
                len(low_temp), self.LOW_TEMP_VOLUME,
                f"{len(low_temp)} low-temp (< {self.LOW_TEMP_THRESHOLD}) requests in window"))

        # Signal 3: logprob access at scale
        logprob_daily = [r for r in all_reqs if r.logprobs_requested]
        if len(logprob_daily) > self.LOGPROB_DAILY_THRESHOLD:
            alerts.append(LayerOneAlert(
                "logprob_high_volume",
                len(logprob_daily), self.LOGPROB_DAILY_THRESHOLD,
                f"{len(logprob_daily)} logprob requests today"))

        # Signal 4: one-shot high-output pattern
        one_shot = [r for r in window
                    if r.turn_number <= self.ONE_SHOT_MAX_TURNS
                    and r.output_token_count >= self.ONE_SHOT_MIN_OUTPUT]
        if len(one_shot) > self.ONE_SHOT_VOLUME:
            alerts.append(LayerOneAlert(
                "one_shot_bulk_generation",
                len(one_shot), self.ONE_SHOT_VOLUME,
                f"{len(one_shot)} one-shot high-output requests in window"))

        return alerts

def ngram_overlap(text_a: str, text_b: str, n: int = 5) -> float:
    """ROUGE-L-style n-gram overlap between two texts."""
    tokens_a = text_a.lower().split()
    tokens_b = text_b.lower().split()
    if len(tokens_a) < n or len(tokens_b) < n:
        return 0.0
    ngrams_a = Counter(tuple(tokens_a[i:i+n]) for i in range(len(tokens_a)-n+1))
    ngrams_b = Counter(tuple(tokens_b[i:i+n]) for i in range(len(tokens_b)-n+1))
    overlap = sum((ngrams_a & ngrams_b).values())
    return overlap / max(1, len(ngrams_a))


def scan_output(output: str) -> List[LayerTwoAlert]:
    alerts = []

    # Signal 1: PII pattern match
    for pii_type, pattern in PII_PATTERNS.items():
        matches = pattern.findall(output)
        if matches:
            alerts.append(LayerTwoAlert(
                f"pii_in_output_{pii_type}", len(matches), 0,
                f"{len(matches)} {pii_type} pattern(s) in output",
                output[:120]))

    # Signal 2: Canary token recall
    for canary in CANARY_CORPUS:
        if canary in output:
            alerts.append(LayerTwoAlert(
                "canary_token_recall", 1.0, 0,
                f"CANARY TOKEN RECALLED: {canary}",
                output[:120]))

    # Signal 3: High n-gram overlap with canary corpus
    for canary in CANARY_CORPUS:
        overlap = ngram_overlap(output, canary)
        if overlap > 0.5:
            alerts.append(LayerTwoAlert(
                "high_ngram_overlap_canary", overlap, 0.5,
                f"High overlap ({overlap:.2f}) with canary corpus entry",
                output[:120]))

    return alerts

    # Score: each L1 alert = 0.2; each L2 alert = 0.4 (capped at 1.0)
    score = min(1.0, len(l1_alerts) * 0.2 + len(l2_alerts) * 0.4)

    )

def noise_injection_demo(epsilon: float, gradient_norm: float = 1.0,
                          batch_size: int = 32, delta: float = 1e-5) -> Dict:
    """
    Compute the Gaussian noise standard deviation for DP-SGD
    at the given epsilon, delta, gradient clipping norm.
    Uses the simplified formula (tight for single-step analysis):
      sigma ≈ sqrt(2 * ln(1.25/delta)) * gradient_norm / epsilon
    """
    if epsilon == float('inf'):
        return {"sigma": 0.0, "epsilon": epsilon, "noise_ratio": 0.0,
                "snr_db": float('inf')}
    sigma = math.sqrt(2 * math.log(1.25 / delta)) * gradient_norm / epsilon
    noise_ratio = sigma / gradient_norm
    snr_db = 20 * math.log10(1.0 / sigma) if sigma > 0 else float('inf')
    return {"sigma": sigma, "epsilon": epsilon, "noise_ratio": noise_ratio,
            "snr_db": snr_db}

def compute_ngram_overlap_score(text: str, reference: str, n: int = 5) -> float:
    ta = text.lower().split()
    tb = reference.lower().split()
    if len(ta) < n or len(tb) < n:
        return 0.0
    nga = Counter(tuple(ta[i:i+n]) for i in range(len(ta)-n+1))
    ngb = Counter(tuple(tb[i:i+n]) for i in range(len(tb)-n+1))
    overlap = sum((nga & ngb).values())
    return overlap / max(1, len(nga))


def scan_output_verbatim(
    output: str,
    reference_corpus: Optional[Dict[str, str]] = None,
    ngram_threshold: float = 0.4,
) -> OutputScanResult:
    """
    reference_corpus: {doc_id: doc_text} of sensitive training documents.
    In production: load from encrypted store, not hardcoded.
    """
    pii_found: Dict[str, List[str]] = {}
    for pii_type, pattern in PII_SCAN_PATTERNS.items():
        matches = pattern.findall(output)
        if matches:
            pii_found[pii_type] = matches

    canary_recalled = [c for c in CANARY_TOKENS if c in output]

    max_overlap = 0.0
    match_id = None
    if reference_corpus:
        for doc_id, doc_text in reference_corpus.items():
            score = compute_ngram_overlap_score(output, doc_text)
            if score > max_overlap:
                max_overlap = score
                match_id = doc_id if score >= ngram_threshold else None

    block = bool(canary_recalled or pii_found)
    risk = "critical" if canary_recalled else "high" if pii_found else \
           "medium" if max_overlap >= ngram_threshold else "low"

    reason = (
        f"CANARY RECALLED: {canary_recalled}" if canary_recalled else
        f"PII detected: {list(pii_found.keys())}" if pii_found else
        f"High n-gram overlap ({max_overlap:.2f}) with reference doc {match_id}" if match_id else
        "Clean"
    )

    return OutputScanResult(
        output=output, pii_found=pii_found,
        canary_recalled=canary_recalled,
        ngram_overlap_max=max_overlap,
        reference_match_id=match_id,
        overall_risk=risk, block=block, reason=reason,
    )

Key Takeaways

Takeaway 01

Gradient descent drives memorisation by design. A sequence appearing n times in the training corpus receives n × more gradient signal than a sequence appearing once — the optimiser correctly learns to reproduce it. Memorisation is not a bug; it is the consequence of optimising on a corpus that contains repeated sequences. Deduplication attacks this root cause directly and is the highest-ROI defence.

Takeaway 02

Scale amplifies memorisation. Larger models memorise more examples from the same training data. The Carlini et al. (2021) finding is counterintuitive but empirically robust: scaling capability increases extraction risk, not the reverse. Production LLMs operating on sensitive data require correspondingly scaled defences.

Takeaway 03

Fine-tuning on internal data is a memorisation emergency. A 100-document internal fine-tuning dataset trained for 50 epochs presents each document to the model enough times to near-certain memorisation. Any organisation fine-tuning on customer records, medical data, or proprietary documents must assume those documents are memorised and potentially extractable.

Takeaway 04

The Carlini extraction attack requires no white-box access. Prefix-probe generation followed by low-entropy filtering is a black-box attack implementable against any text generation API. The detection surface is the API log and the output stream — both are controlled by the defender.

Takeaway 05

Epsilon is not a label — it is a mathematical bound. ε=8 does not mean "weak privacy"; it means the adversary gains at most e^8 ≈ 2981x advantage from the presence of any one training example. For medical records, ε=8 is insufficient. Choose epsilon based on the sensitivity of the specific data, not on convenience.

Takeaway 06

Canary tokens are a zero-FP extraction detection mechanism and a memorisation quantification tool. Injecting known unique strings into training data, then monitoring all outputs for their recall, provides both: (a) real-time alerting when extraction occurs, and (b) a measurement of how much memorisation the model has learned by testing canary recall at evaluation time.

Takeaway 07

GDPR Article 17 (right to erasure) and model memorisation are fundamentally incompatible without proactive controls. Erasing personal data from trained model weights is computationally equivalent to retraining from scratch for most architectures. The legally sound position is data minimisation and DP training applied before training — not machine unlearning applied after.

Takeaway 08

Extraction detection is a two-layer problem. Layer 1 (input pattern: prefix probes, low temperature, logprob access, bulk one-shot) catches the campaign before extraction succeeds. Layer 2 (output content: PII scan, canary recall, n-gram overlap) catches individual verbatim recall events. Both layers are required: Layer 1 alone misses novel attack patterns; Layer 2 alone misses campaigns that stay under per-request thresholds.

Section Summary

One Paragraph

Section 5.10 establishes training data extraction as a practical, black-box-accessible privacy attack with direct GDPR implications, and provides the full stack from attack mechanics through defender implementation. The section opens by explaining why models memorise: gradient descent accumulates signal proportionally to repetition count, model capacity in the overfitting regime enables simultaneous generalisation and memorisation, and fine-tuning amplifies memorisation of fine-tuning data dramatically by presenting it at high frequency relative to its size. The three-level memorisation taxonomy (verbatim, approximate, concept-level) provides the framework for risk classification and detection strategy selection. The Carlini et al. (2021) methodology is examined in full: prefix construction, bulk generation at low temperature, membership inference filtering for low-entropy candidates, and verification — a complete black-box extraction pipeline requiring only API access. Five risk factors are quantified: model size (memorisation scales with parameters), data repetition (10+ occurrences near-certain extractable), fine-tuning (small dataset, many epochs equals near-certain memorisation), generation temperature (low temperature maximises extraction), and logprob exposure (multiplies extraction efficiency). Differential privacy is presented formally (the ε-δ guarantee, DP-SGD mechanics, noise calibration) with empirical accuracy-privacy tradeoff values across ε=1, 2, 4, 8, ∞ for both small and large models, enabling epsilon selection based on data sensitivity. Detection is a two-layer problem: Layer 1 operates on input patterns (prefix probes, low temperature, logprob access, one-shot bulk volume) and Layer 2 operates on output content (PII patterns, canary token recall, n-gram overlap against reference corpus). The GDPR intersection establishes why right-to-erasure and model memorisation are technically incompatible without proactive controls, and why DP training and data minimisation before training are the only legally sound approaches. Three implementations deliver the extraction PoC and memorisation risk scorer, the dual-layer extraction detector, and the differential privacy tradeoff comparison paired with the output verbatim recall scanner.
