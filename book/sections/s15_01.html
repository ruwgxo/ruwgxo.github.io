Chapter 15 · Section 1 · ~6 pages

Production Detector Taxonomy: From Deployed Gateway to Detector Suite

Section Overview

One Line Summary

A deployed gateway and a populated gateway are different things: Part 3 built and deployed the gateway, and Part 4 ships the detectors that run in it. Section 15.01 is the map of that suite -- six hybrid detectors, one unified API, and one quality bar -- so every following section builds a named detector against a shared contract rather than a standalone tool.

Why This Section Exists

Part 3 ended with the production detection gateway: the behavioural monitoring architecture of Chapter 13 engineered for latency, failure, availability, observability, and cost, deployed as a running service. That gateway is deployed and empty. It has the hooks where detection runs, the latency budget detection must fit, and the failure policy detection runs under, but it does not yet contain the detectors themselves. Part 4 fills it, and Chapter 15 builds the core suite. This section is the anchor for the chapter, mirroring the taxonomy anchors of 12.01, 13.01, and 14.01. It makes three commitments that every following section honours. First, the suite is six detectors -- injection, jailbreak, PII, toxicity, exfiltration, and the combined pipeline -- each mapped to a detection concept from Chapters 12 and 13 made concrete. Second, every detector is a hybrid of pattern matching and ML, the Chapter 11 layered-detection principle applied inside a single detector. Third, every detector implements one unified API and is held to one quality bar, so the six become a coherent, configurable, tested suite rather than a pile of scripts. State these once here and the rest of the chapter is a deliberate build against them.

Position In Chapter

Section 15.01 is the taxonomy anchor for the seven sections that follow. It is the map that makes each detector section (15.02-15.06) a response to a named entry in the suite, makes the pipeline section (15.07) the orchestration of a contract defined here, and makes the summary (15.08) a synthesis of a structure set out here. The unified API contract and the quality bar introduced in this section are the two artifacts every subsequent section depends on.

Prose Content

Part 1

Title

A Deployed Gateway Is Empty: The Two-Artifact Argument, Continued

Body

Chapter 14 ended with a running service. The production detection gateway takes the behavioural monitoring architecture of Chapter 13 and engineers it against the deployment concerns -- placement, latency, failure handling, availability, observability, cost -- so that a correct design becomes a service that survives production. It has pre-processing and post-processing hooks where detection runs. It has a latency budget that detection must fit inside. It has a failure policy that governs what happens when a detector throws. It is deployed, monitored, and affordable. It is also empty. The gateway is the place detection runs, not the detection itself. Its hooks are wired to nothing yet; its latency budget is spent by no detector; its failure policy protects against the failure of detectors that have not been written. Chapter 14 answered where and under what conditions detection runs. It did not build a single detector. That is Part 4, and this chapter is where the core suite gets built. This mirrors the two-artifact argument that opened Chapter 14, one level down. There the claim was that a logical architecture and a production service are different artifacts. Here the claim is that a deployed gateway and a populated gateway are different artifacts. A deployed gateway is complete as infrastructure and empty as a detection capability. Part 4 moves the artifact from deployed-and-empty to populated-and-detecting, and Chapter 15 builds the six detectors that do the populating. Everything in this chapter drops into the hooks Chapter 14 built, fits the budget Chapter 14 set, and runs under the policy Chapter 14 defined.

Part 2

Title

The Six-Detector Suite and the Concept Each Makes Concrete

Body

The suite is six detectors. Five are content detectors and the sixth is the pipeline that orchestrates them, but the framing that matters is that each of the five makes concrete a detection concept that earlier chapters established in the abstract. The prompt injection detector (15.02) makes concrete the Chapter 12 injection classifier, wrapping it in a regex layer for the known instruction-override phrasings. The jailbreak detector (15.03) makes concrete the Chapter 12 jailbreak classifier and draws on the Chapter 13 session signal for multi-turn attempts, wrapped in a pattern layer for the known templates. The PII scanner (15.04) extends the Chapter 12 Section 12.09 output PII scanning into a two-sided scanner that combines regex for structured PII with NER for unstructured entities. The toxicity detector (15.05) combines a hosted classification baseline with custom models for the domain-specific toxicity the hosted service misses. The exfiltration detector (15.06) turns the Chapter 13 output-monitoring and canary signals of Section 13.06 into an output-analysis detector, combined with the Chapter 13 aggregate rate and coverage signals for the campaign. The point of this mapping is that Chapter 15 introduces almost no new detection theory. The theory is in Chapters 11 through 13. This chapter is the concretion step: it takes the classifiers, signals, and principles those chapters built and ships them as running, tested, production-grade code. A reader who has followed the detection chapters will recognise every component here; what is new is that the components are now assembled into detectors that meet a bar and share a contract.

Part 3

Title

Every Detector Is a Hybrid: Patterns and ML Inside One Detector

Body

The recurring structure of the suite is the hybrid. Every content detector has a pattern layer and an ML layer, and the two combine into one verdict. This is not an implementation accident; it is the central design claim of the chapter, and it is the Chapter 11 layered-detection principle applied inside a single detector rather than across a stack of them. The two layers exist because each covers what the other misses. The pattern layer -- regex, signatures, known templates -- catches the well-known attacks with high precision and near-zero latency. When an input contains a literal instruction-override phrasing, a signature match is faster and more certain than any model inference. But the pattern layer is brittle: it catches only what it has a pattern for, and an attacker who paraphrases slips past it. The ML layer covers exactly that gap. A trained classifier generalises to the novel, paraphrased, and disguised attempts the patterns never anticipated, at the cost of higher latency and lower precision on any single decision. A detector that used only patterns would be fast and precise and blind to novel attacks. A detector that used only ML would cover novel attacks and be slower and noisier on the known ones a signature would have caught cleanly. The hybrid takes the pattern layer as a high-confidence fast path and the ML layer as the coverage net beneath it, combining them into one verdict. Every detector in this chapter is built this way, and the reason is always the same: patterns give precision on the known, ML gives coverage of the unknown, and a production detector needs both.

Part 4

Title

The Unified API: What Makes Six Detectors One Suite

Body

Six detectors are not a suite until they share a contract. Without one, the pipeline that combines them has to know the shape of each detector individually, configuration has to be written per detector, and the test framework has to special-case every one. With one, the pipeline treats all six identically, configuration and testing apply uniformly, and adding a seventh detector is a matter of implementing the contract. The unified API is what turns a collection into a system. The contract this chapter uses is deliberately small. Every detector implements a single method, detect, that takes a DetectionContext and returns a DetectorResult. The DetectionContext carries the text under inspection, whether it is an input or an output, the request and session identifiers, and a metadata bag. The DetectorResult carries a verdict -- allow, flag, or block -- a score, a confidence, the latency the detector took, and a list of evidence items explaining what fired. That is the whole interface: one method, one input type, one output type. The small size is the point. Because every detector returns the same DetectorResult, the combined pipeline of Section 15.07 can call detect on each detector, collect the results, and combine the verdicts without knowing anything about how any individual detector reached its answer. The pattern-plus-ML internals of each detector are hidden behind the contract. The verdict enum, the score, and the evidence list are rich enough to drive orchestration and explanation, and uniform enough that orchestration never branches on detector type. This contract is defined in this section precisely because everything after it depends on it.

Part 5

Title

The Quality Bar: Three Targets Held Together

Body

A production detector is defined not only by what it detects but by how well it runs, and the quality bar states that as three concrete targets: 95%+ accuracy, sub-50ms latency, and a sub-5% false-positive rate. The bar is what separates a production detector from a proof of concept, and the three targets are held together, not traded against one another. Accuracy is the obvious one: a detector that is wrong more than one time in twenty does not earn trust. Latency is the Chapter 14 budget made a detector requirement: the gateway has a sub-50ms inline budget, and a detector that blows it either forces the gateway over its own budget or gets moved off the hot path where it can no longer block. The false-positive rate is the one most often neglected and the one that most reliably gets a detector disabled. A detector that flags too many legitimate requests disrupts real use, and an operations team under pressure will turn it off regardless of how many true attacks it catches. That is why the false-positive rate is a first-class metric here and not a footnote to accuracy. The three are held together because a detector that hits two and misses one is not production-grade. Accurate but slow gets moved off the hot path. Accurate and fast but noisy gets disabled. Fast and quiet but inaccurate misses attacks. Only a detector that meets all three at once ships and stays shipped. The false-positive target bites hardest on the PII and toxicity detectors, where the material -- names, place words, context-dependent language -- is exactly the kind that a careless detector over-flags, and where over-flagging is most disruptive to legitimate use.

Title

The Chapter Roadmap: Five Detectors, Then the Pipeline

Body

The rest of Chapter 15 follows the taxonomy directly. The five content detectors come first, each in its own section, each built as a pattern-plus-ML hybrid against the unified API and the quality bar. Section 15.02 builds the prompt injection detector, 15.03 the jailbreak detector, 15.04 the PII scanner, 15.05 the toxicity detector, and 15.06 the exfiltration detector. Each section is self-contained: it names the detection concept it makes concrete, builds the pattern layer and the ML layer, combines them into one verdict, validates against a labelled corpus, and confirms the quality bar. Section 15.07 then orchestrates the five into the combined detection pipeline. Because every detector implements the unified API, the pipeline runs them uniformly -- in parallel where latency demands it, in sequence where one gates another -- combines their verdicts into one pipeline verdict, and places into the Part 3 gateway as the detector suite that gateway deploys. The same section adds configuration management and a testing framework that apply across the whole suite, so the six are configured and tested as one system. Section 15.08 synthesises the chapter and previews Chapter 16, where the deployed suite is integrated with SIEM platforms so its detections flow into security operations tooling. Read the roadmap as a single arc. The taxonomy in this section is the destination in miniature: six detectors, one contract, one bar. Every following section fills in one part of that picture, and by the end of the chapter the deployed-but-empty gateway of Chapter 14 is a populated, detecting, tested production system.

Title

The Unified Detector API Contract

Filename

detector_api_contract.py

Description

The one contract every detector in the suite implements: a Detector base class with a single detect method taking a DetectionContext and returning a DetectorResult with verdict, score, confidence, latency_ms, and evidence. This is the artifact the combined pipeline of Section 15.07 orchestrates against and the shape every detector section 15.02-15.06 implements.

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention, abc and dataclasses from the standard library only. Verdict is a three-value enum (allow, flag, block). A small _Timed helper shows how detectors populate latency_ms so the quality bar can be measured. ExampleAllowAll is a reference implementation of the contract shape, not a working detector.

Code

# detector_api_contract.py
# Section 15.01: The Unified Detector API Contract
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 15 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention
#
# The one contract every detector in the suite implements. Because all six
# share this shape, the combined pipeline (15.07) orchestrates them uniformly
# and configuration and testing apply across the suite.

from abc import ABC, abstractmethod
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from enum import Enum
from typing import Any, Dict, List, Optional
import time


class Verdict(Enum):
    ALLOW = 'allow'          # nothing detected
    FLAG = 'flag'            # suspicious, surface for review, do not block
    BLOCK = 'block'          # high-confidence detection, block the request


@dataclass
class DetectionContext:
    # Everything a detector needs to make one decision.
    request_id: str
    text: str                                   # the input or output under inspection
    surface: str = 'input'                      # 'input' or 'output'
    session_id: Optional[str] = None            # for detectors that use session state
    metadata: Dict[str, Any] = field(default_factory=dict)


@dataclass
class Evidence:
    # One piece of support for a verdict -- a rule hit or a model score.
    source: str                                 # 'pattern' or 'ml' or 'aggregate'
    label: str                                  # what fired, e.g. 'instruction_override'
    detail: str = ''                            # human-readable context


@dataclass
class DetectorResult:
    # The single return type of every detector in the suite.
    detector: str                               # detector name, e.g. 'prompt_injection'
    verdict: Verdict
    score: float                                # 0.0-1.0 detection strength
    confidence: float                           # 0.0-1.0 confidence in the score
    latency_ms: float                           # wall-clock time this detector took
    evidence: List[Evidence] = field(default_factory=list)


class Detector(ABC):
    # The unified API. One method, one return type, for all six detectors.

    name: str = 'detector'

    @abstractmethod
    def detect(self, ctx: DetectionContext) -> DetectorResult:
        # Inspect ctx and return a DetectorResult. Must set latency_ms.
        raise NotImplementedError


class _Timed:
    # Helper: detectors wrap their body in this to populate latency_ms.

    def __enter__(self):
        self._start = time.perf_counter()
        return self

    def __exit__(self, *exc):
        self.elapsed_ms = (time.perf_counter() - self._start) * 1000.0
        return False


class ExampleAllowAll(Detector):
    # Reference implementation showing the contract shape only.

    name = 'example'

    def detect(self, ctx: DetectionContext) -> DetectorResult:
        with _Timed() as t:
            pass  # a real detector runs its pattern layer then its ML layer here
        return DetectorResult(
            detector=self.name,
            verdict=Verdict.ALLOW,
            score=0.0,
            confidence=1.0,
            latency_ms=t.elapsed_ms,
            evidence=[],
        )


if __name__ == '__main__':
    d = ExampleAllowAll()
    r = d.detect(DetectionContext(request_id='r1', text='hello'))
    print(r.detector, r.verdict.value, r.score, f'{r.latency_ms:.3f}ms')

Deliverable 3

Title

The Quality Bar

Filename

quality_bar.py

Description

The quality bar as code: a frozen QualityBar with the three thresholds, a DetectorMetrics record, and meets_bar and explain functions that hold a detector to all three targets at once and name which it fails. Every detector section validates against this.

Implementation Notes

Python 3.12, single quotes throughout per book convention, dataclasses from the standard library only. The default QualityBar encodes 95%+ accuracy, sub-50ms p99 latency, and sub-5% false-positive rate. explain returns the specific target(s) missed so a failing detector produces an actionable message rather than a bare boolean.

Code

# quality_bar.py
# Section 15.01: The Quality Bar
# 20260121 | AI Security Mastery -- Chapter 15 by Raghav Dinesh
# Single quotes throughout per book convention
#
# The concrete bar that separates a production detector from a proof of
# concept: 95%+ accuracy, sub-50ms latency, sub-5% false-positive rate. A
# detector that misses any one of the three is not production-grade.

from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class QualityBar:
    min_accuracy: float = 0.95          # 95%+ correct verdicts
    max_latency_ms: float = 50.0        # sub-50ms per detector (Chapter 14.04 budget)
    max_false_positive_rate: float = 0.05   # sub-5% of benign flagged


@dataclass
class DetectorMetrics:
    accuracy: float
    p99_latency_ms: float
    false_positive_rate: float


def meets_bar(m: DetectorMetrics, bar: QualityBar = QualityBar()) -> bool:
    return (
        m.accuracy >= bar.min_accuracy
        and m.p99_latency_ms <= bar.max_latency_ms
        and m.false_positive_rate <= bar.max_false_positive_rate
    )


def explain(m: DetectorMetrics, bar: QualityBar = QualityBar()) -> str:
    reasons = []
    if m.accuracy < bar.min_accuracy:
        reasons.append(f'accuracy {m.accuracy:.2%} below {bar.min_accuracy:.0%}')
    if m.p99_latency_ms > bar.max_latency_ms:
        reasons.append(f'p99 {m.p99_latency_ms:.1f}ms over {bar.max_latency_ms:.0f}ms')
    if m.false_positive_rate > bar.max_false_positive_rate:
        reasons.append(f'FP {m.false_positive_rate:.2%} over {bar.max_false_positive_rate:.0%}')
    return 'meets the bar' if not reasons else 'fails: ' + '; '.join(reasons)


if __name__ == '__main__':
    good = DetectorMetrics(accuracy=0.97, p99_latency_ms=42.0, false_positive_rate=0.03)
    noisy = DetectorMetrics(accuracy=0.99, p99_latency_ms=18.0, false_positive_rate=0.11)
    print('good :', explain(good))
    print('noisy:', explain(noisy))

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1

Description

Treating the Chapter 14 deployed gateway as a working detection system and expecting it to catch attacks before any detector is built

Why It Happens

The deployed gateway is a running service with hooks, a latency budget, a failure policy, and observability, so it reads as complete. The distinction between the place detection runs and the detection itself is easy to lose once the infrastructure is up and green.

Consequence

The gateway is deployed and passes health checks while catching nothing, because its hooks are wired to no detector. The gap is discovered only when a real attack passes through untouched.

Correct Approach

Treat the deployed gateway as 100% complete as infrastructure and empty as a detection capability, and build the six detectors of Chapter 15 to populate it. The gateway detects nothing until the suite runs in its hooks.

Mistake 2

Description

Building each detector as pattern-only or ML-only rather than a hybrid, choosing one layer for simplicity

Why It Happens

A single-layer detector is simpler to build and reason about. Pattern-only feels precise and fast; ML-only feels comprehensive. Each looks adequate against the attacks the builder happens to test.

Consequence

The pattern-only detector is blind to any paraphrase of a known attack; the ML-only detector is slower and noisier on the known attacks a signature would have caught cleanly, and often misses the latency bar. Either way the detector fails on the half of the threat surface its single layer does not cover.

Correct Approach

Build every content detector as a hybrid: a pattern layer for precision and near-zero latency on the known, an ML layer for coverage of the novel, combined into one verdict. This is the Chapter 11 layered-detection principle applied inside a single detector.

Mistake 3

Description

Optimising a detector for accuracy alone and ignoring latency and false positives until deployment

Why It Happens

Accuracy is the metric detection work naturally centres on, and it is the one reported in papers and benchmarks. Latency and false positives feel like operational details to handle later.

Consequence

An accurate but slow detector gets moved off the hot path where it can no longer block; an accurate but noisy detector gets disabled by an operations team it disrupts. In both cases a detector that scored well on accuracy stops protecting anything.

Correct Approach

Hold every detector to the full quality bar -- accuracy, latency, and false-positive rate together -- from the start. A detector that misses any one of the three is not production-grade, and the false-positive rate is a first-class target, not a footnote to accuracy.

Mistake 4

Description

Giving each detector its own bespoke interface and return shape rather than a shared unified API

Why It Happens

Each detector is built in its own section and it is natural to return whatever shape is convenient for that detector -- a boolean here, a score there, a custom object elsewhere. The cost of divergence is not visible until the pipeline tries to combine them.

Consequence

The combined pipeline has to special-case every detector, configuration is written per detector, and the test framework cannot run uniformly. Six detectors stay six tools instead of becoming one suite, and adding a seventh means rewriting the orchestration.

Correct Approach

Define the unified API contract once -- detect(ctx) -> DetectorResult -- and implement it in every detector. The shared contract is what lets the pipeline orchestrate uniformly and lets configuration and testing apply across the suite.

Mistake 5

Description

Reinventing detection theory in Chapter 15 instead of making concrete the classifiers and signals already built in Chapters 11 through 13

Why It Happens

Starting the implementation chapter can feel like a fresh start, and it is tempting to design new detection approaches rather than wire up the existing ones. The earlier chapters can fade from view once code-writing begins.

Consequence

Effort goes into re-deriving detection approaches that Chapters 12 and 13 already established and validated, the new approaches are untested against the corpora the earlier work used, and the chapter drifts from concretion into unplanned research.

Correct Approach

Treat Chapter 15 as the concretion step: take the Chapter 12 classifiers, the Chapter 13 signals, and the Chapter 11 layering principle and ship them as running, tested detectors. Each detector maps to a named concept from the detection chapters, and that mapping is the design.

Compact
